The Library Clock Cards

Arda liked preparing a room almost as much as solving a puzzle. Before visitors arrived at Hill Library, he moved chairs into pairs, lined up pencils, and checked that each table had enough blank paper for the workshop. The workshop room was public, bright, and still quiet except for the scrape of chair legs and the soft hum of the heating system.

On the nearest table, three printed timing cards lay in a neat row. Arda picked them up. The first said 14:00. The second said 14:20. The third said 14:40.

He carried them to the public workshop sheet taped beside the supply box. That sheet listed 14:00, 14:30, and 15:00.

Arda looked from one list to the other. At first, he wondered whether the cards were simply a different way of displaying the same schedule. But the starting times alone did not settle anything. After 14:00, one list advanced by twenty minutes each time, while the other advanced by thirty. The disagreement was about the schedule itself, not merely about how it had been printed.

He took out his small notebook. At the top of a clean page, he wrote: “Goal: resolve timing conflict before visitors arrive.” Under that, he copied all six times exactly.

He also wrote what he knew about Selin’s information. Selin had told him only two things: Room B changed use last week, and the public clock was checked this morning. That was all. She had not told him who made the cards, why the times differed, or what the room change had done to the workshop schedule.

Arda paused with his pencil above the page. A convenient answer was already tempting: perhaps someone had made a mistake, or perhaps the cards belonged to an earlier plan. But neither possibility was evidence yet. He enjoyed logic puzzles because they punished answers that arrived too quickly.

He would compare sources instead of guessing. He could check the public desk and, while supervised, read the staff board. He would not search restricted places or try to discover private information. The rumored ARCHIVE_STAIR was not part of his access, and he had no reason to investigate it. Whatever was beyond it could not safely be used in his explanation.

Arda placed the cards between the pages of his notebook so they would not scatter. Then he carried his written comparison toward the public desk, ready to test the first explanation: that the clock itself might be wrong.

The public desk stood just outside the workshop room. Behind it, the public room clock showed the current time in clear black numbers. Arda checked it against the time reference he was using, then checked again after making sure he had not misread either display. The clock agreed.

That did not prove every schedule in the building was correct, but it was useful evidence. So was Selin’s exact statement that the public clock had been checked this morning. Arda wrote both points in his notebook without adding anything to them.

An adult supervising the workshop noticed his notes and allowed him to read the staff board nearby. Arda stayed where he was directed. The board contained a current note saying: “Room B changed use last week.” He copied the sentence precisely, including the word “changed.” He did not rewrite it as “the workshop moved,” or “the schedule was changed,” because the note did not say either of those things.

Back at the public desk, he drew a table.

Observed source | What it shows

Timing cards | 14:00, 14:20, 14:40
Public workshop sheet | 14:00, 14:30, 15:00
Public room clock | Agrees with the current time reference
Selin’s communicated facts | The clock was checked this morning; Room B changed use last week
Staff-board note | Room B changed use last week

Arda added a second section beneath it.

Possible explanations:

Clock error: possible to test.
Cards are outdated: possible, but not established.
Room B change caused the difference: possible connection, but not stated.
Someone deliberately misled visitors: no evidence.

He tapped the table with his pencil. The evidence did not all have the same strength. The times on the cards were directly visible. The times on the public sheet were directly visible. The clock could be compared with the current reference. The note about Room B was also visible, but it was narrower than a full explanation.

Arda remembered correcting a classroom timetable once. He had checked both the printed sheet and the wall clock before changing anything. That memory did not tell him the answer now, but it reminded him that a printed disagreement did not automatically make the clock guilty—or the paper guilty. Sources had to be compared.

He considered whether to ask for more information. Then he stopped. Asking an adult to reveal a private staff discussion would not make the evidence safer. Neither would trying to learn who printed the cards. He did not know those things, and he could solve the practical problem without pretending that he did.

The clock and the morning check gave him two reasons to examine the clock-error explanation carefully. The two lists gave him a pattern to test: twenty-minute intervals on the cards and thirty-minute intervals on the public sheet. Arda copied that pattern in large writing, circled it, and carried the notebook back toward the workshop room.

Now he had enough evidence to test a competing explanation instead of merely collecting facts.

Arda began with the explanation that seemed easiest to blame: the public clock was wrong. He wrote it as a question rather than a conclusion.

If the clock were wrong, what should the evidence look like? Perhaps the current time reference and the public clock would disagree. Perhaps the workshop times would have been copied or adjusted to match a mistaken display. But the clock agreed with Arda’s reference, and Selin had explicitly said it was checked that morning.

There was another problem. Even if the clock had been wrong, that would not explain the shape of the two lists. The cards went from 14:00 to 14:20 to 14:40: twenty-minute intervals. The public sheet went from 14:00 to 14:30 to 15:00: thirty-minute intervals. A clock showing the wrong time might shift a reading, but it would not by itself turn a twenty-minute pattern into a thirty-minute pattern.

Arda drew a line through “clock error” in the possible-explanations section. Beside it he wrote: “Rejected by direct comparison, morning check, and interval mismatch.” He did not write “impossible.” Good reasoning, he knew, did not need stronger words than the evidence allowed. The explanation was not adequate for this conflict.

Next he examined the Room B note. Room B had changed use last week. Could that be connected to the timing cards? Yes, it could. If materials had been prepared before a change, the cards might be outdated. But the note did not say that a workshop had moved, that the intervals had changed, or that the cards belonged to an earlier plan. It only stated that the room had changed use.

Arda wrote: “Outdated cards: plausible hypothesis.” Then underneath: “Room B connection: possible, not confirmed.”

He was careful not to turn possibility into history. The timing cards might have been left from an earlier arrangement. They might have been made for some other purpose. Or there might be an explanation he had not considered. Since he did not know who printed them, he could not safely claim to know their intended schedule or their source.

Still, uncertainty about the past did not prevent a decision about the present. The public workshop sheet gave three clear times. The public clock agreed with the current reference and had been checked that morning. The Room B note showed that something about room use had changed recently, which made caution about older materials sensible, even though it did not prove a cause.

Arda looked again at the shared starting time, 14:00. That small agreement mattered. The disagreement began afterward, in the intervals. It made the public sheet no less clear and the cards no more reliable as a current visitor schedule.

He closed his notebook, leaving the crossed-out explanation and the unresolved possibilities visible. He could recommend a correction without solving the cards’ history. The safest answer would rest on what was current, checkable, and actually stated—not on a story about what someone might have meant.

The supervising adult joined Arda beside the workshop tables. Arda spread out the three timing cards, the public workshop sheet, and his notebook so that every comparison could be seen at once.

“I recommend replacing the cards, or clearly correcting them,” he said. “The visitor-facing times should be 14:00, 14:30, and 15:00, matching the public workshop sheet.”

The adult looked at the materials, then at Arda’s table. “Explain how you reached that recommendation.”

Arda pointed first to the public sheet. “This is the published workshop schedule available to visitors. It lists 14:00, 14:30, and 15:00. The cards instead list 14:00, 14:20, and 14:40.”

He placed his finger beneath the two sequences. “The cards use twenty-minute intervals. The sheet uses thirty-minute intervals. They share the first time, but they do not show the same later schedule.”

Then he pointed toward the public desk. “I compared the public room clock with the current time reference, and they agreed. Selin also said the public clock was checked this morning. So the clock-error explanation does not fit the evidence. It would not explain the different intervals anyway.”

Finally, he indicated the note he had copied from the supervised staff board. “It says Room B changed use last week. That makes it possible that some material is outdated, but the note does not say the workshop schedule changed, and it does not prove that the room change caused these cards. I’m using it as a reason to be cautious, not as a confirmed explanation.”

The adult nodded but did not reach for the cards. “What do you know about who made them?”

“Nothing certain,” Arda answered. “I don’t know who printed them or exactly why their times differ. I don’t need to know that to choose the safest current correction.”

The adult studied his notebook. “Verify the written times one more time.”

Arda checked the sheet slowly. “14:00, 14:30, 15:00.” He checked again against the numbers he had written. The same. He also confirmed that the old cards said 14:00, 14:20, and 14:40, so no number had been shifted while he was comparing them.

“Then correct the visitor materials,” the adult said. “Your recommendation is approved.”

The approval felt different from being handed an answer. The adult had asked him to show the evidence and verify the transcription; Arda had supplied the reasoning himself. He took a fresh set of cards from the workshop supplies and wrote the three public-sheet times in large, clear numerals. Beside the older cards, he placed a note reading: “Do not use—times conflict with the current public workshop sheet.” He did not label them false, dishonest, or deliberately misleading. Their source and history remained unknown.

Before visitors arrived, he also prepared a concise record for the supervising adult: the correction was supported by the published schedule, the checked public clock, and the supervised note that Room B had changed use last week. The note would state clearly that the Room B connection was only a possibility, not a confirmed cause. Arda underlined the final sentence: “Source and exact history of the older cards remain unknown.”

The table now offered one schedule to visitors instead of two competing ones.

As the first visitors entered the workshop room, they could clearly see 14:00, 14:30, and 15:00 on the corrected cards, matching the public workshop sheet. The older cards were set aside with Arda’s note, not a guess about their history.

His record preserved the important uncertainties: the cards’ source was unknown, and the Room B change might be connected but had not been shown to cause the discrepancy. What was known was enough to act safely.

Arda watched visitors gather around the tables. He had not filled every gap with a story. He had used the exact statements people had made and the evidence he could actually check. Sometimes sound reasoning did not uncover a hidden answer. Sometimes it simply chose the strongest safe action—and marked the remaining questions honestly.
