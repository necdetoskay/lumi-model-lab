# The Route That Might Need Closing

The certification reviewer was already writing when Deniz reached the desk, and did not look up.

"You're early," the reviewer said. "That's new."

"The tide table gets a vote." Deniz laid the chart of the inner strait across the pending paperwork and turned it right-side up. "Beacon Ridge. Falling window. Ninety minutes of usable light on the stone, and the evening freight clears the harbor two hours after that."

"Then talk while you pack." The reviewer finished the line, capped the pen, and looked up—grey eyes, unhurried, the kind that had read a thousand junior reports and believed perhaps forty of them. "Certification-grade field check. One site, one window, one report. You know the procedure."

Deniz did. That was the problem—Deniz knew it too well to pretend it was routine. This was the check that would decide whether the board granted independent certification authority beyond the home strait, or whether an eighteen-year-old with a junior license spent another season carrying someone else's instrument case.

"Route guidance is what I care about," the reviewer said. "You file a provisional anomaly, nothing happens tonight except paper. You recommend a route advisory, the pilots hold the evening freight, the cargo agents start calculating losses by the hour, and the harbor master wants to know why a junior certifier closed a clean route." The harbor logs slid across the desk. "An advisory requires independent, repeatable support. Not a hunch. Not one bad sight. Tell me the difference."

"A provisional record says something was observed, bounded, and documented," Deniz said. "An advisory says the route is unsafe until further notice."

"And the second one costs money every hour it stands. Last month's logs. Read them on the way to the pier. There is no accepted incident on that route—no grounding, no pilot report, no near-miss. Whatever you find out there, you're starting from a clean record and a schedule." The reviewer stood. "File your report by evening or the check doesn't count."

Deniz read the logs standing on the pier while the pilot boat's crew finished loading. Clean. Every page. No accepted incident, and nothing to explain the rumor she'd heard at the chart table last week that Beacon Ridge had been "acting strange." Rumors weren't evidence. She got on the boat.

---

Beacon Ridge was a spine of dark rock half a cable long, and the beacon stood at its outer tip—a column of fitted magnetic stone, banded grey and black, its charted bearing recorded in the harbor master's master copy. Deniz had it memorized anyway: 118 degrees 24 minutes, tolerance five minutes of arc.

She set the theodolite over the survey pin on the ridge's crown, leveled it, collimated it, and took the first sight with the evening freight's first lanterns already coming up the strait behind her.

The number was wrong.

Not wrong the way a tired eye is wrong, or a gust is wrong. Wrong by seventeen minutes of arc, on a tolerance of five. More than three times the allowed error, in a direction no wind could account for.

Her first thought, the one she had to consciously put down, was *the beacon has drifted.* Her second thought, the professional one, was *you have one reading and a reputation for getting excited.* She checked the card. Checked the bearing. Checked the sun angle against the chronometer. Then she read it again.

Seventeen minutes.

She re-leveled the instrument. Checked the index error against the calibration card. Checked the clock against the harbor time she'd copied that morning. The wind gusted from the northwest, and the air had begun to cool with the sun going down—she logged both, because a certifier who didn't log the weather had no business holding a license.

Second sight, same setup.

Fifteen minutes.

Still beyond tolerance. Still wrong. But not the same wrong.

That was when the routine check stopped being routine. A fixed error—a slipped index, a bumped tripod—gave you the same wrong number twice. This gave her two different wrong numbers, both outside tolerance, and no way yet to say which of several causes was making them.

She committed to the series before she could talk herself out of it.

Five readings. Setup untouched. Each one logged with clock time, tide height, and air temperature, in the columns the form gave her, in her own handwriting, because a series she couldn't audit was worse than no series at all.

Third reading: thirteen minutes. Tide down another hand's width.

Fourth: eleven minutes. Air two degrees cooler by the small thermometer clipped to her board.

Fifth: nine minutes.

She sat back on her heels and looked at the column. Descending. Not in noise, not in scatter—step by step, in order, as the tide fell and the evening cooled. The first reading and the fifth were as different as a bad sight and a marginal one, and every step between them was accounted for in her own log.

The offset hadn't gone away. Even the smallest reading was still nearly double the tolerance. But it was moving, and movement meant cause, and cause meant she might be looking at something real.

The signal flags went up on the pilot boat at the ridge's leeward anchorage before she could decide what the movement meant.

QUERY. ROUTE STATUS. TONIGHT'S FREIGHT.

Deniz signaled back: HOLD. IN PROGRESS.

The reply came fast, and it wasn't the pilot's flag. The cargo agent's pennant followed: DELAY COSTS ACCRUING. CONFIRM SAFE PASSAGE.

She looked at her logbook. Five readings, one setup, one tide window, no independent repeat, no second instrument. And two people on the water waiting for her to tell them what was true, in language that would decide whether a ship moved tonight.

There was an older reference on the ridge, and she had walked past it twice already trying not to look at it. A historical alignment mark—a groove cut into the bedrock—set at a bearing that did not match the current charted bearing at all.

She measured it anyway. The mark sat several degrees off from the chart.

Her first thought was that it proved everything. Her second, slower thought was that it proved nothing she could use. A static mark showed where the alignment had been once, at some moment she couldn't know, under some tide state she couldn't reconstruct. It could not tell her when it had been cut, or whether the difference belonged to the beacon, the mark, or the people who had set it. It was context, not evidence. She wrote that down, exactly that way, so she wouldn't be tempted to pretend otherwise later.

Back at the theodolite, she plotted the five readings against time and tide on the back of the field sheet.

The line was almost clean. Offset falling, tide falling, temperature falling. Three variables changing together, and no way in one window to say which one was driving.

But the shape itself was the finding. A fixed error did not decline in neat steps across ninety minutes. A fixed error stayed fixed. This was moving. Whatever was happening at Beacon Ridge, it did not behave like a fixed error. It was a systematic variation inside the session, and she could bound it, and she could not explain it.

Tide. Time. Temperature. Her own instrument. Four candidate causes. Zero confirmed.

And if the offset was real, then every pilot who trusted that bearing tonight was steering by a reference that might be lying to them.

The tide had maybe twenty minutes left in the window. Enough for one thing, and only one.

She could break down the theodolite, carry it twenty paces to the secondary pin, re-site it, re-level it, re-zero it, and take a fresh pair of readings. An independent setup. If the offset reproduced, she would walk back with evidence no reviewer could wave away as a settling tripod. If it didn't reproduce, she would have saved the harbor from a false alarm and probably saved her own career from a very short one.

Or she could stop now, while the light held, and write the record.

Her hands were already loosening the tripod's clamps before she made herself stop.

Because a fresh setup was evidence. It was not a record. If she spent the window on the repeat, she would have a stronger anomaly and nothing formally filed—no accepted entry and no reviewed log, though the five-reading series would remain in her field notes for the next certifier to inspect. The pilot would still be waiting. The freight would still be waiting. And the next person sent to Beacon Ridge would have no reviewed formal entry to build on, because she had chosen a better data point over a durable one.

She thought about the reviewer's voice. *An advisory requires independent, repeatable support.* She didn't have that. She might never get it in one window. But a provisional record didn't claim the route was safe or unsafe. It claimed only that something had been observed, bounded, and written down—and that was a thing the institution could actually use.

She released the clamps she'd tightened and took out the formal log forms instead.

She worked fast and wrote slower. Five readings, clock times, tide heights, temperatures. The historical mark, logged as context only. Then the confounds, listed one by one in the space the form gave her and spilling into the margin: *setup not independently reproduced; tide, elapsed time, and air temperature varied together across series; instrument drift cannot be excluded; cause unknown; recurrence unknown; no accepted incident in harbor logs.*

The recommendation line was the one that mattered, and she made herself write it the way she would want to read it in somebody else's report.

*Provisional anomaly record. Out-of-tolerance bearing offset observed, varying systematically within a single tide window. Data insufficient to establish cause, recurrence, or risk to navigation. Route advisory NOT recommended on present evidence. Recommend monitored re-observation at next certification window.*

Underneath, the working hypothesis, because the reviewer would ask for it: *The ordered decline is less consistent with a single fixed offset, but setup drift and instrument error remain possible.*

She signaled the pilot boat while the ink was still wet.

ROUTE OPEN TONIGHT. NO ADVISORY RECOMMENDED. PROVISIONAL RECORD TO BE SUBMITTED FOR REVIEW.

The reply took a long minute. Not the cargo agent this time. The pilot's own flag: UNDERSTOOD. PROCEEDING.

The tide turned. The window closed.

---

The reviewer read the entry twice, once fast and once slowly, and Deniz stood at the desk and waited to be dismantled.

"You didn't take a repeat," the reviewer said.

"No time for both."

"So your series could be a settling tripod."

"It could." Deniz kept her voice level. "That's why it's in the confounds, in the entry, in my own words. I didn't write around it. I wrote it into the record."

"You declined the advisory."

"The evidence doesn't support one. Five readings from one setup can't carry a route closure, and I'm not going to pretend they can. The pilot needed an answer tonight, so I gave him the one the data earned: route open, anomaly logged, re-observation recommended."

The reviewer looked at the margin full of confounds for a long moment. Out the window behind the desk, the evening freight's lights were already moving up the strait, unheld.

"You know a board with a taste for tidy reports would call this incomplete."

"I know."

"And you filed it anyway, with the holes showing."

"Because the holes are the honest part," Deniz said. "The alternative was a stronger number and no record, or a clean record and no number. I chose the record."

The reviewer signed the entry, dated it, and filed it in the provisional drawer without a word about the freight.

"Next window," the reviewer said, "I want the repeat."

---

Deniz was restocking her satchel in the passage when the harbor clerk came through with the season's schedules.

"Beacon Ridge again?" the clerk asked, sorting papers without looking up.

"Probably. Why?"

"Because you're not the only one. Half the inner marks are being re-sighted this season. Whole rotation." The clerk tucked the folder under one arm and was gone before Deniz could ask which beacons, or why now, or who had ordered it.

She stood there a moment, the provisional drawer's key still in the lock behind her, and thought about the number that had fallen in neat steps as the tide went out, and about the historical mark that pointed somewhere else entirely.

One ridge was an anomaly. One ridge with a tide-linked pattern was a question.

But if the rotation covered half the inner marks, then sooner or later someone was going to find out whether Beacon Ridge was alone.

She wrote the question at the top of her working file, above the empty space where the repeat was supposed to go: *Is this an isolated fault, a local drift, or the edge of something wider?*

Then she picked up her satchel and went to wait for the next window.
