# The Angle That Wouldn't Match

The needle was wrong.

Deniz crouched at the base of the ridge beacon, eye level with the brass sighting compass, and read the angle a third time. Fourteen degrees off true. The tide tables—checked twice that morning, checked again now—allowed four at most at this stage of the ebb. Four degrees was the seasonal margin, the tolerance every surveyor memorized before being trusted with an instrument alone.

Fourteen was not four. Fourteen was not close.

Beacon Ridge was a spine of dark rock that surfaced only at extreme low tides, running half a cable's length out into the channel. At its end stood the beacon itself—darkened by years of salt and spray. A squat pillar of dense stone with a lodestone core, its face carved with sighting marks that had been calibrated decades before Deniz was born. Ships used its magnetic influence to confirm their bearings through the strait. The official charts recorded exactly how much it should deflect a compass at any given tide and season. The charts were never wrong—or so the harbor claimed, in the same tone it used for everything it had decided was settled.

The exposed rock smelled of wet mineral and kelp, and the sound of the place was strange: water pulled thin on both sides, dripping from the ridge's edges, the channel too shallow for waves. Just dripping, and the tick of Deniz's watch, and the small sound of the needle settling after each reading.

Deniz sat back and pulled the reference satchel closer. The tide tables were dog-eared, the margins dense with Kural's handwriting—corrections, seasonal adjustments, decade-old notes in faded ink. Deniz found today's date, found the tide stage, worked through the correction column step by step, the way the manual insisted.

The number held. Four degrees, maximum expected deviation at this tide state.

Then the beacon manual, flipped to the section on lodestone pillars. Known causes of angular drift: stone degradation over decades (slow, uniform, not sudden). Mounting shift (constant offset, not varying with conditions). Instrument wear (present across all readings, not one). Nothing explained a deviation this large appearing at a specific tide stage and nowhere else in the record.

This wasn't a transcription error. The numbers were right. The numbers didn't match the world.

The easy thing—the thing Deniz wanted to do—was to log the reading as an anomaly, note it as *possible instrument error*, and move on. That was what the form had a checkbox for. Nobody would question it. Single odd readings got dismissed all the time; that was what tolerance margins were for.

But if it wasn't an error—if the beacon's magnetic influence was actually shifting with the tide—then dismissing it meant it went unrecorded until next season's calibration, if anyone thought to check again. And by then the trail would be cold.

Deniz thought about the shipping summaries. A runner had brought them to the survey boat before dawn—Kural's routine on every calibration run, the harbor's way of saying *here is what ships experienced this week; see if your instruments agree.* Deniz had read them during the row out. Nothing from the ridge. No delayed passages, no complaints about bearing errors, no captain's note mentioning anything unusual near the beacon.

If a real drift were affecting navigation, someone would have logged it. Nobody had.

Which meant either it was too new to have shown up in a ship's experience, or it wasn't big enough to matter yet, or it wasn't real. Deniz couldn't tell which from a summary page. The only evidence that could settle it was here, at this tide, before the water erased the conditions that made it measurable.

Deniz made a decision: stop taking single readings. Start taking a series.

The field log came out. The watch from the instrument case was wound and checked against the sun. From this point, every reading would be timestamped, and nothing would be adjusted between measurements—not the tripod legs, not the sighting arm, not the compass housing. If the drift was moving, the movement itself would be the evidence. One suspicious reading was an anecdote. A series was a pattern, or the absence of one.

*Tide: falling, near-low. Drift: 14.1°.*

Ten minutes. The water had dropped another handspan, pulling back from rocks it hadn't shown an hour ago, exposing a band of dark, barnacled stone that would be underwater again within the hour. Deniz sighted, read, recorded. The needle had moved—not much, but enough.

*Drift: 14.2°.*

Another ten minutes. The ridge was at its lowest safe exposure now, the channel on either side drawn back to thin ribbons of brown water. A gull landed on the beacon's cap, looked at Deniz, and left.

*Drift: 14.3°.*

Deniz's pulse kicked. This wasn't scatter. The drift was growing with the falling tide, smoothly, reading after reading, like something responding to the water's state.

The tide reached its floor. Held. The thin water stopped moving. Deniz waited, watching the surface go still, and took a reading at the slack.

*Drift: 14.3°.*

Nearly unchanged. Holding with the tide.

Then the first ripple crept back over the lowest rocks. Coming in. Deniz waited for the water to climb a handspan, then sighted again.

*Drift: 13.6°.*

Smaller.

Deniz stared at the log. Five readings, roughly ten minutes apart, tracking the tide's fall and turn the way a shadow follows a body. Not random. Not noise. A pattern—within this session, visible only because the tide was extreme enough to expose whatever was causing it.

Deniz checked the tide clock and the sky together, the way Kural had taught. Light: good for perhaps another hour. Tide: turned, rising. The window that had allowed a full series was now allowing fewer readings by the minute. The barnacled band was already slick again.

One more thing before anything else. Deniz needed to know if the instrument itself was lying.

Without touching the tripod, without adjusting anything, Deniz re-sighted the same reference mark five times in quick succession. The needle barely trembled. Each reading came back within a tenth of a degree of the last. The instrument was stable in its current mount. It was not drifting on its own. Not now.

That didn't clear the original reading. Something could have shifted the tripod during the first observation—a gust, a misstep, a sleeve brushing the housing. But if the instrument was steady enough to track the tide's turn smoothly across five consecutive readings, a one-time glitch became harder to argue. Not impossible. Harder. And harder was worth something, if you were honest about what it was worth.

Footsteps on the ridge. Deniz turned.

Kural was crossing the exposed rocks, survey bag over one shoulder, moving faster than usual. Not running—Kural didn't run—but covering ground with the efficiency of someone who had calculated exactly how much time remained and intended to use every minute of it. Kural's eyes went to the instrument first, then to Deniz, then to the open field log.

"You're still here." Flat. Not surprised. "Site gets secured once the tide's fully turned. Half an hour. Maybe less."

"I know." Deniz stood, log in hand. "I have something."

Kural stopped. Waited. The particular silence that meant *go on, and make it worth my time.*

"Show me."

Deniz had a moment to think, and used it.

What was actually known: the drift had grown as the tide fell, held at slack, and begun shrinking as the tide rose. That was a within-session pattern—observed, timestamped, consistent across five readings.

What was probably true: the instrument hadn't simply broken and corrected itself. The control check showed stability at rest. The smooth tracking made random error unlikely.

What wasn't known: whether this happened on every extreme tide, or only this one. Whether other beacons in the chain showed the same behavior. What could physically make a lodestone's influence shift with water depth. Whether any of it mattered for real navigation yet, or would next season, or ever.

The honest claim was narrow. Deniz could defend it now, or could try to strengthen it first. Not both.

There was one other option. Dismantle the instrument entirely. Rebuild it from scratch at a new position on the ridge. Take one more reading—a fresh-setup reading that would test whether the drift persisted after a completely new mounting. That would address setup-dependent error directly, and it would make the evidence harder to dismiss. If the drift showed up again from an entirely new setup, nobody could say it was a quirk of one particular mounting.

But it would eat nearly all the remaining time. And if the fresh reading showed nothing—because the tide had already turned too far past the extreme, because the conditions that produced the pattern were gone—then the session's whole observation would look like noise. And there'd be almost no time left to write up what had already been seen, which meant Kural would have nothing to evaluate. The whole morning would go unrecorded.

Kural was waiting. The water was climbing. The site had to be secured.

More data that couldn't be recorded was worth nothing. A bounded, honest report of what had already been observed was worth something.

"I'm reporting what I have," Deniz said. "Now, while I can write it properly."

Kural said nothing. Waited.

Deniz sat on the highest rock still dry, opened the field log to a fresh page, and wrote. Each observation labeled. Each limit stated. The working hypothesis: that the drift was tied to tide extremity, not instrument error—provisional, within-session only, unconfirmed for other tides or other beacons, cause unknown. The control check noted, with its own limitation written plainly: performed after the drift peaked, not before. Deniz wrote the sentence *I cannot yet distinguish between a real tide-linked effect and an unidentified instrument fault that mimics one* and left it there, because it was true.

When it was done, Deniz handed the log across without ceremony.

Kural read. The silence stretched long enough that Deniz could hear the water moving over the lower rocks, filling the channel back in.

"You're telling me your instrument drifted fourteen degrees off chart and then drifted back."

"I'm telling you what I measured. I can't tell you why."

"Your control check only shows it was stable afterward. It doesn't clear the original reading."

"Yes. I couldn't run it before without breaking the series. That's a limitation. I wrote it down."

Kural looked up. "You wrote down your own limitations."

"The manual says to. 'Report what bounds your certainty.'"

"Most apprentices write what they want found." Kural looked at the log again. Then at the beacon, water already climbing its base. "You wrote what you actually know."

"I wrote what I could defend."

Kural looked at Deniz—longer this time, with something Deniz couldn't quite name. Not warmth. Not approval, exactly. Something closer to recalibration.

"This is bounded," Kural said finally. "This is honest. This is—" A pause. Choosing the word with the same care Deniz had used choosing the claims. "—adequate methodology."

From Kural, that was close to praise.

"I'm adding a provisional note." Kural took out a pencil and wrote in the margin of the log, in the same precise hand that filled the tide tables' margins. *Provisional anomaly: within-session tide correlation observed; recurrence and cause unconfirmed.*

Kural handed the log back. "What happens next depends on what the next observations show," Kural said.

The tide was climbing steadily now, water swirling around the lower markers. Kural shouldered the bag. "Secure the site. We're leaving."

Deniz packed the instrument and coiled the tripod straps. As water covered the lower markers, Deniz and Kural retreated along the ridge and climbed into the survey boat. Behind them, the workable rock disappeared back into the channel until there was nothing left to show that anything unusual had happened there at all—except the log, the note in its margin, and whatever the next extreme tide decided to reveal.

From the boat, Deniz watched the water rise around the beacon and wondered what the next cycle would show.
