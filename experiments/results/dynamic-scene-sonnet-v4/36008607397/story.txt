The Missing Pattern

On Saturday morning, Deniz pushed open the glass doors of the Learning Center with one hand and held the volunteer sign-in sheet with the other. The room smelled faintly of paper, dust, and the lemon cleaner used on the tables.

“Good morning, Deniz,” called Ms. Aylin, the center coordinator. She was standing beside the Pattern Wall, which usually filled one long section of the room with bright tiles.

Deniz hurried over. “What happened?”

One row near the middle of the wall had an empty space. The other tiles sat neatly in place, but a square opening showed the pale backing behind them.

“A tile went missing during the renovation,” Ms. Aylin said. “The unveiling is this afternoon. Families, teachers, and visitors will be here at three o’clock, and the wall needs to be complete before then.”

Deniz looked at the empty spot. “Do you know which tile belongs there?”

“If I did, I wouldn’t need to ask you.” Ms. Aylin smiled, though she glanced at the clock. “Can you help me find it?”

Deniz nodded. A normal volunteer shift had suddenly become a puzzle. “I’ll try.”

Ms. Aylin pointed around the room. “You can use anything that might help. The building map is on the information table. The storage labels are posted on the supply shelves, and the schedule board has notes about the wall’s construction.”

Deniz studied each resource. The building map showed rooms and narrow connecting halls. The storage labels were arranged in tidy rows: paper, paint, tools, display parts. On the schedule board, colored pins marked classes, repairs, and events. Several older notices were tucked beneath the current week’s schedule.

Deniz returned to the Pattern Wall. The tiles had different colors and shapes, but from this distance they seemed to make one careful design. The empty row looked like a missing sentence in a book.

Three o’clock was only a few hours away. Deniz took a pencil from the sign-in table and opened a clean page in the volunteer notebook.

“First,” Deniz said, writing the time at the top, “I need to find out what belongs in the empty space.”

Then Deniz began looking closely, without moving any tiles.

Deniz crouched so the empty row was at eye level. The tiles on both sides of the gap were blue triangles. The next two tiles farther along were blue, too.

“Blue triangle,” Deniz said. “That seems pretty clear.”

In the notebook, Deniz wrote: **First guess: blue triangle, because the nearby tiles match.** Then Deniz looked again. The guess felt sensible, but sensible was not the same as certain.

The schedule board stood only a few steps away. Its colored pins and notices covered most of the cork. Deniz remembered Ms. Aylin saying that notes about the wall’s construction were kept there. Deniz lifted the corner of a current event poster and found an old photograph pinned underneath.

The photograph showed the Pattern Wall before the renovation. It was a little faded, but the rows and shapes were sharp enough to see. A handwritten label below it read: **Pattern Wall, before repair — May.**

Deniz carried the notebook closer and compared the photograph with the wall.

“There’s the row,” Deniz murmured.

In the photograph, the same gap was not empty. A yellow square sat in that spot. The blue triangles still appeared on both sides, but the tile in the middle was definitely yellow and square. Its corners lined up with the edges of the row.

Deniz glanced from the photograph to the real wall, then back again. The blue-triangle guess had been based only on the tiles nearby. The old photograph showed something different.

Deniz drew a line through **blue triangle**.

“That guess doesn’t work,” Deniz said. “If the wall used the same arrangement before the repair, the missing tile wasn’t blue at all.”

For a moment, the answer seemed easier. The photograph appeared to say yellow square. Deniz almost wrote that in large letters. But then Deniz stopped. The photograph showed what had been in the space, not why it belonged there. Maybe the wall had been rearranged during the renovation. Maybe the colors made a pattern that was harder to see from one row.

Deniz wrote a second note: **Photo proves the color guess is wrong. Find the rule before choosing.**

The old photograph was real evidence, but it was only one piece. Deniz carefully unpinned it from the schedule board and held it beside the notebook. If the answer was not decided by the nearby colors, another clue had to explain the arrangement.

Deniz looked across the room at the building map. The storage shelves—and all those labels—might tell a different part of the story.

Deniz folded the photograph carefully and tucked it beside the notebook. Then Deniz crossed the room to the building map, which hung near the front desk.

The map showed the Learning Center in small colored sections. Main Room was marked in green. A narrow hallway led to rooms with labels such as ART SUPPLIES, BOOK REPAIR, and TILE STORAGE.

“Tile Storage,” Deniz said, tracing the route with one finger. “That’s where I need to go.”

The hallway smelled faintly of paper and dust. At the end, Deniz found a door with a white label: TILE STORAGE—PATTERN WALL SUPPLIES. A key hung on a hook beside it, labeled VOLUNTEERS. Deniz took the key, unlocked the door, and switched on the light.

Shelves filled the small room from floor to ceiling. Boxes held painted shapes: circles, squares, and triangles. Some boxes had color names on them, but other labels had numbers written in thick black marker.

Deniz remembered the photograph. The yellow square in the empty spot had been real evidence, but it had not explained the whole arrangement. Maybe the numbers would.

On a low desk lay a thick book with a dusty blue cover. Its label read: TILE INVENTORY LOG—DELIVERIES AND PLACEMENT.

Deniz opened it. Each page had columns for a tile number, its shape, its color, and the date it arrived. The tiles were not listed in color groups. A yellow square might appear between a blue triangle and a red circle because of its delivery number.

Deniz ran a pencil down one page.

**21 — triangle — blue**
**22 — circle — red**
**24 — square — yellow**
**25 — triangle — blue**
**27 — circle — red**
**28 — square — yellow**

Deniz stopped. The numbers did not run 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28. The same kind of gap appeared again: after two numbers, one number was missing.

“Twenty-three is missing, then twenty-six,” Deniz murmured. “That looks like a pattern.”

Deniz checked the next line, but it belonged to a different delivery batch. The page did not say yet whether the missing numbers were important to the wall or merely skipped numbers in the warehouse records. The shapes and colors seemed to repeat, too, but the list alone could not explain why.

Deniz wrote in the notebook: **Inventory uses numbers, not just colors. Possible repeating skips: 21, 22, 24, 25, 27, 28. Check against the wall.**

The logbook was useful, but it was not enough to choose a tile. Deniz carried it back toward the main room, ready to compare the numbers with the schedule board and the labels there.

Deniz reached the main room with the logbook tucked under one arm. The schedule board hung beside the Pattern Wall, covered with notices, class times, and small cards about the wall’s construction.

Deniz opened the logbook to the delivery page and studied the dates beside the numbers.

21 — triangle — blue — May 6
22 — circle — red — May 7
24 — square — yellow — May 9
25 — triangle — blue — May 10
27 — circle — red — May 12
28 — square — yellow — May 13

Deniz copied the dates into the notebook, then drew lines between the logbook entries and the placement cards on the schedule board. The cards showed the same order. Tile 21 had been placed first, tile 22 second, tile 24 third, and so on. The missing numbers, 23 and 26, were not mistakes. The rule was exact: two numbers were listed, then the next number was skipped. After that, two more numbers were listed, then the next number was skipped again. So the sequence went 21, 22, skip 23, then 24, 25, skip 26.

“Two numbers, then a gap,” Deniz said. “Two numbers, then another gap.”

But what did the gaps mean? Deniz walked along the shelves beneath the schedule board. Each shelf had a label with a number range and a color code. One label read TRIANGLES: BLUE CODE. Another read CIRCLES: RED CODE. A third read SQUARES: YELLOW CODE.

Deniz checked the logbook again. The shapes and colors matched the shelf codes exactly. Blue went with the triangle numbers, red with the circle numbers, and yellow with the square numbers. The colors were not chosen because neighboring tiles looked nice together. They belonged to a repeating three-part order: blue triangle, red circle, yellow square. The numbered entries followed the same cycle, while the number count followed a separate clear rule: list two numbers, skip one number, and repeat.

Deniz placed the old photograph beside the notebook. In the photograph, a yellow square sat in the blank position. That matched the third part of the shape-and-color cycle shown by the logbook. The dated cards also showed that this order had been used when the wall was built.

Deniz traced the sequence with a pencil. The first two entries were 21 and 22. The next listed entry was 24, because 23 was skipped. Then two more numbers, 25 and 27, appeared with 26 skipped. The numbers, shapes, colors, and placement dates all supported the same rule: after each pair of listed numbers, exactly one number was left out.

The photograph, logbook, dates, and shelf labels pointed to one tested pattern. Together, they showed why the old blue-triangle guess was wrong and explained the yellow square in the photograph. Deniz still needed to use the confirmed cycle and the storage information to identify the exact numbered tile. No single clue had solved the puzzle; each source had checked the others.

Deniz closed the notebook. The rule was now confirmed, and the exact tile could be located by following its number range on the map and shelves. The unveiling was still ahead. Deniz hurried toward the storage instructions, ready to retrieve the tile before three o’clock.

Deniz looked from the notebook to the empty place on the Pattern Wall. It was the third place in the row—the place that came after the entries for 21 and 22.

“Two numbers, then a gap,” Deniz murmured. “So the third listed tile is 24.”

Deniz checked the logbook. Beside 24 were the words square and yellow. Then Deniz looked at the old photograph. The same position held a yellow square before the wall had been repaired. Finally, Deniz checked the shelf labels again. Yellow matched squares.

“The logbook gives the number,” Deniz said, tapping the page. “The photograph checks the position, and the shelf label checks the shape and color. All three agree: tile 24, a yellow square.”

This time, Deniz did not make a quick guess. The blue triangle had seemed sensible because blue triangles stood nearby, but the evidence had pointed somewhere else. Number, position, shape, and color all pointed to one tile.

Deniz grabbed the building map and spread it on the table. A finger followed the thick hallway from the main room to the labeled Tile Storage—Pattern Wall Supplies. Deniz had walked there before, so the route was clear: past the reading tables, turn at the art room, then use the volunteer key at the storage door.

The clock above the schedule board showed twenty minutes before three.

Deniz tucked the notebook and logbook under one arm and hurried along the map’s route. In Tile Storage, boxes filled the shelves. Deniz went straight to the shelf marked with the number range that included 24, then checked the labels instead of grabbing the first yellow object.

“Twenty-four,” Deniz whispered.

A shallow box held several square tiles. Deniz read each small number until finding 24. The tile was yellow, and its four equal sides matched the square code on the shelf label. Deniz lifted it carefully and hurried back to the main room.

At the Pattern Wall, Deniz paused once more. The row showed 21, 22, then an empty place. The next entries continued with 25 and 27. Tile 24 belonged in the empty spot because the wall’s order listed two numbers, skipped one, and repeated the shape-and-color cycle: blue triangle, red circle, yellow square.

Deniz slid the yellow square into its space. It clicked gently against the backing board.

The whole row now made sense. The numbers followed the tested sequence. The shapes and colors repeated in the right order. The completed wall also matched the yellow square in the old photograph.

Ms. Aylin appeared at the doorway just as Deniz stepped back.

“You found it!” she said.

“Tile 24,” Deniz replied. “I used the logbook, the photograph, and the shelf labels together. The map got me to the storage room quickly.”

Ms. Aylin checked the row, then glanced at the clock. There were eight minutes left before the unveiling. She smiled and began straightening the chairs while Deniz returned the map and carried the records to the table.

The Pattern Wall was complete, and every piece had been chosen by a rule that could be checked.

With eight minutes left, Deniz finished returning the map and records to the table. Ms. Aylin straightened the last chair just as the first visitors came through the Learning Center doors.

“Welcome,” she called. “The Pattern Wall is ready.”

Families gathered in front of the bright rows of tiles. The completed row drew several curious looks. Tile 24, a yellow square, sat neatly between the red circle before it and the blue triangle that followed. Nothing seemed out of place now.

Ms. Aylin lifted her hand. “Deniz solved the missing-tile puzzle. Would you like to tell everyone how?”

Deniz felt a small flutter in the stomach but stepped closer to the wall. “At first, I guessed blue triangle because the nearby tiles were blue triangles,” Deniz said. “But I found an old photograph on the schedule board. It showed a yellow square in this spot, so my first guess could not be right.”

A girl near the front leaned toward the wall. “Then how did you know which yellow square to use?”

Deniz pointed to the row. “The logbook showed the number order: 21, 22, then 24. The wall lists two numbers and then skips one. The schedule dates showed that this was the order the tiles had been placed, not just a random list.”

Deniz tapped the small label beside the display shelf. “The shelf labels told me that yellow goes with squares. The photograph checked the position, and the logbook gave the number. All the clues agreed on tile 24.”

“So you did not just choose the color that looked right?” Ms. Aylin asked.

“No,” Deniz said. “I tested the guess against the evidence. The pattern was stronger than what it looked like at first.”

Ms. Aylin smiled. “That is exactly what this wall is meant to teach.”

The visitors moved closer, following the repeating blue triangle, red circle, yellow square cycle with their fingers without touching the tiles. A few tried spotting the skipped numbers. One boy found the gap after 25 and announced it proudly. Deniz showed him how to check the next pair before deciding.

When the unveiling ended, the room filled with pleased chatter. Ms. Aylin handed Deniz the volunteer notebook.

“Next month,” she said, “we will put up a new wall with a different pattern. You may have another puzzle to solve.”

Deniz looked at the finished row. “I’ll wait until I have evidence.”

The new puzzle could wait. For today, the Pattern Wall was complete, the visitors had seen it, and the answer had been tested instead of guessed.
