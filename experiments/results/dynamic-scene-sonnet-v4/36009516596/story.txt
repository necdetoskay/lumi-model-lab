The Missing Pattern

On Saturday morning, Deniz pushed open the glass doors of the community learning center and breathed in the familiar smell of paper, dust, and lemon cleaner. Sunlight fell across the room’s maps and labeled shelves. Near the windows, a table waited for the Explorer Club’s treasure hunt.

Deniz had helped prepare it before. The large map of the town lay beneath a sheet of clear plastic. Four numbered pieces were supposed to fit into the empty spaces: 7, 12, 19, and 24. A box of pencils, four clipboards, and a stack of clue cards stood neatly beside it.

But when Deniz lifted the plastic, four spaces showed the pale tabletop underneath.

“That’s not right,” Deniz whispered.

Deniz checked the box, even though the pieces were too large to fit inside. Then Deniz looked under the table, behind the chair, and beneath the folded tablecloth. Nothing. The map still had its other pieces, but the missing spaces made the coastline stop suddenly and left a blank patch in the middle of town.

At the front desk, the supervisor was busy carrying a stack of returned books to a cart. Deniz did not want to interrupt. Instead, Deniz walked to the schedule board.

EXPLORER CLUB TREASURE HUNT — 11:00 A.M.

The clock above the desk said 9:02.

Deniz counted on two fingers. One hour until ten. One more hour until the children arrived. Two hours to find the pieces and make the map ready.

For a moment, Deniz imagined a crowd of disappointed club members staring at the unfinished map. Then Deniz took a blue pencil from the supply box and turned over a scrap of paper.

Guessing would not help. Deniz drew three lines:

1. Someone might have thrown the pieces away by accident.
2. Someone might have moved them while cleaning or arranging the room.
3. Someone might have borrowed them or put them somewhere else.

Deniz added a fourth line: “Check what the labels and records show.”

The list made the problem feel smaller. Each idea could be checked. If one did not match the evidence, Deniz could cross it out.

Deniz placed the scrap beside the empty map spaces, picked up the pencil, and headed toward the recycling area. There were two hours left, and a careful search could begin with the simplest guess.

The recycling area was beside the back door, where two blue bins stood beneath a sign that said PAPER ONLY. A cleaning cart waited nearby, and one of the cleaning staff members was sorting newspapers into a cardboard box.

“Could I check the paper bin?” Deniz asked. “Some map pieces are missing from the Explorer Club table.”

The staff member nodded. “Go ahead. I have not emptied it yet.”

Deniz lifted the lid and searched carefully. Flyers, old worksheets, and a torn magazine filled the bin. Deniz checked the corners and moved the papers with a ruler. No stiff, map-colored pieces appeared.

The first idea on the scrap of paper got a neat line through it. If the pieces had been thrown away, at least one should probably have been there. The empty bin was not proof of everything, but it was good evidence against accidental disposal.

Next, Deniz went to the front desk and asked to see the sign-in log for the learning room. The supervisor was still busy with the book cart, so Deniz explained, “I’m checking who used the map table before the club event.”

The log listed times and reasons for visits. At 8:15, someone had written “arranged activity tables.” At 8:32, a cousin’s name appeared beside “looked at town maps.” At 8:47, the entry read “substitute helper—shelving and tidying.”

Deniz copied the times onto the scrap. The cousin was nearby, reading at a round table. Deniz asked, “Did you take any of the four numbered pieces?”

The cousin shook their head. “No. I only looked at the maps. I put everything back on the table.”

Deniz checked the log again. The cousin had signed out at 8:38, nine minutes before the substitute helper arrived. The timing did not prove exactly what had happened, but it gave no support to the idea that the cousin had taken the pieces. Deniz crossed out “borrowed by a visitor.”

Two guesses had now failed to fit the evidence: the pieces were not in the recycling bin, and the log did not point to a visitor taking them. The substitute helper remained a possible source of a shelving mistake, not proof of one.

Then Deniz noticed a note in the log’s margin. Beside “arranged activity tables,” the supervisor had written, “Return numbered pieces to shelf sections.”

Deniz looked across the room. The shelves near the map table had large letter labels—A, B, C, D, and more. Smaller labels underneath showed numbers. A shelf held cards marked 1, 11, and 21. Another showed 2, 12, and 22. Farther along were 7, 17, and 27.

Deniz looked down at the missing numbers: 7, 12, 19, and 24. Their endings matched groups on the shelves, but that did not yet show which lettered section held each piece. The numbers might be a code rather than exact shelf destinations.

Deniz drew a question mark beside the idea. The shelf labels suggested a pattern, but the pattern still needed a real test. Deniz glanced at the clock. It was nearly 10:00, leaving about an hour before the 11:00 treasure hunt.

“First evidence, then a conclusion,” Deniz murmured, moving toward the labeled shelves. “I need to see whether the numbers actually predict a place.”

Deniz stood in front of the shelves and studied the labels instead of grabbing the first card that looked familiar.

The shelves were arranged in order. A was beside labels ending in 1, B beside labels ending in 2, and C beside labels ending in 3. Farther along, G stood above cards marked 7, 17, and 27. The pattern was clearer now.

“The last digit tells the shelf letter,” Deniz said softly. “One means A, two means B, and seven means G.”

It was a useful idea, but an idea was not proof. Deniz needed to test it.

Piece 7 should be in section G. Deniz walked to the G shelf and checked each pocket. At first there were only activity cards and folded labels. Then a corner of stiff, blue paper showed behind a card marked 17.

Deniz pulled it out carefully. A large black 7 was printed on the back.

“Found one!”

The piece had been pushed into the wrong pocket, behind 17 instead of with the other 7-ending labels. It had not been left in its proper numbered place. The substitute helper must have seen the numbers, guessed that any card with a seven belonged together, and tucked it wherever there was room. But the piece was still in section G, exactly where the last-digit rule predicted.

Deniz turned the piece over. Its picture showed the eastern footbridge, and its edges matched one of the blank spaces on the town map. Deniz carried it back to the table, fitted it into place, and pressed the clear cover flat.

The blue piece clicked into the outline. The bridge appeared again, with a tiny compass rose beside it.

One test could have been luck, so Deniz checked the evidence once more. The shelf labels did not merely look similar to the missing numbers. The order showed a repeat: 1, 11, and 21 belonged with A; 2, 12, and 22 with B; 7, 17, and 27 with G. The last digit stayed the same while the tens changed. That was why piece 7 belonged in G, even though it had been shoved into the wrong pocket there.

Deniz wrote the rule on the scrap of paper in large letters:

LAST DIGIT → SHELF LETTER

Then Deniz listed the pieces still missing: 12, 19, and 24. Their destinations should be B, I, and D. Deniz looked toward those shelves, but did not rush ahead blindly. The rule had worked once because it had been tested against the labels and the recovered piece. Now it could be used again, carefully and in order.

The wall clock showed 10:06. Less than an hour remained.

Deniz picked up the scrap and took a steady breath. The pattern was no longer just a guess. It was a tested tool. Three pieces were still missing, and the shelves waited.

Deniz started with section B because piece 12 was predicted to belong there. The scrap of paper said it clearly: LAST DIGIT → SHELF LETTER.

Twelve ended in 2, so B was the first section to test. Deniz hurried to the B shelves, then stopped before touching anything. Guessing would only make the search messy. The labels showed 2, 12, and 22. Deniz checked each pocket in order, looking for blue map paper or a number on a corner.

Nothing was in the pocket marked 12. But a stiff edge poked out from behind a card marked 22.

Deniz slid the card aside. A large black 12 stared back.

“Yes!” Deniz whispered.

Piece 12 showed the library fountain. It fit the next empty space on the map. Deniz carried it to the table, matched the edges, and pressed it beneath the clear cover. Two blank spaces remained.

The clock read 10:18.

Next came piece 19. Its last digit was 9, and the shelf pattern said 9 belonged with I. Deniz crossed off B on the scrap and moved to section I.

The pocket marked 19 was empty too. Deniz searched slowly. Behind a card marked 29, something green was folded against the back wall. Deniz pulled it free and saw the number 19.

The picture showed the hilltop weather station. Its shape matched one of the map’s empty spaces.

Deniz studied the nearby cards. Piece 19 had stayed in the correct lettered section, just as piece 7 had, but it was behind the wrong numbered pocket. The substitute helper had apparently used the lettered sections correctly but guessed at the exact pockets. That explained why the pieces were difficult to find without being truly lost.

Deniz fitted piece 19 into the map. Only piece 24 was missing.

The final prediction was D. Piece 24 ended in 4, and the shelves showed 4, 14, and 24 under D. Deniz checked the clock while walking there.

“10:31,” Deniz said. “Twenty-nine minutes. That is enough if the rule keeps working.”

At section D, the pocket marked 24 was empty. Deniz checked behind 14, then behind 4. No map piece appeared. For one quick moment, worry squeezed Deniz’s stomach. Maybe the pattern had failed. Maybe piece 24 had been moved somewhere else.

Then Deniz remembered the other two discoveries: the pieces had remained in their lettered sections even when the numbered pockets were wrong.

Deniz scanned the whole D shelf from left to right. Behind a thick folder labeled 34, a corner of yellow paper showed.

There it was.

Piece 24 had been pushed behind 34, just as 12 had been pushed behind 22 and 19 behind 29. The same shelving mistake had happened three times. The last digit had pointed to the correct lettered section each time; the substitute helper had guessed the exact pocket incorrectly.

Deniz ran back to the map table with piece 24 held flat in both hands. It showed the old train station. The final edges slid into place.

The map was whole.

Deniz checked every join: fountain beside the market, weather station above the hill path, train station along the western road, and the footbridge in the east. All four pieces were secure beneath the clear cover.

At 10:47, voices sounded in the hallway. The first Explorer Club children arrived early, carrying water bottles and small notebooks. The treasure hunt had not begun yet, but the map was ready. Deniz placed the clue cards around it and straightened the pencils.

One child leaned over the table. “Is the treasure hunt ready?”

Deniz smiled and looked at the four restored places on the map. “Ready.”

The search had not been solved by choosing the most exciting guess. Deniz had tested the bin, the sign-in record, and finally the number pattern. The pattern had identified the correct shelf sections for every missing piece, while the repeated pocket mistakes explained why the pieces had seemed lost.

As the children gathered for the start, Deniz kept the scrap of paper nearby. LAST DIGIT → SHELF LETTER was still written across it in thick pencil. The map was assembled before 11:00, and Deniz already knew one thing to suggest afterward: the shelves needed labels that made the rule impossible to miss.

At eleven o’clock, the supervisor clapped twice. “Explorer Club, gather around the map!”

The children leaned closer as Deniz explained the first clue. They traced the town road with their fingers, then hurried to the fountain, where a blue envelope waited beneath a bench. Each clue led to the next place: the market, the hill path, the old train station, and finally the footbridge in the east.

Because the map was complete, nobody had to stop and wonder what belonged in the blank spaces. The children compared symbols, argued politely about directions, and checked their notebooks. When they found the last envelope, they cheered so loudly that people at the nearby tables smiled.

The treasure hunt had worked.

After the children began sorting their pencils and clue cards, Deniz carried the scrap of paper to the supervisor. The words LAST DIGIT → SHELF LETTER were still bold and clear.

“I found out what happened to the pieces,” Deniz said. “They stayed in the right lettered sections, but they were put behind the wrong number pockets. Piece 12 was behind 22, piece 19 was behind 29, and piece 24 was behind 34. Piece 7 was also in section G, but in the wrong pocket.”

The supervisor looked toward the shelves. “So someone followed the letters but guessed the exact numbers?”

“Probably,” Deniz said. “The pattern worked every time. The last digit showed the shelf letter, but the tens digit showed which pocket. The substitute helper may not have known that.”

The supervisor nodded slowly. “That would be easy to do when the labels are crowded.”

“That’s why I have an idea,” Deniz said. “Could we make the labels clearer? We could put a big letter at the top of each shelf, then write the pocket numbers in a straight row. Maybe we could add a small note: ‘Last digit tells the shelf letter.’ Then people would not have to guess.”

“That is a useful plan,” the supervisor said. “Let’s make the new labels next week, when the room is quieter. You can help me check that the numbers match.”

Deniz looked at the assembled map. The fountain, weather station, train station, and footbridge all fit together now. Earlier, the recycling bin had seemed like a likely answer. Then the sign-in log had made borrowing seem possible. Neither guess explained the evidence. The tested pattern did.

“Guessing gave me lots of possibilities,” Deniz said, “but testing the pattern showed which one was actually true.”

The supervisor smiled. “And it got the map ready on time.”

Across the room, one child waved. “Deniz! We found all the clues!”

“Coming!” Deniz called.

Deniz returned to the group, carrying the scrap of paper. The event was finished, the map was safe, and the new labels were planned. Next time, the shelves would make their pattern easier to see. For now, Deniz joined the children as they told the treasure hunt story all over again, each one sure that finding the last envelope had been the best part.
