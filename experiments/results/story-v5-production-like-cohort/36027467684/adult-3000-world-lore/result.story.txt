The Second Tide Line

Ada found the problem in a sentence no longer than a breath.

The draft exhibit panel stood on the reading room table beneath a sheet of clear protective film. Its heading read OLD HARBOR QUAY. Below a paragraph about changing shorelines and municipal works, one line declared: “The old quay was raised in 1962.”

The sentence looked finished. That was part of the trouble.

Ada, thirty-four and methodical by habit rather than temperament, took a pencil from behind her ear. She did not cross out the date. Not yet. Instead, she copied the sentence into her notebook and drew two columns beneath it.

DOCUMENTED.

INTERPRETED.

A date printed in a draft was not evidence simply because it had survived a round of editing. The panel needed a statement visitors could use, but usefulness did not require pretending that every gap had been filled. Ada had learned that lesson before, when a caption had compressed two separate events into one convenient date. The correction had been small, but the principle was not. A clear sentence could still be wrong in the way a straight line could fail to describe a crooked shore.

She read the panel again. The confident wording suggested one construction event, one year, and a clean division between the quay before and after. Perhaps that was what had happened. But “perhaps” belonged in the second column.

Her question had to be narrower than the panel's claim. Not, What year did someone intend to print? Not even, What year did the quay probably change? She wrote:

What can the available public record responsibly establish about when the old quay was raised, and what must remain interpretation?

The archive offered three kinds of evidence within her access: dated municipal map editions in the public map index, indexed maintenance summaries in the reading room, and the visible tide-height marks on the public quay. Each source had a different purpose. That difference mattered. A map showed a profile. A maintenance index described work or a condition. A mark on stone recorded something physical, though not necessarily in words.

At the edge of the archive's administrative plan was SEALED_STORE, an unavailable storage area. Ada knew it existed only as an administratively inaccessible part of the building. She had not entered it, did not know its contents, and would not use an unknown box to support a public claim. The boundary was not a challenge. It was a fact about the limits of this investigation.

She placed the draft panel in its folder and carried her notebook toward the public map index. The sentence about 1962 remained where it was, temporarily undisturbed. Ada's task was not to find a better-sounding date. It was to find out whether the date deserved to be there at all.

The public map index occupied a bank of shallow drawers and a digital catalogue arranged beside them. Ada began with the dated municipal editions, recording each relevant entry without filling gaps that the index did not fill.

The 1961 edition was available as a reproduction in the reading room. She spread it beneath the lamp and followed the harbor edge with a ruler. The old quay appeared there with its earlier profile. The line was not a verbal explanation, and the map did not say what had caused the profile or when anyone had altered it. Still, it was a dated representation of the quay as it appeared in 1961.

Ada entered the fact in her notebook:

1961 map: earlier quay profile shown.

She added a second line beneath it:

This establishes appearance in 1961, not the date of any later work.

The distinction felt almost excessively careful until she looked again at the draft panel. That sentence had treated a later condition as if it came with its own birth certificate. The map did not provide one.

In the indexed maintenance summaries, she located the 1964 entry. The wording was brief: repairs at the “raised west quay.” Ada copied the phrase exactly, including the quotation marks she used to show that it belonged to the record rather than to her own description.

1964 maintenance index: refers to repairs at the “raised west quay.”

She paused before writing anything else. The entry did not describe the exact construction event. It referred to repairs and used “raised west quay” as a name or condition already meaningful to the record at that time. It established that, by 1964, the west quay was being identified in that way. It did not state when the raising had occurred.

The temptation was immediate and familiar. If the 1961 map showed the earlier profile, and the 1964 index referred to the raised west quay, then 1962 seemed like a reasonable bridge. It might even be the year someone had chosen because it fit the sequence neatly.

Ada wrote the tempting inference in the margin:

Possible change around 1962.

Then she drew a line through “exact” where she had first begun to write it.

The map and the maintenance index were independent records, but they did different work. One depicted a profile at a dated moment. The other described later maintenance at a quay already called raised. Together they narrowed the interval: the change belonged after the earlier profile shown in 1961 and no later than the condition referred to in 1964. They did not select a single year inside that interval.

She turned back to the draft panel and considered whether “raised by 1964” would be enough. It was more defensible than 1962, but it might imply a simple, completed event when the physical evidence could complicate that picture. The map and index had brought her to the edge of the question, not across it.

Ada closed the catalogue and returned her notes to the folder. The public quay was only a short walk from the reading room, and its visible marks were part of the authorized evidence. She would examine them without asking them to say more than they could.

On the final page of her notes she wrote the interim account:

Documented interval: after the 1961 earlier profile; by 1964, repairs referred to the “raised west quay.”

Inferred exact date: none established.

The second line was not an admission of failure. It was the result the records had earned.

The public quay held the afternoon light in flat bands. Water moved below the edge, rising and withdrawing with a patient sound that made the stone seem older than any date attached to it.

Ada walked to the two visible tide-height marks she had come to examine. They were close enough to compare but not identical. One had a different style from the other, and they stood at different elevations. Neither carried a clear year.

She did not measure them. The difference itself was part of the available evidence, and the absence of dates was just as important. A mark could show that someone had recorded a height or boundary. It could not, by itself, announce when the quay had been raised or whether the mark belonged to construction, maintenance, or another public practice.

Ada opened her notebook.

Physical evidence: two visible tide-height marks; differing styles and elevations; no clear year on either.

She considered the first interpretation.

Interpretation One: a single raising occurred around 1962, and the two differing marks reflect related public or maintenance marking practices.

This was possible. The 1961 map showed the earlier profile, and by 1964 the index referred to the raised west quay. The marks did not contradict a change around 1962. A single event could explain the sequence, with the marks representing connected practices afterward or nearby.

But possible was not the same as supported. No available source stated 1962. The physical marks had no year, and their differences did not identify one event.

She considered the second interpretation.

Interpretation Two: the quay's alteration may have been phased, or it may represent more than one period of change, with the work occurring somewhere after the 1961 profile and by the 1964 maintenance reference.

This, too, was an interpretation. The marks did not conclusively prove phased construction. Their differing styles and elevations only made a single, precisely dated account less secure. They left open the possibility that the quay's visible history involved separate changes or practices rather than one clean transformation.

Ada looked from one mark to the other. History often acquired false confidence when different kinds of evidence were forced to agree too quickly. A map line, a maintenance phrase, and a mark on stone could belong in the same account without becoming the same kind of fact.

She wrote a final comparison beneath the two interpretations. The first preserved the possibility of work around 1962 but depended on an exactness none of the sources supplied. The second matched the documentary interval and respected the physical evidence's limits. It did not solve the date. It explained why the date remained unsettled.

The tide withdrew, exposing a darker band below the marks. Ada closed the notebook. The public record did not need her to choose the most dramatic explanation, and there was no drama in the uncertainty itself. Her responsibility was simpler and harder: select the account that the evidence could carry.

She returned to the reading room with the two interpretations clearly separated in her notes. One was possible. The other was better supported.

Back at the reading room table, Ada arranged the evidence in three groups.

The 1961 map reproduction lay to the left. Its dated image showed the earlier quay profile. In the center was her transcription of the 1964 maintenance index, with the phrase “raised west quay” underlined once. To the right she placed the notes from the public quay: two visible tide-height marks, different in style and elevation, neither carrying a clear year.

She did not let the three sources blur into one impression. The map documented an appearance. The index documented a later maintenance reference. The marks documented visible physical differences, but not their dates or exact purpose. Their independence gave the account strength; their differences imposed limits.

Ada returned to the two interpretations.

A single raising around 1962 remained possible. It fit between the dated records and could accommodate the marks if they reflected related public or maintenance practices. But no available source stated that year. The 1962 date belonged to the draft panel, not to the map, the maintenance index, or either physical mark. Treating it as established would turn a convenient inference into a fact.

The phased-or-undated interpretation was better supported. The 1961 map fixed one end of the documentary sequence: the earlier profile was still shown then. The 1964 index fixed the other useful point: by that time, repairs referred to the “raised west quay.” The tide marks, differing in style and elevation, left open whether the alteration had been phased or whether distinct periods of change and marking had become visible together. They could not fix a year, but they made a simple exact-date narrative less secure.

Ada wrote that distinction in full before touching the panel:

The sources document a sequence. The interpretation explains that sequence. Neither establishes 1962 as an exact construction date.

Then she removed the sentence that claimed certainty and drafted its replacement.

“The 1961 map shows the quay in its earlier profile. By 1964, the maintenance index refers to repairs at the ‘raised west quay.’ Two visible tide-height marks, different in style and elevation, suggest that the quay’s changes or marking practices may belong to more than one period. The available public records therefore place the raising after the 1961 profile and by the 1964 reference, but do not establish a single construction year. Work around 1962 remains possible, not proven.”

Ada read it aloud, listening for the point at which a documented fact might quietly become an assertion. The first sentence stayed with the map. The second stayed with the index. The third named what the marks could suggest without declaring what they proved. The final sentence gave the public an interval and an honest limit.

She considered shortening it. A panel had to be readable. But removing the uncertainty would make the sentence smoother by making the history weaker. She revised only the rhythm, not the meaning.

“The 1961 map shows the quay’s earlier profile, while the 1964 maintenance index refers to repairs at the ‘raised west quay.’ Two visible tide-height marks differ in style and elevation, though neither carries a clear year. These sources suggest that the quay was altered or raised sometime after the 1961 profile and by the 1964 reference. The change may have been phased or may reflect more than one period of work or marking. An exact construction date cannot be established from the available public records; 1962 is possible, but not proven.”

That was the account she could defend. It did not erase 1962. It put the date back into its proper category: a possibility produced by interpretation, not a fact supplied by evidence.

Ada copied the revised wording into the panel file and marked the original sentence for removal. No supervisor appeared to approve her choice, and no outside authority was needed to make it valid. The records had been compared. The limits had been stated. The conclusion followed from the work.

She gathered the map, the index notes, and the quay observations into one folder. The public account was ready. What remained was to place uncertainty where visitors could see it.

Ada finalized the panel in the reading room, where daylight crossed the table and caught the clear film above the new text.

It now read:

“The 1961 map shows the quay’s earlier profile. By 1964, the maintenance index refers to repairs at the ‘raised west quay.’ Two visible tide-height marks differ in style and elevation, but neither carries a clear year. The quay was therefore altered or raised sometime after the 1961 profile and by the 1964 reference. The change may have been phased or may reflect more than one period of work or marking. The exact construction date remains uncertain. 1962 is possible, but not established by the available public records.”

Ada checked the sequence once more. Earlier profile in 1961. Raised west quay in the 1964 maintenance reference. Differing physical marks without clear years. A bounded interval, two plausible readings, and the better-supported interpretation stated without disguise.

She entered the unresolved question in the archive's public record: the exact year of construction could not be established from the available evidence. It was not a defect to be hidden or an invitation to invent an answer. It was part of what the record responsibly said.

When visitors read the panel, they would receive more than a date. They would receive a supported history and a reason for its limits. The old confident sentence was gone, but the account had not become empty. It was more accurate because it preserved what remained unknown.

Ada placed the corrected panel with the exhibit materials and closed her notebook. The tide marks still waited on the public quay, carrying their differences without dates. The archive could not make them speak more clearly than they did.

It could, however, make sure their silence was recorded honestly.
