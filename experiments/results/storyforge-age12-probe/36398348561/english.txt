Route Two

The living room floor was quiet, but Deniz had decided it wasn't going to stay that way for long. A rescue mission was required, and the terrain was going to be hostile.

First, the terrain itself. Deniz dragged the small blanket off the arm of the sofa and snapped it out flat over the carpet. The woven pattern of tiny blue diamonds looked, with a little imagination, exactly like a murky, bubbling swamp. To make the crossing genuinely dangerous, Deniz placed the two large sofa cushions on the blanket, with a gap between them. That gap was the deep water. Anything that fell in was considered lost to the mud, at least until it could be fished out by hand.

The toy dinosaur—the mission's stranded cargo—was placed at the very edge of the blanket. It had a long tail and a stubborn look in its painted eye. It was the cargo.

For the bridge, Deniz took an empty cardboard box and stood it up on its side. The box formed a high, curved arch over the gap between the cushions. It looked impressive. It looked like a proper shortcut.

Deniz sat back on the heels and pulled the notebook and pencil closer. Drawing a quick, messy diagram of the layout, Deniz sketched the blanket, the two cushion islands, and the towering box arch. Underneath the drawing, in careful, blocky letters, Deniz wrote: *ROUTE 1: THE GREAT ARCH.* Beside the box, a small note was added: *Risky Part. Could wobble. But fast.*

It was true. If the dinosaur could just get up the slope of the box and down the other side, the crossing would take two seconds. No long detours through the swamp. Just a quick dash over the top.

Deniz picked up the dinosaur and set it at the base of the cardboard slope.

"Okay," Deniz muttered, using a finger to gently nudge the dinosaur forward. "Easy does it. Don't look down."

The dinosaur climbed. The cardboard held. Deniz pushed a little harder to get the toy over the curve of the arch.

Instantly, the whole structure betrayed them.

The box didn't just wobble; it jumped. The bottom edge of the cardboard scraped loudly against the smooth blanket, sliding a good three inches to the left. The sudden movement threw off the angle. The arch tipped sideways, lost its balance, and collapsed into the gap between the cushions. The dinosaur tumbled down the inside of the folding cardboard and landed face-first in the "deep water" on the blanket.

Silence.

Deniz stared at the wreckage. Then, slowly, a hand reached into the gap and retrieved the dinosaur, brushing a piece of lint off its snout. The toy was fine, of course. It was plastic. But the route was a disaster.

"Structural failure," Deniz announced to the empty room, setting the dinosaur safely on a cushion. "Total meltdown."

Deniz didn't just rebuild the arch and hope for the best. That was amateur thinking. Deniz pushed the box back into position and pressed a single finger against its side, pushing gently. The box slid instantly, skating across the blanket with almost no resistance. Deniz pushed again, lighter this time. It slid again.

The evidence was right there. The blanket was too smooth. The box was too light. There was no grip at all between the two surfaces. The moment any real weight—like a dinosaur—started pushing down on that high, curved slope, the box's bottom edge slid sideways and the whole thing turned into a slide.

Deniz pulled the notebook over and wrote down the findings: *Route 1 Failed. Box slides on blanket. No grip. Too light + too smooth = collapse.*

Staring at the failed sketch, Deniz tapped the pencil against the page. The problem wasn't the gap; it was the shape and the weight. A high arch concentrates weight on tiny points and needs a heavy base. A flat shape spreads the weight out and stays put.

"Right," Deniz said. "We go low. We go wide."

The redesign began. Instead of standing the box up, Deniz flattened it out completely, opening the flaps until it was just a long, wide sheet of double-thick cardboard. This created a solid platform. To make sure the flaps didn't sag or fold in the middle, Deniz took a short length of ordinary string and tied it tightly around the middle of the flattened board, cinching it together so the flaps stayed together.

Next, the cushions. Deniz pressed them down hard, flattening them slightly so they were firmer and less squishy. They looked less like soft islands now and more like solid stepping stones.

The new layout was different. The flattened box was laid across the start of the blanket, resting on the carpet, acting as a stable ramp onto the first cushion. Then, the second cushion was placed close enough to the first that the dinosaur could just step across. No high arches. No unstable slopes. Just a flat, heavy-looking path.

Deniz drew the new map in the notebook. *ROUTE 2: THE LOW ROAD.* Underneath, the notes read: *Flattened box. Tied with string. Firm cushions. Should hold.*

Time for the second test.

Deniz placed the dinosaur at the start of the flattened cardboard ramp. A finger pushed the toy forward. The box didn't budge. The weight of the dinosaur was spread across the wide surface, and the cardboard gripped the carpet underneath rather than sliding on the blanket.

The dinosaur climbed the gentle ramp. It reached the top and stepped onto the first cushion. The cushion was firm, pressed flat, and didn't wobble. Deniz nudged the dinosaur across the fabric. It stepped onto the second cushion. Still firm. Still steady.

One more push, and the dinosaur hopped off the second cushion, landing safely on the bare floor at the far edge of the blanket. Out of the swamp. Safe and dry.

Deniz picked up the dinosaur, inspected it for cracks, and found none. It had made it across in one piece.

Sitting back, Deniz looked at the notebook. Next to the messy, collapsed sketch of Route 1, Deniz wrote a neat description of Route 2, exactly noting what had changed: *Slope to flat. Loose to tied. High to low.*

But a single success wasn't enough for a proper experiment. Deniz placed the dinosaur back at the very beginning and ran the whole course one last time, a clean pass from start to finish. The ramp held. The cushions held. The dinosaur traveled smoothly across the entire swamp without a single slip and was set down gently on the far edge, looking out over the blue diamond water it had conquered.

Deniz uncapped the pencil—metaphorically, since it was a standard wooden pencil—and drew a sharp, decisive checkmark next to *ROUTE 2*.

The notebook snapped shut. The swamp was crossed.
