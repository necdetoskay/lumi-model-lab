The Chalk Arrow

Ece liked the shared garden table because it was just the right height for small hands. On it sat their seed box, filled with tiny pots, soil, and packets of seeds. Mert had placed the box there that morning. Ece was ready to label the pots when she noticed a fresh white chalk arrow on the ground. It pointed from the table toward the public watering shelf. “Look,” said Mert. He lifted the top tray of the seed box. “The blue plant-label packet is gone.” His eyebrows pulled together. “Maybe someone moved it. Maybe someone borrowed it.” Ece felt a little worried too, but she shook her head gently. “We saw that the tray is empty,” she said. “We saw that the arrow points to the shelf. But we do not know where the packet went, or who made the arrow.” Mert nodded. Ece remembered once blaming the wind for a missing paper label. She had found it tucked under her own notebook. “Let’s follow only what we can see,” she said. Together, they left the seed box safely on the table.

First, they looked carefully around the table. The seed box was still there. The top tray was still empty, and no blue packet lay under the table legs. Chalk dust marked the beginning of the arrow. Ece and Mert followed its direction along the open garden path. They passed green beds with little plant labels poking from the soil. Some labels were yellow, some white, and one was pink. None was the blue packet. The path stayed open beneath the bright sky. Soon they could see the public watering shelf beside a row of pots. Farther away stood the closed greenhouse door. It was only something they could see from the public path. Mert glanced toward it. People sometimes talked about a glass passage beyond the closed door. “We are not going through the door,” Ece said. “And we are not investigating a passage we have only heard about. Following a visible arrow is different from following a rumor.” Mert agreed. They stayed in the open garden and walked to the shelf. The arrow ended in that direction, but it did not say who had drawn it or why. Near the shelf, Ece turned to Mert. “Before we decide what it means, tell me exactly what you see.”

Mert looked slowly, as if his eyes were little garden rakes. “I see the watering shelf,” he said. “I see the arrow pointing here. I see that the blue packet is not in the seed box’s top tray. And I see the seed box is still on the table.” “Good,” said Ece. “Those are things we saw.” Mert thought. “I guessed that someone borrowed the packet. I also guessed the arrow was meant for our project.” “Those are guesses,” Ece said. “They might be true, but we do not know.” She crouched beside the shelf. “We can add another guess: perhaps the packet was carried here and set down.” Mert looked relieved that this was possible, but he did not claim it was the answer. Ece remembered the label under her notebook. Small things could hide in ordinary places. “Let’s check beside the shelf, under its lower board, and around the pots,” she said. “We can look for evidence before we blame anyone.” Mert nodded. They searched the public shelf and the nearby path. They did not touch the closed greenhouse door. They did not need to. Their question was about the seed-box project, and the shelf was right in front of them.

Mert moved one empty watering can. Ece peered beside a stack of small pots. Then sunlight flashed on something blue beside the bottom edge of the public watering shelf. “There!” she cried. She reached down and picked up the packet of blue plant labels. It was plainly visible from the garden path, though it had been easy to miss among the pots and tools. Mert opened his mouth, then smiled. “Our labels are here! The project is not missing them.” He held the packet carefully with both hands. “So someone did not steal them.” Ece paused. “We can say the packet was missing from the top tray. We can say we found it beside the shelf. We cannot say who moved it.” Mert looked at the chalk arrow behind them. “The packet’s place fits the arrow’s direction.” “Yes,” said Ece. “That is something we can notice. But it does not prove the arrow directed us to the packet. It does not prove who drew it, either, or exactly what the arrow meant.” Mert considered this. The arrow might have been connected to the packet. It might not have been. They had no evidence for more. Still, they had found what their project needed without entering any closed place or accusing anyone. Mert tucked the packet against his shirt. “I feel much better,” he said. Ece smiled. “Me too. Let’s take it back and decide what we really know.”

At the shared table, Mert placed the blue packet beside the seed box. Then he put it safely in the top tray. “Thank you for checking instead of accusing someone,” he said. Ece smiled. “We can tell the truth simply: we saw the arrow, and we found the packet by the shelf.” “But we do not know who drew the arrow or why,” Mert added. “Exactly.” The chalk arrow stayed on the path, harmless and unanswered. Ece opened the packet, and she and Mert began labeling the little pots. Blue labels stood neatly in the soil beside green shoots. They worked together in the open garden, asking before assuming and checking before blaming. Ece still wondered about the arrow, but she knew something more important: not every unanswered question was a failure. Sometimes helping a friend meant finding the facts first.
