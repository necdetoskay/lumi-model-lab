# The Compass and the Storm

Ekin had been standing at the harbour stall for a full minute, turning the brass compass over in her palms, when the shadow fell across her. It came from above and behind, fast and silent, and by the time she looked up, a broad-bodied dragon, no taller than Ekin's waist, had landed on the pier, its tail curling around the stall's post. Its wings were folded like a grey cloak, its belly wide and pale, and it didn't roar or breathe fire at all. It simply leaned down, its long neck stretching over her shoulder, and plucked the compass from her hands with two neat claws, and the short braided cord that had been looped around her wrist snapped and stayed behind.

"Hey—that's mine!" Ekin grabbed at empty air. The dragon's wing swept backward as it turned, knocking a stack of paper receipts off the stall and scattering them across the wet stone like startled gulls. The fishmonger swore and ducked. The dragon paid no attention to any of it. It lifted the compass to its eye, tilted its head as if admiring the shine, and then launched itself off the pier with three powerful wingbeats.

Ekin ran. The dragon rose, banked, and she lost it against the bright grey sky, her heart hammering. She spun around, searching the rooftops, the masts, the breakwater—then a voice behind her said, "You're going the wrong way. The lighthouse."

She turned. A girl her own age stood there, arms crossed, dark hair whipping in the wind. Ekin had seen her around the harbour before but had never spoken to her.

"It went toward the cliffs," Ekin said.

"It went toward the lighthouse." The girl pointed. "See the window? There's a glint in it. Metal catches the light."

Ekin squinted. High on the old lighthouse's seaward face, in a broken upper window, something flashed—brief, brass-bright, gone.

"That's my grandfather's compass," Ekin said. "He used to take me out along the coast with it. I was just looking at it. That dragon just—"

"Took it. Yes." The girl was already walking, briskly, toward the path that climbed the headland. "Are you coming or not?"

Ekin hesitated for one breath, then ran to catch up.

"I'm Duru, by the way," the girl said, without looking back. "This is the coast path. Stay on it."

The path ran above the tide line, dry and sheltered, winding along the shoulder of the headland toward the lighthouse. Below it, the shore was a chaos of wet stone and rock pools, and it looked shorter—Ekin could see the lighthouse directly across the jumble of rocks.

"The shore's faster," she said.

"The shore's where the waves will be in half an hour." Duru nodded at the horizon, where a wall of dark cloud had begun to lean against the sky. The wind had picked up since the harbour, and whitecaps were showing far out. "Look at the tide line on those rocks. See the dark stain? The water reaches there when it blows from the west. That's where we're standing if we go down."

Ekin looked. The stain was high—higher than she'd guessed. She felt a small, stubborn itch to argue anyway, just because Duru had said it like a teacher, but the wind chose that moment to shove her sideways, and she decided the path was fine. It was fine.

They climbed in a rattling silence broken by Duru pointing things out despite herself: the old anchor chain half-buried in the grass, the hollow where fishermen used to dry their nets. "Nobody's been up to the lighthouse in years," Duru said. "The keeper moved to the mainland. But the door—"

"The door's open," Ekin said.

It stood where the path ended, a heavy iron-banded thing set into the tower's upper side, ajar for the first time in what looked like a very long while. Sand and gull feathers had drifted across the threshold. A smell came out of the dark inside—salt, and something warmer, like sun-baked stone.

Duru stopped with her hand on the door. "I always wanted to see inside," she admitted, quietly, as if confessing to something.

"Then let's go," Ekin said, and slipped past her through the opening.

The door opened straight onto a landing of cold air and drifting light, and a few steps took them inside. Daylight poured through a wide broken window, and the tower opened into a broad room—dry, bright, littered with old machinery and dust. And in the far corner, heaped against the wall beneath the broken glass, was the dragon.

The room was bigger than Ekin had expected, and stranger. The wind moaned through the broken window in low, shifting notes, like someone humming behind a closed door. The glass in the remaining panes had gone cloudy with salt, so the light came through thick and pearly. Old winch gears stood along one wall under shrouds of dust, and every bright thing in the room had been collected into one corner: a brass ship's bell, a lantern with a polished reflector, a handful of doorknobs, a tin lid, a fisherman's lucky coin, all piled in a wide, shallow nest of dried grass and rope ends.

And on top of the pile, unmistakable, lay the compass.

Ekin's chest tightened. There it was—her grandfather's compass, dented at the edge where she'd once dropped it, the needle casing fogged with salt from a hundred mornings at the shore. The dragon was curled around the nest, its body filling the whole corner, its head resting on its forelegs. Its eye was open. It was watching them.

"Don't move yet," Duru whispered.

"Why not—"

"Because of that." Duru pointed, very slightly, with her chin.

Ekin followed her look and saw it: a small shape at the edge of the nest, hardly bigger than a cat, peering out from behind its mother's wing. A hatchling—sleek, grey-green, with enormous eyes and a damp, unfinished look about it, the way kittens have. It was nudging at the tin lid with its nose, utterly absorbed.

"It's a baby," Ekin breathed.

"And the mother is between us and the whole room," Duru said. "So we wait. We watch. We don't walk up to a dragon's nest and grab things, even if—"

But Ekin had already stopped listening, because one of the dragon's wings had shifted, just an inch, and beneath it she could see the compass's brass edge catching the pearly light. Every hiss and rustle from the mother felt aimed at her, like a dare. Duru wasn't the boss of this. It was her compass. Her grandfather's.

She noticed, distantly, a narrow doorway in the far wall, and a staircase corkscrewing down into the dark of the tower's lower floor. From somewhere below came the sound of waves—close, hollow, drumming against stone. The sea was already speaking through the building.

"Wait," Duru said again, sharper now.

Ekin didn't.

She crossed the room in six quick strides and reached for the compass.

The mother came off the nest like a door slamming. One wing flared wide, wider than Ekin's outstretched arms, and the hiss that came out of the animal was pure fury and pure warning all at once, and Ekin stumbled backward, her hand never touching the brass. The compass, knocked by the edge of the wing, rolled off the nest, bounced once on the stone floor, and skittered to the narrow doorway, where it tipped over the top step and went clattering down the narrow corkscrew stairs, bouncing from step to step until it landed on the floor of the lower room.

The hatchling shrieked, a sound like a kettle coming to the boil, and bolted. Ekin saw it go—saw it pour itself down the same narrow staircase in a tumble of claws and tail, vanishing round the turn of the stair and into the lower dark after a hiding place only it knew. The mother lunged after it, got her head and shoulders into the stairwell, and jammed. Her chest was simply too broad for the turning stair. She shoved, wings battering the doorframe, dust and grit raining down, and got nowhere. She was stuck, half-in, half-out, hissing down into the dark where her baby had gone.

"She can't fit," Duru said. She had not moved from her spot, and her voice was tight. "The stairs turn too tight. She can't follow it down."

"Then I'll—"

"No." Duru caught her arm. "Wait. Look."

Ekin leaned into the stairwell, peering down the spiral. The little stair was short, one tight spiral that opened onto the lower room; from the upper turn she could see down into the gloom, where the compass lay on the stone floor near the seaward wall—and beyond the compass, a wide opening in that wall, closed by a fixed iron grille, and beyond the grille, the sea. The waves were no longer drumming from a distance. They were coming through. White water surged against the bars, and spray was already spitting through the gaps, wetting the floor in a growing, creeping sheet.

A swell surged harder than the rest. Water foamed through the grille, rushed across the floor, and dragged the compass with it. Ekin watched it scrape over the stone, catch for one horrible half-second on the bars, and then slide through the gap and out, into the grey churn, gone. Only the cord, still hanging from her wrist where it had snapped at the harbour, was left to her.

"No," she said. "No, no—"

Below, the hatchling scrambled up onto a dry stone ledge at the foot of the stairs, only a few steps below the turn, out of the water's reach, and pressed itself there, shivering, its eyes two dots of reflected white.

The wind rose. The waves rose. The mother, wedged in the stairwell above them, hissed down into the dark at her baby, at the water, at everything, and could do nothing at all.

For a long moment Ekin couldn't look away from the grille. The compass was out there, somewhere under the grey water, and her whole body wanted to run down those stairs and find some way—any way—to get it back.

"We have to go," Duru said. "The water's coming up. This whole floor will be under it."

"But it's right there—"

"It's gone, Ekin. It's in the sea."

Ekin knew it. She could see it. Some stubborn part of her refused to accept it, and that part was shouting down every other thought. Then the hatchling cried out again—small, high, desperate—from its ledge, and the mother thrashed against the doorframe above, and Ekin felt something in her chest turn over.

"I scared it," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "It ran because of me. It's down there because of me. The compass too—if I hadn't lunged at it—"

"Yes," Duru said. She didn't soften it and she didn't pile on. She just said it, and then she said: "So what are you going to do?"

Ekin looked at the stairs, at the hatchling on its ledge, at the water climbing the floor below in slow, glittering advances. She thought about the dry room above, and the open door, and the path home, and how easy it would be to just leave. To run. The compass was lost either way.

"Help me," she said.

Duru's jaw was set and her eyes were angry, and she stayed. "The ledge near the bottom is still dry," she said. "If it comes to the stairs, we can get it up."

Ekin went down. The steps were dry all the way down the spiral; the water had not reached them. She crouched on the lowest dry step, a few steps above the ledge, opened her jacket, and held it out—not toward the hatchling, not chasing it, just open, like a nest, like a suggestion. The hatchling pressed itself back against the stone, hissing, its whole small body shaking.

"I know," Ekin said softly. "I know. I'm the one who scared you. I'm sorry."

Behind and above her, Duru stood one step higher, watching the lower room over Ekin's shoulder, calling the water level: "Still dry. Still dry. Coming up on the left—move your foot." Her voice steadied Ekin more than she could say.

The swell came, and the ledge began to vanish under the foam, and the hatchling had nowhere left to retreat except toward the stairs—toward Ekin. It hesitated, trembling. Ekin didn't grab. She waited, jacket open, heart pounding, and the wave decided for all of them: the hatchling sprang up the last steps, landed against her chest, and burrowed into the jacket like it had always lived there.

Ekin rose, one hand cupping the small warm weight, and climbed the same steps back up. Duru took her arm on the upper steps, steadied her, and pulled her through the doorway into the dry light of the upper room. The mother was already off the stairwell, wings wide, and Ekin walked straight to her and knelt and let the hatchling tumble out of her jacket onto the nest.

The mother's head came down over her baby. The hiss this time was low, and brief, and ended.

Ekin backed away, slowly, and the dragon let her. She stood beside Duru in the middle of the room, wind howling through the broken window, and watched as the mother folded herself around the hatchling, one great wing drawing over it like a roof. The dragon did not speak. It did not nod or thank them. It did not reach into the nest and bring out a gift, and Ekin understood, in a way that settled into her bones, that it never would. The hatchling peeked out from under the wing once, looked at her with its enormous eyes, and tucked itself back in.

"Come on," Duru said. "Before the path gets wet."

They went back across the dry room, away from the narrow stair and the flooding room below it, to the door they'd come through, and out into the wind. The coast path was still dry, still sheltered, though the sea below had climbed to its dark stain on the rocks and the rain was starting in slanting sheets. Duru led. At the fork where the path bent inland, she called back, "This way—the low ground floods," and Ekin followed without a word, without arguing, without even feeling the usual itch to. It wasn't obedience, exactly. It was just that Duru was right, and Ekin could admit it now.

They walked a while in the rain's soft noise. Duru didn't mock her about the compass, and somehow that was worse and better than teasing all at once.

"It's really gone," Ekin said at last.

"Yes."

"I keep thinking I'll see it on the beach tomorrow. Washed up. But I won't, will I."

"No."

Ekin's hand went to her pocket, where she had stuffed the short braided cord on the way out—a cord with nothing on the end of it now. She wound it around her fingers, once, twice, feeling the emptiness of it, and found that she didn't want to hide it or lie about what had happened. She would tell her grandfather the truth, all of it, including her own part. The dragon took it, and I lunged, and the sea took it. The words were not easy, but they were hers.

Duru had gone a few paces ahead, and Ekin let her. "Left at the fork," Duru called back, not mocking. "I know," Ekin said.

Duru glanced back, and something in her face had softened. "Watch the wet stones," she said. "Thanks," Ekin said. The word came out easily this time.

The rain kept falling, and the harbour lights wavered ahead. Out past the breakwater, the grey water kept rolling in and out. Ahead, Duru slowed a little, though she didn't look back.

Ekin turned the empty cord around her fingers once more, the loose end brushing her knuckles, and hurried to catch up with Duru.
