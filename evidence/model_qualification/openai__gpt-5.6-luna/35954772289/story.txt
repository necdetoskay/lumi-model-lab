Beyond the Cloud Bridge

Selin stood at the edge of her floating island and looked across the open sky. On the neighboring island, a small message capsule had been lost near one of its windmills. She wanted to bring it home, but the space between the islands was not an ordinary gap.

Cloud bridges appeared only once each day, during the low-sun hour. They formed when the islands' wind patterns turned the windmills in the right rhythm. When the sun sank farther and the central shadow reached the base of the tallest windmill, the bridge faded away.

Selin watched the three windmills nearest her. Their sails creaked and swung, pointing first east, then slightly south. “The wind is still deciding,” she murmured. She pictured herself hurrying onto a bridge that twisted in the air, and her stomach gave a small, sensible flutter. Danger was not a reason to quit. It was a reason to prepare.

She spread her choices on the grass. A heavy blanket stayed behind. So did her large cooking pot and a box of books. Into her light satchel she placed a small bottle of water, a bright wind ribbon, and a looped cord. The ribbon would show which way the air moved. The cord might help her hold or secure something. She checked each knot twice.

Then Selin fastened the satchel close to her back and studied the shadows beneath the windmills. The low-sun hour was still ahead. She would wait at the bridge approach, watch the sails, and decide only when the wind gave her enough information.

Ready at last, she stepped toward the path—but not onto the sky. The bridge window and the safest wind direction were still uncertain.

Selin reached the bridge approach just as the sun lowered toward the low-sun hour. She set her satchel down for a moment and watched the windmills. Their sails had been turning east and slightly south, but now they jerked in different directions.

A high bank of clouds had slid between the islands and the sun. It bent the wind downward and around the floating rocks. The tallest windmill groaned, spun half a turn, and stopped. Below it, the smaller mills whipped their sails toward one another.

Selin looked at the direct path she had planned. If the bridge formed there, the wind would push from the side and twist the cloud steps. Even before the bridge appeared, loose wisps of cloud were sliding across that route.

She tightened the strap of her satchel. “Not that way,” she said. Her voice sounded small beneath the creaking sails, but her decision felt steady. She would not hurry onto an unstable bridge just because the waiting was nearly over.

Near the tallest windmill stood a low stone shelter, tucked behind its round base. Selin carried her satchel there and waited where the wall blocked the strongest gusts. She held the bright wind ribbon by its loop and let it hang.

At first, the ribbon snapped straight toward the direct path. Then it fluttered, dipped, and pulled around the shelter toward a narrow space between two drifting islands. Selin watched the windmills to check the ribbon’s message. One small mill turned its sails into that same opening. Then the second followed. The tallest windmill began to turn more smoothly too.

Selin marked the safer direction in her mind: not straight across, but along the sheltered opening. She checked her water, touched the looped cord, and waited a little longer. The bridge would appear only during its proper low-sun hour; waiting could not create another one, but rushing could waste the one coming.

The central shadow crept close to the tallest windmill’s base. At last, pale cloud steps gathered along the safer route, linking the two islands. The bridge had formed, but the shadows were already lengthening. Selin lifted her satchel. There was little time to cross and search.

Selin lifted one foot toward the first cloud step. Before placing it down, she pressed her toe against the pale puff. It dipped, then sprang gently back.

“Strong enough,” she whispered, though she kept her knees bent.

She held the wind ribbon in one hand. It streamed toward the narrow opening, showing her the direction the smaller windmills had chosen. With the other hand, she kept her satchel close against her side. The water tugged at the bag, and the looped cord bumped softly against her hip.

Step by step, Selin crossed.

The cloud bridge was not a solid road. Each step trembled when a gust slipped between the islands. Some spaces were wider than her shoe, so she waited for the next cloud puff to drift into place. She never jumped. She tested, balanced, and moved only when the ribbon fluttered steadily ahead.

Behind her, the home island grew smaller. Ahead, the neighboring island’s rocky edge came nearer. The bridge thinned beneath the long shadow of the tallest windmill. Selin glanced back once. The central shadow was creeping toward its base.

“I have to keep moving,” she said, “but not too quickly.”

At last, her foot touched the neighboring island. The final cloud step shivered and faded behind her.

Selin followed the windmill’s turning sails around its round stone tower. There, beneath the outer platform, she saw a small silver capsule caught in a pocket of shadow. Its red thread was tangled around a wooden support, and one end rested beyond a narrow ledge.

Selin lay flat and looked over the edge. Wind curled upward from the open air below. The ledge was scarcely wider than her hand, and loose pebbles clicked down into the clouds.

She reached out, then stopped.

Her fingers were still far from the capsule. To grab it, she would have to put her weight on the narrow stone, where the wind could pull her off balance. Selin drew her hand back and tightened her satchel strap.

The capsule was close enough to see, but not safe enough to take. She would need a better plan.

Selin studied the ledge again. The wind ribbon snapped upward, then drooped. A gust was rushing past, but between gusts the ribbon hung almost still.

“Not yet,” Selin said.

She took the looped cord from her satchel and searched for a solid place to anchor it. The windmill’s wooden support stood above the capsule, thick and steady. Selin wrapped the cord around it twice and pulled hard. It held.

She tied one end around her wrist, then lowered the loop toward the capsule. The first try missed. The second brushed the silver case but slid away when the wind curled up from below.

Selin did not lean farther. She watched the ribbon instead.

When it lifted gently toward the windmill’s sails, she waited. When it fluttered wildly, she waited again. The windmill creaked and turned, one sail after another, while the shadow beneath the platform shifted like a slow clock hand.

At last, the ribbon streamed steadily outward.

Selin lowered the loop. It caught the capsule’s red thread. She pulled a little, then stopped. The thread tightened around the wooden support. With careful fingers, she loosened the snag by drawing the cord sideways, not straight up. The red thread slipped free.

Selin pulled again. The capsule scraped over the stone and came within reach. She clasped it with both hands and checked that its silver lid was still closed. Then she tied the capsule firmly against the inside of her satchel, using the cord twice around the bag’s flap.

A long shadow touched the base of the tallest windmill.

Behind her, the cloud bridge shivered. One by one, its pale steps thinned into mist until the path between the islands was gone.

Selin felt a small, worried flutter in her chest, but she did not run to the edge. The rule had held. The bridge would return only at the next low-sun hour, when the windmills made the right pattern again.

She moved beneath the windmill’s broad platform, where the stone was sheltered from the gusts. With the capsule safe against her side, she marked the wind’s turns in her mind and settled down to wait.

The next low-sun hour arrived slowly. Selin had watched the windmills turn through the night, remembering each change. Now the tallest windmill’s shadow shortened, and the smaller sails began to point in the same direction.

Selin stood and checked her satchel. The silver capsule rested against the inside flap. She tightened the looped cord once more, pulling until the knot held firm. Then she tucked the wind ribbon where she could see it easily and took one careful sip of water.

The windmills aligned.

Across the open air, pale cloud steps gathered between the islands. They did not appear all at once. First came a short white curl, then another, until the bridge stretched along the sheltered route Selin had found before.

“Now,” she said, and stepped onto the first cloud step.

It dipped beneath her boot but held. Selin tested each step before placing her weight on it. The satchel stayed close against her side, and the capsule made a tiny, reassuring tap against the cord.

Halfway across, the wind changed.

A crosswind swept between the islands. The cloud bridge bent sideways, its steps leaning like a row of pale leaves. Selin stopped at once. Her stomach gave a nervous flip, but she did not hurry forward.

She lifted the wind ribbon. It snapped toward the left, then fluttered down. A stronger gust was passing. Selin lowered her foot back onto the step and waited.

The ribbon lifted again, this time in a steadier stream toward the sheltered opening. The nearest windmill turned with it. Selin remembered the safe direction from the first crossing and shifted one step at a time, keeping her shoulders turned into the wind.

Another gust bent the bridge. Selin paused again. The central shadow was creeping toward the tallest windmill’s base, and the bridge would not last forever. Still, she watched, waited, and moved only when the ribbon streamed steadily.

Step by careful step, she reached the home island. The last cloud step thinned behind her and vanished into the bright air.

Selin knelt on solid ground and opened the satchel just enough to check the capsule. Its silver lid was closed, and the cord was tight. She smiled, then held the satchel safely against her chest.

The journey had taken patience, careful watching, and more than one change of plan. But the message capsule was home.

Selin carried the satchel home and set it on the little table beside the window. Beyond the glass, the cloud bridge was gone. Only open sky remained between the islands, bright and blue.

She untied the looped cord and lifted out the silver capsule. It was cool in her hands and still undamaged. Selin turned the lid slowly. It clicked once, then loosened.

Inside lay a folded sheet of cloud-thin paper.

Selin opened it carefully. The first line read:

“Thank you, careful traveler. You carried this message farther than the wind could carry it.”

She smiled. The words felt warmer than sunlight.

Below the thanks was a drawing. It showed a floating island Selin did not recognize, with a small windmill standing near its edge. Unlike the windmills she knew, this one had a star-shaped mark on its center wheel. A dotted line curved away from the island, ending at a pale bridge drawn among the clouds.

Selin leaned closer. In the corner, someone had written three words: “Wait for alignment.”

She looked from the sketch to the real windmills outside. Their sails were turning in different directions now, and the next bridge would not appear simply because she wished for it. It would need the right hour and the right wind pattern, just as the first bridge had.

Selin took out her little travel notebook. She drew the star-shaped mark, copying each point carefully. Beside it, she wrote: “One cloud bridge beyond the visible islands. Time and wind unknown.”

Then she closed the capsule and placed it safely beside the notebook.

The message journey was finished. The capsule had crossed the windy ledge, the fading bridge, and the long return safely. Selin had learned that courage could wear a satchel, watch a ribbon, wait through a gust, and try a wiser path.

Outside, one far-off windmill flashed in the afternoon light. For a moment, Selin thought its center looked almost like a star.

She did not run to the window or make a new plan. She simply marked the place in her notebook.

One day, when the hour and the wind were right, she would look again.
