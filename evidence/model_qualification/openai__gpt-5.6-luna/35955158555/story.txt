The Shared Map

The market square was bright with baskets of pears, painted pots, and ribbons fluttering above the fountain. Garden Day was only a few days away, and Ege spread a large sheet of paper across a wooden bench.

“We need a map that children can follow from the square to every garden!” he said. “First, past the fountain, then over the little bridge, then through the sunflower gate—and perhaps a loop around the lily pond!”

His pencil zipped across the paper. Arrows curled between the market stalls and the colorful gardens. Ege added stars, flags, stepping-stones, and three different shortcuts.

Lina leaned close. “The blue flowers could be clues,” she said. “Children might look for three blue blossoms near the herb garden. And this path beside the roses is quiet. They could take it if they want to wander slowly.”

“Wonderful!” Ege said, though he was already drawing. He swept a thick arrow over the place Lina had pointed to. “This can be the fastest way to the pumpkin gate.”

Lina waited, then tapped another corner. “What about the tiny scent garden? It is easy to miss. We could draw a little nose symbol.”

“A nose! Yes—and a bridge symbol here!” Ege replied. His bridge stretched across her flower clue. “Then children will know exactly where to go.”

Lina gently moved the paper toward herself. “Ege, the flower clue is under your bridge.”

He paused. Around the bench, arrows crossed like tangled ribbons. One route raced past the gardens; another curled through flowers and quiet corners. There were so many ideas that no one could tell which path children should follow.

Ege and Lina looked at the crowded sheet.

“Perhaps,” Lina said, “we should make our ideas separately tonight and compare them tomorrow.”

Ege nodded, still studying the busy map. “Tomorrow. We’ll see which parts belong.”

The next morning, Ege and Lina returned to the bench beside the fountain. Their crowded shared sheet lay between them, but each carried a fresh map.

Ege’s paper was full of strong, clear lines. A red route began at the market square, passed the fountain, crossed the little bridge, and hurried through the sunflower gate. He marked the pumpkin gate with a golden star and added arrows to the lily pond and the herb garden.

“Children can see where to go at a glance,” Ege said. “This route links the best landmarks without any backtracking.”

Lina unfolded her map. It looked almost like a garden quilt. Blue dots showed the flowers near the herb garden. A tiny nose marked the scent garden. Green curls led along the quiet path beside the roses, then turned toward small places where children could pause and notice.

“My map lets explorers choose,” Lina explained. “They can follow the lavender path, find three kinds of yellow flowers, or wander slowly beneath the rose arches. The garden has so many things to discover.”

Ege pointed to the long curling path. “But this takes children all the way around. What if they miss the fountain show?”

Lina tapped his straight red line. “And what if they hurry past the flowers, the scents, and the tiny paths? They might reach the gate without really exploring.”

They placed the maps side by side. Ege’s route was easy to follow but skipped many garden details. Lina’s map held wonderful discoveries but had no clear quickest way from one landmark to the next.

“Children should follow mine,” Ege said.

“Children should choose mine,” Lina replied.

Neither map seemed ready to lead everyone. Ege traced his arrows while Lina studied her flower dots. At last, Lina looked up.

“Let’s walk one garden route before we decide,” she said. “We can notice what explorers might do with both maps.”

Ege folded the two drafts carefully. “All right. Tomorrow, we test them on the paths.”

The next morning, Ege and Lina carried their separate maps through the sunflower gate and into the gardens.

“First, we’ll test the quickest route,” Ege said. He followed his red line from the gate to the lily pond, past the little bridge, and toward the fountain beyond the herb garden. His feet knew exactly where to go.

Lina walked beside him, holding her map open. “You passed the blue flowers,” she said. “They are right beside the path.”

Ege stopped and looked back. The flowers were small, but their petals shone like bits of sky. “I marked the herb garden,” he said. “Isn’t that enough?”

“Not for someone who wants to notice the flowers,” Lina replied. She pointed to a green curling line on her map. “And the scent garden is just around this corner. Your red route goes straight past it.”

Ege glanced at his arrows. They led quickly from one golden star to the next, but they left many of Lina’s blue dots and green paths unmentioned.

At the rose arches, they saw three children with a garden helper. One child pointed toward the fountain. “Can we get there quickly?” she asked. Another child leaned toward a patch of purple blossoms. “Can we look at these first?”

The helper smiled. “You can do both. Stay where you can find one another.”

The children tried to choose together. One hurried toward the fountain while the others bent over the flowers. Soon they called back and forth, unsure whether to continue or wait. No one was upset, but their group had become stretched along the path.

Lina watched them, then spoke softly. “They don’t need one route that makes everyone hurry, or one route that makes everyone wander. They need choices that meet again. A short path could lead to the fountain, while a discovery path visits the flowers. Then both paths could join at a place everyone can see.”

Ege listened without tracing his map. He looked from the children to Lina’s green curls, then to his red arrows.

“You’re right,” he said. “I was trying to make one fastest route for everyone. Let’s connect a short route and a discovery route with meeting places.”

He drew a small circle beside the rose arches. Lina added a blue flower beside it. Together they began searching for the next clear place where explorers could meet.

Ege and Lina spread their two maps on a flat stone beside the rose arches.

“Let’s make one map,” said Ege. “I’ll draw the main line and the landmarks.”

“And I’ll add the garden clues and discovery stops,” said Lina. “Then we can check every choice together.”

Ege redrew the route from the sunflower gate. He marked the little bridge, the lily pond, the herb garden, and the fountain with golden stars. Between the stars, he drew a clear red line for explorers who wanted to keep moving.

Lina placed blue flowers beside the tiny flower patch and near the scent garden. She added a green curling path through the roses and a small nose symbol for the fragrant herbs.

At the rose arches, they compared the lines. “The discovery path should begin here,” Lina said, tapping the green curl. “But it must return to the short route.”

Ege drew a second line. It curved past the blue flowers and joined the red route at a bright yellow circle. “This can be a meeting place,” he said. “Everyone can pause here and choose what to do next.”

They added another circle near the fountain and a blue flower beside it. The circles stood on both routes, like little promises that the paths would come together again.

Then they tested the drawing. Ege held the map while Lina walked the short route. At the bridge, she stopped. “I can see the star, but not the line that leads to the first meeting place.”

Ege studied the page. “The bridge star is too large. It covers the turn.” He made it smaller and added a red arrow.

Next, Lina followed the discovery path. “This blue flower is near two flower patches. Which one does it mean?”

“Let’s add three blue petals beside the correct one,” Ege suggested.

They walked the route again. This time, the arrows, stars, curls, flowers, and circles were easy to follow. Ege copied the tested design onto a clean sheet, while Lina checked each symbol against the gardens around them.

At the bottom, they wrote: SHORT WAY or DISCOVERY WAY—MEET AT THE CIRCLES.

Ege rolled the finished map carefully. “It’s ready for Garden Day,” he said.

Lina smiled. “Ready to be tested by real explorers.”

On Garden Day, the market square shimmered with ribbons, baskets, and bright paper flowers. Children gathered around Ege and Lina as Ege unrolled their map beside the fountain.

“Look,” said Lina, pointing to the words at the bottom. “You may choose the SHORT WAY or the DISCOVERY WAY. Everyone meets at the circles.”

The children studied the golden stars. One group followed the red line from the fountain to the little bridge. Another group chose the green curling path toward the flower gardens.

At the bridge, a girl named Pera pointed to the smaller bridge star and the red arrow. “This way leads to the yellow circle!” she called.

The quick explorers hurried along the short route. They passed the sunflower gate, counted the lily pond’s stepping stones, and reached the first meeting place. There, they waited beneath the rose arches.

The discovery group moved more slowly. A boy spotted three blue petals on the map and searched beside the path. “Here they are!” he cried. “The blue flowers!” His group leaned close to smell them and then followed the green line toward the tiny scent garden.

When the discovery group reached the yellow circle, the waiting children made room for them.

“What did you find?” asked Pera.

“Flowers that smell like honey,” said the boy.

“And the pond has a white lily opening,” added another child from the short route.

The children marked both discoveries with their fingers on the map. Then they chose the next section together. Some wanted to hurry to the herb garden. Others wanted to follow the blue clues near the roses. The map showed them how to do both and where to meet again.

At the second bright circle near the fountain, the groups came together once more. Children compared the landmarks they had passed with the garden details they had noticed. No one had been left behind, and no one had needed to choose between racing ahead and stopping to look closely.

Ege watched a child trace the red route while Lina helped another child find the nose symbol for the scent garden. The map’s stars, arrows, flowers, green path, and yellow circles were all busy guiding small fingers.

Ege nudged Lina happily. “They’re using every part of it.”

“And changing choices as they go,” Lina said.

By afternoon, the children returned to the market square with stories from both routes. Ege and Lina looked at the map, now covered with cheerful pointing fingers and new discoveries, while the groups gathered around them to share one last tale.

The children gathered around the map in the market square, each eager to share one more discovery.

“Let’s write them down before we forget,” said Lina.

Ege spread the map flat on a wooden bench. Together, they added a tiny honey-colored flower beside the three-blue-petal clue. Lina carefully wrote, “Smells like honey.” Near the lily pond, Ege drew a white lily with its petals open.

Pera leaned over the map. “The lily opened while we were on the short way!”

“And the honey flowers were on the discovery path,” said the boy who had found them. “Other explorers should know where to look.”

Ege picked up his pencil. “I can add a star here, then an arrow—” He paused and looked at Lina. “What symbol would help children notice the honey flowers quickly?”

Lina studied the map. “A little golden flower would show that it is something to smell, not just something to pass by. We could put three small lines above it, like a scent drifting upward.”

Ege did not draw at once. He listened as Lina explained where the symbol should go and why it should be beside the blue petals, not covering them. Then he nodded and sketched the golden flower with three curling lines.

“Like this?” he asked.

“Exactly,” Lina said. “Now the clue and the discovery belong together.”

They checked the whole map once more. The red route led from the fountain past the bridge and gates. The green path curled toward flowers and scents. Yellow circles showed where everyone could meet again. Ege traced each line while Lina checked each symbol, and they fixed one smudged arrow together.

When the market square grew quiet, they carried the map to the market hall. The keeper helped them hang it beneath the wide window, where afternoon light shone across the stars, petals, arrows, and bright meeting circles.

“Future explorers can start here,” Lina said.

Ege smiled. “And next time, what shall we map?”

Lina pointed toward the far end of town, where a row of painted doors peeked between the shops. Ege reached for a fresh sheet of paper, then waited for her to explain her idea as they walked home together.
