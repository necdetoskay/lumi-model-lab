The Quiet Signal

Deniz was seven, and she liked the quiet corner of the town library. She sat by the window with her small notebook while the librarian stamped books nearby.

Tap, tap, tap.

Deniz looked up. A pause followed. Then came the same sound again.

Tap, tap, tap.

She held her pencil still. The taps were soft, but they were clear. They seemed to come through the window.

Deniz wrote:

3:10 — three taps, pause, three taps.

She pressed her nose close to the glass and looked outside. The street was calm. A bicycle leaned against a wall. Leaves moved in the sea breeze. No one stood near the window.

“Maybe it is a branch,” Deniz whispered. “Or maybe someone is tapping.”

The sound did not come again right away. Deniz waited, but guessing felt too easy. One sound could fool her. She needed to listen in another place.

She closed her notebook carefully and put it in her bag. The public garden was just down the street. From there, she could compare what she heard with what she had written.

Deniz glanced once more at the quiet window. She did not know if the taps were a warning, a message, or only an ordinary sound. So she decided to listen first and guess later.

Deniz walked to the public garden with her notebook tucked under her arm. The garden path curved between flower beds and a row of young trees. She stopped near the gate and listened.

Tap, tap, tap.

A pause.

Tap, tap, tap.

Deniz opened her notebook. The garden was quiet except for bees and the far-off hush of the sea. She checked the little clock on the path sign.

“3:25,” she said, and wrote it beside the library time.

Library: 3:10 — three taps, pause, three taps.
Garden: 3:25 — three taps, pause, three taps.

The rhythm matched. That felt important, but it did not tell her what made the sound.

Deniz walked ten slow steps along the path and listened again. The taps seemed faint behind her. She turned toward the hill, where the old observation point stood above the town.

She moved a little closer to the hill. The next taps sounded clearer there. Deniz tried one more place, beside a stone bench. The sound was clearer still, though it bounced between the trees.

“Maybe it is coming from the hill,” she said. “But an echo can play tricks.”

Near the bench, a fallen branch lay across the grass. Under it, Deniz noticed three fresh, narrow wood chips. They were pale against the dark soil.

She did not pick them up. She drew their shapes in her notebook and added, “Found near the path.”

The hill seemed like a good place to check next. Still, Deniz needed more than a good guess.

Deniz carried her notebook to the town workshop. Mina, the workshop keeper, was sorting safe pieces of scrap wood at a low table.

“I found chips in the garden,” Deniz said. “And I hear three taps, a pause, and three taps.”

She showed Mina the drawing and the notes from the library and garden.

Mina placed two small pieces on the table. One was solid wood. The other was an old cedar scrap with a hollow middle. She held each piece firmly while Deniz tapped it three times with her fingertip.

Tap, tap, tap.

The solid piece made a short, dull sound. The hollow cedar made a sharper sound that seemed to last longer. A little echo trembled in the workshop.

Deniz looked at her notebook. “The sound from the garden lasted, too.”

“Wood can carry sound,” Mina said. “But this test cannot tell us what made the taps.”

Beside the cedar scrap, Deniz noticed tiny fresh marks. They were narrow and close together. She placed her garden drawing next to them. The marks looked much like the three chips she had seen under the fallen branch.

She wrote: Cedar has fresh narrow marks. They look like garden chips.

“Maybe something is tapping wood,” Deniz said. “Or maybe someone is making a signal.”

“Both are still possible,” Mina replied. “The best next step is to watch the place where the clues meet.”

Deniz thought of the hill and the old observation point. She closed her notebook carefully. The workshop had not solved the mystery, but it had made one thing clearer: the sound might begin in hollow wood. Now she needed to see what made it.

Mina walked with Deniz up the hill to the old observation point. She stayed on the path while Deniz carried her notebook and watched the wooden lookout.

The post beside it was made of cedar. Near its bottom lay three fresh, narrow chips. A few small feathers rested in the grass beside them. Deniz did not touch anything. She only looked, then drew the clues in her notebook.

“Could the chips come from this post?” she whispered.

“Maybe,” Mina said. “Let’s listen.”

They stood quietly. A soft sound came from the post.

Tap, tap, tap.

Deniz held her breath. After a pause, the sound came again.

Tap, tap, tap.

She checked her notes. The rhythm was just like the one at the library and in the garden. At the garden, the hill had seemed to make the taps clearer. Now Deniz understood why. The hollow cedar post could carry the sound down the hill and across the town.

She pointed to the chips. “The wood is marked, too. And there are feathers.”

“Those are clues,” Mina said. “But they do not tell us exactly who made the marks.”

Deniz sat on a flat stone a short distance away. She watched the post without touching it. The taps came once more, but she saw no bird and no person. Leaves moved in the sea breeze. The post stood still.

Deniz wrote: Hollow post. Same rhythm. Chips and feathers. Source still unseen.

The clues fit together, but one question remained. What was making the taps?

The next afternoon, Deniz returned with Mina at the time written in her notebook. She sat on the same flat stone. Mina waited quietly on the path.

Deniz kept her hands in her lap. She did not touch the post. She did not tap back.

For a while, nothing happened. A gull called far below. Grass bent in the sea breeze. Deniz watched the hollow cedar and waited.

Then a small bird flew to the post.

It had a pointed beak and a neat black-and-white body. The bird held on to the cedar. Tap, tap, tap. It paused, tilted its head, and tapped again.

Deniz stared. The bird pecked at a small spot in the wood, then stopped to look for food. Tiny bits of cedar fell to the grass.

She looked at the clues in her notebook. The bird's beak fit the narrow marks. The new chips matched the old chips. The feathers beside the post could have come from a bird. The hollow cedar had carried the tapping down the hill, past the garden, and toward the library. Most important, the bird made the same three taps and the same pause.

“So it is not a warning,” Deniz whispered. “And it is not a secret message.”

“No,” Mina said softly. “You watched carefully and found the answer.”

The woodpecker tapped once more. Deniz wanted to answer it, but she remembered that the post might be a nesting place. A tap from her could disturb the bird.

She pointed without getting closer. “Mina, we should tell people to leave this post quiet. It may hold a nest.”

Mina nodded. “That is a careful choice.”

Deniz wrote the final clue: Woodpecker feeding on hollow post. Three taps, pause, three taps. The mystery was no longer a worry. It was a sound with a source.

The next morning, Mina walked with Deniz to the library. Deniz showed the librarian her notebook.

She showed the first time: three taps, a pause, and three taps by the window. Then she showed the garden notes, the wood chips, and the hill. She explained how the hollow cedar post carried the sound down toward town.

Last, she drew the woodpecker tapping the post.

The librarian looked at the careful drawings. “Now I understand why the sound seemed to come from different places,” she said. “It traveled.”

Mina and Deniz went to the workshop next. Deniz showed Mina’s cedar comparison and the marks that matched the chips. The workshop keeper agreed to tell visitors to stay away from the post.

Together, the adults made a small sign. It said, PLEASE KEEP QUIET. BIRD NESTING AREA.

They placed the sign beside the path, far enough away that it did not touch or block the old post. No one climbed on the lookout. No one tapped the wood. The bird would have its quiet place.

Deniz stood with Mina below the hill. Soon, the sound came softly through the sea air.

Tap, tap, tap. Pause. Tap, tap, tap.

Deniz smiled. She could name every part now: the woodpecker’s beak, the fresh chips, the feathers, the hollow cedar, and the echo that carried the sound toward the garden and library.

“It sounded like a message,” she said, “but it was a bird looking for food.”

Mina nodded. “Your notes helped everyone hear the whole story.”

Deniz listened again. The taps were no longer a worry. They were the woodpecker’s ordinary work, safely continuing at the old observation point.
