The Story Behind the Marker

Deniz had crossed the River Arch hundreds of times, usually while hurrying to school or racing his cousin to the bakery. Today he stopped beneath its gray stone curve.

A metal marker was fixed to one side. Deniz leaned close and read:

FIRST MARKET GATE.

He looked through the arch at the river shining beyond it. Boats no longer passed there, but the river still pressed against the bank, and the old stones still held their wide, rounded shape.

“First Market Gate?” Deniz murmured. “First for what?”

At the local archive, Ms. Rana found him staring at a photograph of the arch.

“You noticed the marker,” she said.

“I noticed what it says,” Deniz replied. “But I don’t know if that is the whole story.”

Ms. Rana smiled and led him into the old map room. The air smelled of paper and dust. Drawers covered one wall, and a large map lay beneath a sheet of clear plastic.

“There it is,” she said, pointing near the river bend.

The map showed the same curved line of river, the same road beside it, and a small stone shape at the crossing. But beside that shape, in faded brown ink, were the words:

EAST FERRY GATE.

Deniz checked the river bend, then the road. They matched the arch he knew. He looked back at the marker’s words in his mind.

“Why does one place have two names?” he asked.

“Good question,” said Ms. Rana. “A map can tell us what someone wrote on it. It may not tell us everything about why they wrote it.”

Deniz took out his notebook. He drew two columns. Under “Sources,” he wrote “present marker” and “old map.” Under “Questions,” he wrote:

Why does one landmark have different stories?

He tapped his pencil against the page. The answer might be in the archive’s oral-history recordings—or in the stones themselves.

“I want to hear what people remember,” he said. “Then I’ll go back and read the inscription carefully.”

Ms. Rana placed a recording slip beside the map. Deniz copied the map’s words into his notebook, leaving room underneath.

East Ferry Gate: what did this name mean?

Before leaving, Deniz checked the label once more and copied the map reference so he could find it again. He planned to return to the archive for the recording before deciding what either name meant.

The next afternoon, Deniz returned to the archive with his notebook tucked under his arm. Ms. Rana had left the old map beneath its clear cover, exactly where they had seen it.

“Before we listen to the recording,” Deniz said, “I want to look harder.”

He bent over the map. In one corner, small writing gave its date: 1887. A thin blue line marked the river. A brown road curved beside it, and a tiny stone shape stood where the River Arch still stood today. The label was clear enough now:

EAST FERRY GATE.

Deniz copied the date and drew a box around the label. Then he made two headings in his notebook.

KNOWN FROM THE MAP

- It is dated 1887.
- It shows the river bend and the road.
- It marks a stone arch.
- It calls the arch East Ferry Gate.

He paused before the second heading.

GUESSES

- People crossed the river there.
- Boats stopped nearby.
- The arch was built for a ferry.

“Are those guesses too wild?” Deniz asked.

“They are possible,” Ms. Rana said. “But which part can you actually see?”

“The river, road, arch, and words.” Deniz pointed to each one. “I can’t see boats. And the map doesn’t draw a ferry.”

“Good. A label is evidence that someone used those words. It is not proof of every story we might build around them.”

Deniz erased the line about boats. He changed the next one to: “The name may connect to a ferry, but the map does not explain how.”

The map felt less like an answer now and more like a careful clue. It placed the arch in 1887 and recorded one name, but it did not tell him whether the name described the arch’s purpose, a nearby crossing, or something people had simply called it.

Ms. Rana picked up the recording slip. “The oral-history file mentions the river road. You can listen for details that help you decide whether it describes this same place.”

Deniz wrote one more note beneath the map facts:

LISTEN FOR: river bend, road, stone arch, nearby streets.

He looked at East Ferry Gate again. The words still raised questions, but now he knew which questions belonged to the map—and which would have to wait for another source.

“I’m ready,” he said, sliding the notebook beside the recorder.

Deniz pressed the play button. At first, the recording held only a soft hiss. Then an older woman’s voice filled the map room.

“My name is Leyla Demir. I lived on Willow Street when I was little, in the house with the blue shutters.”

Deniz glanced at Ms. Rana. Willow Street ran beside the river road, just as the map showed.

Leyla continued. “When the river climbed over its banks, everyone hurried to the stone arch. The ground beneath it was higher than the street. Families crowded there with blankets, baskets, and sleepy children. My brother carried our cat inside his coat.”

Deniz could almost see it: rain flashing on the river, wet shoes packed beneath the arch, a child peeking out at the rising water. The recording made the old stones seem warm with people, even though Leyla spoke from many years ago.

“We called it the Safe Arch,” Leyla said. “Our parents told us to stay close until the water went down. I remember the bakery bell ringing across Willow Street, and the river rushing below the road.”

The recording ended with a click.

For a moment, Deniz said nothing. He looked again at the map’s thin blue river and brown road. The bakery was not drawn, but Willow Street matched the nearby street Leyla had named. The river bend matched too. Most importantly, the stone arch stood in the same place.

“It’s the River Arch,” Deniz said. “She’s talking about the same one.”

“I think so,” Ms. Rana replied. “What makes you think that?”

“Willow Street, the river road, the bend, and the stone arch all fit.” Deniz tapped the map. “But she calls it the Safe Arch, not East Ferry Gate.”

He opened his notebook and turned to a fresh page. Under a heading called REMEMBERED EXPERIENCE, he wrote:

- Leyla remembers families sheltering beneath the arch during a flood.
- She remembers the name Safe Arch.
- She remembers Willow Street, the river road, and the river rising.

Then he added: “No exact date.”

“Why is that important?” Ms. Rana asked.

“Because she says ‘when I was little,’” Deniz answered. “That tells us it happened in her childhood, but not which year. And remembering something clearly doesn’t make the date exact.”

He drew a line between the two pages. On the map page, he had written: “In 1887, someone labeled the arch East Ferry Gate.” On the new page, he wrote: “At an unknown time, Leyla’s family used or heard the name Safe Arch.”

The two accounts did not match neatly. Still, Deniz did not cross either one out. The map recorded a name. Leyla remembered what the place meant to people during a flood. Both could be true, but he could not yet say how they connected.

Ms. Rana handed him the recording slip. “The inscription at the arch may give you a third description.”

Deniz tucked the slip into his notebook. “Then I need to see what the stones say today.”

The next afternoon, Deniz and Ms. Rana walked to the River Arch. It stood where the map said it would: beside the river bend, at the end of the road, with Willow Street opening behind it.

Up close, the stones were larger than Deniz had imagined. Some were pale and smooth. Others had dark marks from rain and years of passing hands. A metal plaque was fixed to one side.

Deniz read it aloud. “The First Market Gate. Built to welcome traders to the river market.”

He blinked. “That’s another name.”

Ms. Rana did not answer. She pointed toward the river. “What can you observe?”

Deniz opened his notebook. He sketched the arch, the river-facing side, the road, and the corner where Willow Street began. “The arch faces the river. The road passes through it. Willow Street is nearby. It’s the same shape and place as the arch on the map.”

“Good,” said Ms. Rana. “What does the plaque tell you?”

“That someone calls it the First Market Gate now—or at least calls it that on this plaque.” Deniz copied the words carefully. “It says it welcomed traders. But it doesn’t say when the market began.”

He looked at the stones again. “And it doesn’t say anything about a ferry or a flood.”

“Does that make the other accounts wrong?”

Deniz thought about Leyla’s voice: families pressed together beneath the arch, blankets and baskets around them, the river rushing below the road. “No. The plaque describes a market. The map gives the name East Ferry Gate. Leyla remembers people calling it the Safe Arch.”

He drew three boxes in his notebook. At the top of the first he wrote KNOWN. In it, he listed the matching river bend, road, arch, and nearby Willow Street. In the second, he wrote REMEMBERED and copied Leyla’s flood memory. In the third, he wrote WRITTEN ON THE PLAQUE and recorded First Market Gate.

“What belongs under INFERRED?” Ms. Rana asked.

Deniz tapped his pencil against the page. “Maybe the arch had different uses at different times. A ferry could have crossed nearby. Later, people might have gathered there during floods. And a market might have used the road.”

“Could you prove that from these sources?”

“No.” He added a question mark beside the idea. “The dates are missing. The map is from 1887, but Leyla didn’t say when the flood happened, and the plaque doesn’t give a date either.”

Deniz drew one last box: UNCERTAIN. He wrote: “We do not know which name came first, or what the arch was originally built for.”

The three names still felt untidy, like stones that did not quite fit. But his sketch showed that the names belonged to one real place, not three different arches.

Ms. Rana studied the page. “You have not forced them into one answer.”

“I’m not ready to,” Deniz said. He closed his notebook. “I need to make the account show what each source can actually tell us.”

The next morning, Deniz returned to the old map room with his notebook, the recording slip, and a clean archive card.

He drew three lines across the card. At the top, he wrote: RIVER ARCH: THREE ACCOUNTS.

“Start with what you can support,” Ms. Rana said. “Not what sounds most exciting.”

Deniz nodded. He looked at the map copy first.

“Known,” he said, writing as he spoke. “An 1887 map shows this arch at the river bend and labels it East Ferry Gate.” He paused. “But the map does not say that boats went through it. So I can’t write that it was definitely built for a ferry.”

Ms. Rana smiled. “That is an important difference.”

Deniz moved to the recording notes. “Remembered: Leyla Demir recalled families sheltering beneath the arch during a flood. She remembered people calling it the Safe Arch.” He added, “The recording matches the river, road, Willow Street, and stone arch. But Leyla didn’t give the flood’s date.”

He tapped his pencil. “Her memory tells us what people experienced. It doesn’t give us every date from the past.”

“Exactly.”

Last came the plaque. Deniz copied its words carefully: First Market Gate. Built to welcome traders to the river market.

“Known from the present plaque,” he said. “But it doesn’t tell us when the market began, either.”

For a moment, he stared at the three names. East Ferry Gate. Safe Arch. First Market Gate. They still looked like three people arguing over one hat.

Deniz turned the card over and began again. This time he wrote a short account instead of three separate lists.

“The River Arch is one landmark beside the river bend near Willow Street. An 1887 map calls it East Ferry Gate. A later memory calls it the Safe Arch because families sheltered there during a flood. A present-day plaque calls it the First Market Gate and connects it with traders and a river market.”

He read the sentence twice. Then he added:

“These names may describe different uses or times. That is possible, but the sources do not prove exactly when the names changed, or whether the arch was first built for a ferry, a market, or something else.”

Deniz looked at Ms. Rana. “Is ‘later memory’ all right? We don’t actually know when the flood happened.”

“Good catch,” she said. “Use ‘an oral memory’ instead. It keeps the timing honest.”

Deniz crossed out the words and replaced them. Under the account, he made four small labels: KNOWN, REMEMBERED, INFERRED, UNCERTAIN. He placed each claim where it belonged, checking that none had quietly slipped into the wrong box.

At the bottom, he wrote: “Further records are needed to learn the arch’s earliest name, construction date, and original purpose.”

Ms. Rana read the card without changing a word. “This is careful work.”

Deniz let out a breath. He had wanted one answer that would make every name fit perfectly. Instead, he had made something better: an account that showed where each answer came from—and where the evidence stopped.

That afternoon, Ms. Rana placed Deniz’s archive card in the front room of the local archive.

Beside it, she set a copy of the 1887 map, Deniz’s notes from Leyla Demir’s recording, and a photograph of the present-day plaque. A small sign invited visitors to compare the sources.

Deniz stood nearby as neighbors came in from the street. Mr. Selim, who ran the bakery, leaned close to the map. “East Ferry Gate,” he read. “I’ve always called it the Market Arch.”

“That is what the plaque calls it,” Deniz said, pointing to the photograph. “But the map is older, and it uses a different name.”

A girl about Deniz’s age read the recording notes. “Safe Arch? Why?”

“Leyla remembered families sheltering beneath it during a flood,” Deniz explained. “That part is remembered experience. We know she told the story, but we don’t know the flood’s exact date.”

The girl looked from the notes to the card. “So is one name right?”

Deniz glanced at the three sources. He still felt a small tug inside him, the wish for one neat answer. Then he remembered the map’s blank space, the recording’s missing date, and the plaque’s confident words.

“They can all be connected to the same arch,” he said. “The map proves that someone used the name East Ferry Gate in 1887. Leyla’s recording tells us what people remembered happening there. The plaque gives the present description. We can infer that the arch may have had different uses or names at different times, but we can’t prove the whole order yet.”

Ms. Rana nodded, but she let Deniz finish.

He showed the girl the four labels on the card. “Known means the source says it or we can see it. Remembered means someone recalled it. Inferred means it is a possible explanation. Uncertain means we still need evidence.”

The girl studied the labels. “That makes the card easier to trust.”

Deniz smiled. His goal had not been to choose a winning name. It had been to explain why the names differed, and now the archive held an account that showed what each source could really tell them. The unanswered parts were not mistakes. They were marked honestly.

Before closing time, Deniz returned the copies to their folders. He paused beside the map.

“Ms. Rana,” he said, “could an old building permit help? If the arch was repaired or built for a market, a permit might give its first name—or at least a date.”

“It might,” she said. “That would be a sensible record to search.”

Deniz wrote one final question in his notebook: Could an old building permit confirm when the River Arch was first built or renamed?

Then he shut the notebook and looked once more at the card on display. The River Arch still had three descriptions, but now the neighborhood could see how they belonged together—and where the evidence stopped.
