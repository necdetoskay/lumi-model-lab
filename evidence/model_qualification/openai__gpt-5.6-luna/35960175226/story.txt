The Shared Map

In the market square, Ege spread a large sheet across a wooden bench. Garden Day was only a few mornings away, and he wanted children to explore the town’s colorful gardens with a map made by him and Lina.

“Look!” Ege said, drawing a sweeping line from the sunflower garden to the pond garden, around the herb beds, and up to the rose walk. “One grand route! It can join every garden. I’ll add a giant sunflower here, a blue fountain there, and maybe a vine bridge—”

“There isn’t a vine bridge,” Lina said gently. She held her smaller draft beside his. It showed a yellow flower for sunny places, a green leaf for quiet places, and a circle for a meeting place. “Children could choose. Some might want the busy music garden. Others might prefer the shady herb garden.”

“Wonderful!” Ege said, already drawing looping arrows. “We can make one exciting route first. Everyone can follow it, and then—”

“Perhaps we could mark—” Lina began.

“—and the route could pass the fountain twice!” Ege continued. “A map should feel like an adventure.”

Lina studied the curling lines. “It should also be easy to understand.”

Ege paused only long enough to add a red flag beside the rose walk. New ideas popped into his mind like bright marbles. He loved making maps, but he kept hearing his next idea instead of Lina’s whole one.

At last, Lina folded her draft. “Our maps are telling different stories.”

Ege looked from his crowded route to her clear symbols. “Then we need to see which story children can use.”

They picked up both unfinished drafts and headed toward the gardens. Their designs still disagreed, but they had a plan: walk the paths, look closely, and find out what explorers might need.

They entered the sunflower garden, where tall yellow heads nodded above the path. Ege unfolded his draft and followed the sweeping line with his finger.

“Sunflowers to pond, pond to herbs, herbs to roses,” he said. “The route joins everything!”

But at the pond garden, his red arrow pointed in three directions. One path led to a quiet bench beneath willow branches. Another curved toward the fountain, where water splashed brightly. The third went to the music garden, where bells chimed whenever the breeze moved through.

“Which way does your map tell explorers to choose?” Lina asked.

Ege looked at the crowded page. His line connected the paths, but the tiny arrows and added landmarks tangled together. The map showed that children could travel everywhere, yet it did not show which place might be calm, noisy, or interesting to pause in.

Lina placed her draft beside his. Her yellow flower clearly marked the sunny sunflower garden, and her green leaf marked the shady herb beds. A small blue circle showed the fountain. The symbols made the gardens easy to recognize. But when Lina traced her line from the herb beds to the rose walk, there was no line to follow. Her map named the places without showing how to get between them.

Near the herb beds, they tested both drafts against the real paths. Ege’s route crossed the busy fountain way and skipped the narrow path behind the mint. Lina’s symbols marked the mint as quiet, but her page offered no way to reach it from the pond.

Ege tapped the two drafts. “Your map helps explorers choose a place, and mine helps them travel there.”

Lina nodded. “Maybe children need both. They might want the lively music garden, a slower walk by the mint, or a clear place to meet again.”

They sat beside the rose walk, listening to bees hum among the petals. Neither draft could do all of that yet. Their next map would need connected paths, understandable choices, and meeting places—but they still had to decide how to join the ideas.

Ege spread both drafts on the warm stones beside the rose walk. His finger hovered over the sweeping red line.

“I could make the route thicker,” he began. “Then it would be easier to follow, and I could add—”

He stopped. Lina was looking at the pond garden, where the fountain sparkled on one side and the willow bench rested in deep shade on the other.

“Could I explain my idea first?” she asked.

Ege nodded and folded his hands in his lap.

Lina pointed to her symbols. “The blue circle can show a busy place, like the fountain or music garden. A soft green leaf can show a calm place, like the mint path or willow bench. And a little star can mark a meeting spot. Children can choose what suits them and still know where to find their friends.”

Ege listened all the way to the end. He looked at his single red route. It joined every garden, but it ran straight through the choices, as if every explorer had to enjoy the same walk. If he made the line thicker, Lina’s symbols would be buried beneath it.

“So my grand route is asking everyone to travel one way,” he said slowly. “It connects the gardens, but it hides the choices.”

Lina smiled. “What if it connects more than one way?”

Ege picked up his pencil. “Then I’m changing it. The main line can lead from the sunflower garden to the pond. Here, it can split: one line to the fountain and music garden, another to the willow bench and mint path. They can join again at a meeting spot by the rose walk.”

He erased part of his sweeping route—not all of it—and redrew several bright lines that curved around the gardens. Lina leaned close and placed a blue circle by the fountain, a green leaf beside the willow bench, and a star near the roses.

“Your lines show how to travel,” she said. “My symbols show what each choice feels like.”

Ege added a second star beside the herb beds. Their once-separate drafts now shared one page: connected routes, clear choices, and places to meet. It was still only a draft, but both friends could see their ideas beginning to travel together.

The next morning, Ege and Lina returned with a fresh sheet of paper, colored pencils, and a long ruler. They sat beneath the rose arbor and divided the work.

“I’ll draw the connecting lines in sections,” said Ege. “You can check each garden and place the symbols.”

“And we’ll check them together,” Lina added.

Ege drew a red line from the sunflower garden to the pond. At the pond, it split. One line curved toward the splashing fountain and chiming music garden. Another slipped past the willow bench and behind the mint. Lina compared every part with the gardens around them. She placed a blue circle at the fountain, a green leaf beside the mint path, and stars at the rose walk and herb beds.

They tested the map by covering the names with their hands. Ege traced a route with his finger.

“Start at the sunflower,” he said. “Choose the fountain or the willow bench. If friends choose different paths, meet at the star by the roses.”

Then he held the paper at arm’s length. Lina squinted.

“Wait,” she said. “The blue circle and the meeting star both look like tiny spots from here.”

Ege followed the lines again. He nearly pointed to the star as if it were the fountain. “You’re right. A child could follow the route correctly and still choose the wrong place.”

Neither friend defended the mark. Lina drew three little splash lines inside the blue circle. Ege outlined each meeting star with a square, then checked that the squares did not cover the rose or herb-bed landmarks.

They tested the map once more. The splashes meant lively water or music. The leaves meant calm paths and shady places. The squared stars meant, “Friends can meet here.”

Ege traced the connected options while Lina called out the landmarks. Together they folded the finished map, unfolded it, and checked every line and sign again. This time, the choices stood clear, and the routes still joined the gardens.

They placed the map in a bright folder marked GARDEN DAY. It was ready for children to try—but its real test would begin when the town filled with explorers.

At sunrise, Garden Day filled the market square with flower garlands, baskets, and cheerful voices. Ege and Lina opened the bright folder beside the fountain and showed the map to the first group of explorers.

“Start at the sunflower garden,” Ege explained, tracing the red connecting line. “Then you can choose where to go.”

A girl named Mira studied the symbols. “What does this leaf mean?” she asked, pointing toward the mint path.

“It means a calm place,” Lina said. “You can follow the green line behind the mint if you want shade and quiet.”

Mira nodded and led two friends along that route. Another child tapped the splash-marked circle. “We want the lively choice!”

“That line goes to the fountain and music garden,” said Ege. He watched the children follow the route, their fingers moving from the pond to the bright splash marks. The map did not force everyone into one adventure. It let them choose while keeping every path connected.

Soon, the two groups reached different gardens. Mira and her friends paused beneath the willow shade, while the lively group listened to bells near the music garden. Then a boy held up the map.

“Where do we meet again?”

Lina pointed to a squared star by the rose walk. “Follow the line from your garden. The star means friends can meet there.”

The children arrived at the roses from two directions, comparing mint leaves, fountain splashes, and music they had heard. No one had been left out, and no one had needed to choose the same path.

More children came with more questions. One girl pointed to the green leaf. “Could we use that sign for the herb beds too? They look quiet.”

Ege began to answer quickly, then stopped. Earlier, he had thought the leaf symbol was too small and had wanted to leave it out. Now he looked at Lina and listened as she explained that the leaf helped children recognize a calm choice before they reached it.

“You’re right,” Ege said. He turned the map so the girl could see. “The leaf marks calm places. The mint path is one, and the herb beds are another. If you want, you can follow this connected line and meet us at the star.”

Lina added a tiny note beside the map’s key: “Leaf = calm.” Together, they answered the next questions, checking each route and symbol as children explored. By afternoon, the garden paths were full of different journeys that kept joining at the marked stars.

By late afternoon, the garden paths grew quiet. Children gathered near the rose walk, still holding the Garden Day maps with their fingers tucked along the folded lines.

“I liked the mint path best,” Mira said. “It was quiet, and I still met everyone at the star.”

“I liked the fountain!” said another child. “The splash mark showed us where to go.”

A boy pointed from the pond garden to the music garden on his map. “We took different lines, but we heard the same bells and met by the roses.”

Ege looked at the map in his hands. Its connected lines curved between the gardens, while Lina’s leaves, splash marks, and squared stars made each choice easy to find. The lines and symbols seemed to hold hands across the page.

“Your symbols helped children choose before they arrived,” Ege told Lina. “I would have drawn one grand line and missed that.”

Lina smiled. “And your lines helped every choice lead somewhere. My symbols would have been lonely without them.”

They placed the map back in its bright folder, now filled with tiny pencil notes from the day: a leaf beside the herb beds, a splash mark near the fountain, and arrows showing where children had traveled. Ege carefully saved the folder in the town hall’s map shelf, where they could use it again.

Then he carried two fresh sheets of paper to the fountain’s low stone wall. Lina sat beside him.

“What shall we map next?” Ege asked. He uncapped his pencil, then held it above the blank page. “You choose the first mark.”

Lina drew a small square in one corner. “A place to begin.”

Ege listened as she described a new adventure through the market town. He drew a line only after she finished, connecting the square to a wide, empty space. Lina added another symbol beside it.

Soon, the blank corner filled with joined lines and careful marks, while the evening light turned the fountain gold. Around them, children compared their garden journeys, each route different, each story shared.
