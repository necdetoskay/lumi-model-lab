The Space Between Words

By Thursday afternoon, Deniz could find almost everything in the community center without asking: the box of colored clips behind the folding chairs, the charging cables in the drawer marked AV, and Noor at the long table beneath the windows.

They had been preparing the listening-wall exhibit for Saturday’s neighborhood gathering for nearly three weeks. Visitors would pin up cards about places, sounds, or memories that mattered to them. A few cards would link to short audio clips. Deniz was helping with the interviews, trimming transcripts, and arranging the display. Noor was coordinating the project, which meant Deniz could make real decisions without being responsible for the whole community center.

Before they started sorting the newest recordings, Noor held up a yellow card. “One rule, even if we’re rushing?”

“Final consent check before anything goes public,” Deniz said.

“Every card and every clip.”

Deniz tapped the stack. “No accidental neighborhood fame.”

Noor smiled. “Exactly.”

They had written the rule at the top of their shared planning document. It was sensible, especially because people sometimes mentioned family members or private details without realizing how widely a recording could travel. Still, Deniz felt a little impatient. Saturday was close, and their interview with Mr. Alvarez from the bike-repair shop had turned out better than expected. He had talked about the sound of freewheels clicking down the block, and Deniz had shaped the conversation into a lively two-minute draft.

That evening, Deniz uploaded the transcript and rough audio to the shared folder. They labeled both files DRAFT—CHECK and sent Noor a message: Look what I cleaned up! Tell me if the timing works. Deniz knew it was early. They planned to review the consent notes together the next day. But the clip sounded good, and waiting felt harder than it should have.

The next afternoon, the project group gathered in the small meeting room with the coordinator. Deniz arrived carrying a bundle of blank cards and saw the display board already taking shape. Their interview card was missing.

Maybe Noor had moved it to another pile. Deniz checked the table, then the floor beside the printer. Nothing.

Across the room, Noor was speaking quietly with the coordinator. Deniz only caught the last part.

“Not yet,” Noor said.

The coordinator nodded, and Noor glanced toward the shared laptop. Deniz’s stomach tightened. They set the blank cards down and joined the others, pretending to listen while people argued about whether the title should be painted blue or green.

After the meeting, Deniz messaged Noor: Did something happen to the bike-shop card? I saw it was gone.

The message stayed unread through the bus ride home. It was still unanswered when Deniz checked again after dinner.

The missing card was a fact. So was Noor’s “Not yet,” and the silence afterward. But Deniz’s mind kept arranging those facts into a sharper sentence: Noor was taking them off the project.

The thought hurt. Worse, it embarrassed Deniz. They had been proud of the interview, proud that Noor trusted them with it. Now they wondered whether everyone else had understood something Deniz had missed. They didn’t know if that was true. They only knew the exhibit was unfinished, the card was gone, and Noor was not explaining why.

Deniz opened the message thread again while standing in the kitchen, one hand braced against the counter.

They typed: So you’re just removing me from the project now?

The sentence looked strong for about three seconds. Then it looked like something that could not be taken back.

Deniz added, You could at least tell me what I did wrong.

That one felt worse. It assumed Noor had decided Deniz was the problem, when all Deniz actually knew was that a card had disappeared and a message had gone unanswered.

They set the phone down and paced to the window. The feeling in their chest had been shifting all evening. First it had been anger, hot and quick. Underneath that was hurt. Underneath the hurt was embarrassment: the fear that Noor had lost confidence in them, possibly for a reason everyone else already understood.

Deniz picked up the phone again and deleted both sentences. They did not delete the feeling. Naming it made it less blurry, even if it did not make it disappear.

Hurt. Embarrassed. Worried.

Not proof.

They opened a blank note, intending to write a calmer message, but instead made two headings.

WHAT I KNOW

The bike-shop card was on the display table before the meeting.

It was gone afterward.

Noor said, “Not yet,” to the coordinator.

My message is still unanswered.

I uploaded the rough transcript and audio before we did the final consent check.

Deniz paused over the last line. They had included it because it was true, though it did not fit neatly with the story forming in their head.

WHAT I’M GUESSING

Noor is taking me off the project.

Noor thinks I cannot be trusted.

Noor told the coordinator something about me but will not say it to my face.

The guesses felt possible. That was the problem. They were not ridiculous, but possibility was not the same as evidence.

Deniz leaned back against the counter and looked at the two lists. The first one was plain and annoying. The second one had sharp edges. If they sent the angry message, they would be arguing with the second list as if it were fact.

They opened the shared project folder instead.

The folder was not secret. Noor and Deniz had both been using it for weeks, and its files were arranged by interview: raw audio, transcript, consent notes, display card. Deniz clicked the bike-shop folder, then stopped before opening anything. They were not going to search Noor’s private messages or poke through unrelated documents. The project records were enough. If there was context, it should be there.

A file titled BikeShop_consent_notes sat beneath the rough transcript. Another file, Transcript_edit—N, had been modified that afternoon.

Deniz’s pulse quickened. Noor had done something to the transcript. That could mean almost anything.

They rested the cursor over the file without clicking. The angry draft was still saved in the phone’s deleted messages, recoverable if Deniz changed their mind. For a moment, they wanted to send it anyway, just to force an answer.

Instead, they opened the consent notes first.

Whatever Noor had meant by “Not yet,” Deniz wanted to know what had actually changed before asking why. Then, if the records explained the missing card, Noor would still have to explain the silence.

Deniz clicked the file.

The consent notes opened in a narrow window beside the transcript. Deniz read the first lines quickly, then went back.

Interviewee: Mara K.
Consent status: approved for recording and draft review.
Display status: confirm before publication.

Below that, Noor had added a comment in gray: Check the school reference again. It may identify her son too specifically.

Deniz scrolled.

In the recording, Mara had talked about repairing bicycles after work and mentioned that her son had started at a small school on the east side. The school’s name was not written in the display card, but the neighborhood, the bicycle shop, and the school together could make the family easy to identify.

A second note, dated that afternoon, said: Asked Mara whether this detail is okay for the public clip. Waiting for confirmation. Do not display yet.

Deniz stared at the last sentence.

The card had not vanished because Noor wanted Deniz gone. There was an actual privacy problem. A small one, maybe, but not imaginary. If the exhibit brought strangers into the community center, someone might recognize the details and connect them to Mara’s son without meaning to.

They opened Transcript_edit—N.

The rough transcript was still there, but several lines were crossed out in red. Beside the school reference, Noor had typed: Remove unless Mara confirms. Everything else remained in Deniz’s wording, including the introduction and the parts about Mara’s bike-repair work.

Noor had edited the transcript. They had not erased Deniz’s work.

Deniz let out a breath and sank into the kitchen chair. The evidence pushed against the sharpest version of the story in their head. Noor had not secretly told the coordinator that Deniz was careless. At least, the files did not show that. They showed Noor trying to stop one detail from reaching the wall before it was safe.

They also showed that Deniz had uploaded the rough audio and transcript too early.

The agreement had been clear: no card, no clip, no public display until the final consent check. Deniz had remembered that rule when they made it. Then Saturday had started feeling close, the draft had sounded good, and they had wanted Noor to see that the interview was ready. They had treated “rough” like it meant harmless.

It had not been harmless if the rough file was already being used to build the display.

Deniz rubbed their forehead. Admitting that did not make the unanswered message disappear.

Noor could have written: There’s a privacy issue with one detail. I’m checking it.

Instead, Noor had removed the card during the meeting and said, “Not yet,” as if Deniz were standing outside a door they were not allowed to enter. Maybe Noor had been busy. Maybe the coordinator had needed an answer immediately. Maybe Noor had been angry about the early upload. All of those were possible. None of them explained the silence.

Deniz clicked back through the files, checking that they had not missed a message or comment. There was nothing else in the bike-shop folder. No explanation. No note addressed to them. Only the edited transcript and the consent reminder.

The hurt returned, quieter now but heavier. Deniz could own the mistake and still dislike how Noor handled it. Both things fit.

They opened the message thread. Their thumb hovered over the keyboard. The old accusation was gone, but the question remained.

What happened with the card?

It was too vague. It made Noor responsible for guessing which part Deniz meant.

Deniz typed, then paused.

I saw the edit in the shared folder. I understand why the school detail needs another check. But I still want to know why you didn’t tell me the card was being held back.

They did not send it yet. Not because they planned to avoid Noor, but because a message might turn the whole problem into another set of careful sentences. They needed to speak where Noor could answer, and where Deniz could say the part that was harder to type: I felt pushed out.

Deniz closed the folder, leaving the consent note and edited transcript in their mind. The records had explained Noor’s caution. They had not repaired the silence.

The next afternoon, Deniz waited until the community center’s last meeting ended. Chairs scraped across the floor, the hallway emptied, and the listening-wall panels stood against the far wall like unfinished doors.

Deniz found Noor coiling an audio cable beside the display table.

“Can we talk for a minute?” Deniz asked. “I felt pushed out when the card disappeared; can we check what happened?”

Noor’s hands stopped moving. “Okay.”

They sat on two folded chairs near the window. Outside, someone was locking the bike rack, and the metal clink carried through the glass.

Deniz kept their hands between their knees. “The facts I know are that I uploaded the rough recording before we did the final consent check. Then the card was gone, you said ‘Not yet’ in the meeting, and you didn’t answer my message.”

Noor nodded once.

“What I guessed was that you were taking me off the project,” Deniz continued. “I know that isn’t the same thing. But it still felt like that.”

Noor looked down at the cable. “I wasn’t trying to take you off. The school detail worried me. When Mara mentioned her son’s small school, plus the shop and the neighborhood, I thought people might figure out exactly who he was. I asked her if it was okay for the public clip, but she hadn’t answered yet.”

“I understand that part now,” Deniz said. “The note made sense.”

“I should have told you.” Noor pulled one loose strand of hair behind their ear. “I know.”

The words sounded less like a speech than something Noor had been carrying around and did not want to hold anymore.

Deniz waited.

“When I saw the upload,” Noor said, “I got worried because we had agreed to check everything together. Then the coordinator asked which cards were ready. I moved yours aside because I didn’t want the school detail copied onto the wall. After that, I kept thinking I needed to explain properly, not send a rushed message. Then I didn’t know how to start.”

“So you avoided it,” Deniz said.

“Yes.” Noor exhaled. “That was not a great plan.”

“No.” Deniz almost smiled, but the feeling did not quite reach their face. “I shouldn’t have uploaded it either. I wanted you to see the interview before Saturday, and I treated ‘rough’ like it meant nobody would use it yet.”

“I wasn’t angry that you did all the work,” Noor said. “The transcript was good. I was scared the file would get used before Mara confirmed that detail. And then I handled being scared by making you guess.”

The last sentence landed more heavily than an apology might have. Deniz looked toward the empty display table. Their card was still missing, but the reason no longer filled every space around it.

“I can accept changing the clip,” Deniz said. “I don’t want that school reference on the wall if Mara hasn’t agreed. But when you stayed silent, it made my mistake feel like rejection.”

Noor met their eyes. “I get why.”

For a moment, neither of them spoke. The project was still not ready. The audio still needed a decision, and Deniz’s credit was not on the table.

“We need to fix it together,” Noor said finally. “Not just decide what happens to the one sentence.”

“Yeah,” Deniz said. “We can compare the note and the transcript, figure out what to remove or recheck, and make sure the card shows both our work.”

Noor nodded. “Tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow.”

They stood. Nothing between them had snapped back into place, but the silence had changed shape. It was no longer a locked door. It was a space where the next conversation could happen.

The next afternoon, Deniz and Noor met at the community center with the laptop, the printed consent note, and two cups of vending-machine tea that tasted mostly like warm sugar.

They spread everything across the display table. The bike-shop transcript was open on the screen. Noor tapped the marked sentence.

“School on the east side,” they said. “That’s the part we can’t use unless Mara confirms it’s okay.”

Deniz read the lines before and after it. Without the sentence, the interview still had the sound of the shop’s bell, Mara’s story about fixing a bent wheel, and the part about neighbors helping each other find affordable repairs. The school detail added context, but it was not the center of the interview.

“We can cut this,” Deniz said. “The rest still works.”

Noor looked at them. “Are you sure? It’s your recording.”

“It’s our exhibit. And I agree with the concern.” Deniz highlighted the sentence. “But I want to keep the introduction and the repair section. Those are mine.”

“Absolutely.”

They made the cut together, listening twice to check that it did not sound abrupt. The new version moved from Mara describing the shop to her explanation of why she kept some repairs inexpensive. It sounded clean. More importantly, it did not leave a gap that made anyone curious about what had been removed.

Noor opened a message to Mara. “Should I ask, or do you want to?”

“Together,” Deniz said.

They wrote: We revised the clip and removed the reference to the school. Are you comfortable with us using this version and the bike-shop interview card at Saturday’s gathering? We can change or remove anything else you’d rather keep private.

Deniz read the message once. It did not pretend the earlier version had been fine. It also did not make Mara responsible for explaining more than she wanted to.

Noor sent it.

While they waited, they rebuilt the card. Noor brought back the blue border they had designed together, and Deniz typed the interview title beneath it. Under the title, Noor added: Interview by Deniz Aydin and Noor Hassan.

Deniz noticed the credit line and paused. “You put my name first.”

“You did the interview.”

“We both edited it.”

“Then both names stay.” Noor slid the laptop toward them. “Check the spelling.”

Deniz checked it. Their name was there, clear and impossible to miss. The small relief that followed was almost embarrassing in its strength. They had not realized how much the blank space on the table had been connected to that missing line.

Mara’s reply arrived a few minutes later. She was comfortable with the revised clip and card, as long as the school reference stayed out. Noor showed Deniz the message instead of answering alone.

“Then it’s cleared,” Deniz said.

“Cleared for this version,” Noor replied. “We should save the confirmation with the project notes.”

They did. Deniz renamed the audio file BikeShop_final_no-school-reference and moved it into the folder marked Ready for Saturday. Noor replaced the old draft link on the exhibit checklist so nobody would accidentally play the first version.

“That’s the part I missed before,” Deniz said. “I thought labeling something rough was enough. Next time, I want to review the upload with you before it goes anywhere, even if it’s only in the shared folder.”

Noor nodded. “And if I move something or hold it back, I’ll tell you directly. Not after three days of making it weird.”

“Good.”

They found a scrap of orange paper and wrote two lines:

1. Check consent together before posting or displaying.
2. Tell each other directly when a file, card, or plan changes.

Noor reached for the tape. “Too formal?”

“Not if we keep it where we can see it.”

They taped the note inside the project folder, beside the consent records. It was not a rule from the coordinator or a promise that they would never get things wrong. It was simply a way to stop uncertainty from filling in the blanks for them.

By closing time, the revised audio played from the small speaker, the card stood on the display table, and both their names were printed beneath the title. Deniz still felt a faint pinch when they remembered the missing message and the empty space where the card had been. But now that memory had a second part: Noor showing the message, restoring the credit, and staying beside them while they fixed the work.

They packed the laptop together.

“Saturday?” Noor asked.

“Saturday,” Deniz said. “And if something changes?”

“I tell you.”

“Then I ask before guessing.”

Noor smiled. “That works.”

By Saturday afternoon, the community center had filled with the ordinary noise of a neighborhood gathering: chairs scraping, people greeting one another, and the soft burst of laughter from the refreshments table. Sunlight came through the high windows and landed across the listening wall.

Deniz stood beside the bike-shop card while Noor tested the small speaker. The revised clip played clearly. Mara’s voice described fixing a bent wheel, and then the recording moved into her explanation of keeping some repairs affordable. No school, no identifying detail, nothing that needed to be hidden behind a vague answer.

A visitor about Deniz’s age stopped in front of the display.

“Is this the whole interview?” she asked. “It sounds like Mara was talking for longer.”

Deniz glanced at the card. Under the title, it said: AUDIO INTERVIEW.

“It’s the—” Deniz began.

Noor lifted two fingers, then pointed at the card. It was a small movement, but Deniz felt the old jolt anyway. For one quick second, the gesture seemed to mean, You’re about to get it wrong.

The feeling arrived before the explanation: embarrassment, followed by the tight wish to defend themself.

Deniz stopped. They looked at the card, then at Noor.

“What changed?” Deniz asked quietly.

Noor leaned closer. “The label. We saved a selected clip, not the whole recording. I should have noticed that before we opened.”

The visitor waited, interested but not impatient.

Deniz let out a breath. “So it’s a shorter part of the interview,” they said. “We chose it because it focuses on repairs and neighbors helping each other. The full recording isn’t on the wall.”

“Could you make that clearer?” the visitor asked.

“Definitely.”

This time Deniz did not hear Noor’s signal as a judgment. It was information. Still, the old pinch stayed for another moment, like a bruise pressed by accident.

Deniz opened the project folder on the tablet and checked the exhibit checklist. The final file was marked Selected clip—cleared for Saturday. Noor stood beside them instead of reaching over and changing the card alone.

“Should we change the title to ‘Selected audio clip’?” Noor asked.

“Yes. And maybe add that it’s from an interview with Mara at the bike shop.”

“Good.”

Deniz edited the card while Noor read the wording over their shoulder. They printed the new strip at the information table, trimmed the edges, and placed it beneath the title. The visitor watched, then nodded when the clearer version went up.

“Now it makes sense,” she said. “Can I listen?”

Deniz checked the volume sign and pressed play. The clip began again.

A few minutes later, another visitor asked about the blue border on the card. Deniz explained that they and Noor had designed it together. Noor added that the card had been revised after a consent check, and Deniz noticed the word revised no longer sounded like a record of failure. It sounded accurate.

During a quiet stretch, Noor nudged the edge of the card. “When I pointed at it, did it feel like I was correcting you in front of everyone?”

Deniz considered the question. The honest answer was not as simple as yes or no.

“For a second,” they said. “Then I asked instead of deciding what you meant.”

Noor nodded. “I’ll say it out loud next time. Even if it’s a tiny thing.”

“And I’ll check before I fill in the blank.”

The speaker continued playing Mara’s approved words. People leaned closer to listen, then moved along the wall to the next card. The earlier mistake had not vanished; the first rough version was still part of how they had learned to handle the work. But the exhibit stood open, with the right clip, the right credit, and a clearer label.

Deniz watched Noor greet the next visitor. The remaining tension eased—not because uncertainty had disappeared, but because it no longer had to become a private story before either of them could ask a question.
