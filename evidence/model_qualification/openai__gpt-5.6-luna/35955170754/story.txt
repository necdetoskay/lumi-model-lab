Beyond the Cloud Bridge

Selin found the message capsule at the very edge of her island, where the clouds curled below like a white sea. Across the open sky floated the neighboring island. A tiny silver glint rested near one of its windmills.

“Not far,” Selin whispered. “But far enough to be dangerous.”

She did not step onto the cloud path just because she wanted the capsule. First, she studied the four windmills turning around the island. Their broad sails pointed east, then shifted toward the north as the air changed. The oldest windmill had a painted mark on its tower: a sun over a cloud.

Grandmother had explained the rule. The cloud bridge formed only once each day, during the short hour when the sun touched that mark. Then the cloud path faded, whether a traveler had reached the other side or not. The windmills showed which way the air was moving, but they could not promise that it would stay still.

Selin opened her travel pack. She left behind her heavy book, extra clothes, and a jar of marbles. She packed a strong rope, a square of sailcloth, a bottle of water, and one small honey cake. The rope could help her stay connected to solid ground. The sailcloth might block a hard gust. Water and the cake would keep her steady if the journey took time.

She checked each knot twice, tested the rope against a fence post, and watched the windmill sails again. East. North. East.

At last, pale clouds began gathering between the islands. Selin stood beside the first forming bridge, her pack light and her hands ready. She felt afraid, but she did not turn away. She would wait, watch, and cross only when the bridge and the wind agreed.

The cloud bridge thickened beneath the painted sun-and-cloud mark, its pale steps joining Selin’s island to the neighboring one. Selin waited until three steps held their shape. Then she placed one boot on the first.

The bridge felt springy, like a firm pillow under her foot. She kept one hand on the rope and looked back at the fence post where its other end was tied. The neighboring island’s windmills turned slowly. East, then north. So far, the air matched her careful plan.

Selin took another step.

A sail on the nearest windmill snapped around. The other sails followed, flashing from north to east. A crosswind rushed between the islands. The cloud steps shivered sideways.

Selin bent her knees. Her pack tugged at her shoulders, and the sailcloth in it fluttered like a bird trying to escape. Ahead, the silver message capsule winked beside the far windmill.

“I see you,” Selin called. “But I’m not going to tumble after you.”

Another gust swept across the bridge. The cloud path swung sharply, and a thin edge crumbled away into mist.

Selin stopped. She pressed one palm against the cloud, testing it. It rolled beneath her hand instead of staying firm. Her stomach felt jumpy, but her thoughts grew clear. The wind had changed when the sails turned. The bridge was no longer safe for crossing.

She pulled the rope close, wrapped it twice around a sturdy cloud knot, and began backing toward her island. She moved slowly, keeping three points steady: two feet and one hand. When the bridge swayed, she waited. When it settled, she took another step.

At last, Selin reached the nearest windmill. Its stone tower had a sheltered side, hidden from the crosswind. She slipped behind it and held the rope against her chest. From there, she watched the bridge grow thinner. The cloud steps faded one by one until only a drifting ribbon remained between the islands.

Selin was disappointed, but she had not failed. She had learned exactly what the turning sails could do. Tomorrow, she would watch them more carefully before choosing her path.

Selin stayed behind the windmill until the last ribbon of cloud vanished. She did not hurry away. Instead, she watched the sails.

At first, they pointed east. Then one sail trembled, and the others swung toward north. Selin scratched a tiny line in the dust beside the tower. A little later, the sails turned back. The wind had changed again.

She watched through the afternoon and into the quiet gold before evening. The pattern slowly became clear. Just before the painted sun-and-cloud mark’s bridge hour, the sails stopped arguing with one another. They settled in nearly the same direction for a short while. Not long enough for carelessness, Selin thought, but perhaps long enough for a shorter crossing.

She studied the ground between the windmill and the island’s edge. A broad stone foot at the base of the tower would make a strong anchor point. Selin tested it with both hands. It did not wobble. She tied her rope around it, pulled hard, and checked the knot twice.

Then she opened her pack. The rope stayed. So did the folded sailcloth, useful as a shield against the crosswind. She kept her water and the small honey cake, but left every unneeded thing tucked safely beside the tower. There was no room for a heavy book, extra clothes, or marbles on a cloud bridge. Balance mattered more than comfort.

Selin folded the sailcloth into a wide, stiff panel and practiced holding it close to her side. If the wind pushed, she could turn the cloth edge-on instead of letting it billow. She marked a second line in the dust: the shortest path from the anchor point to the bridge’s first firm step.

The plan was simple. Tie in, wait for the sails to settle, cross quickly but steadily, and stop if the bridge began to sway. The next bridge would form only during its one brief daily hour. Even with calmer wind, that rule had not changed.

Selin looked toward the neighboring island, hidden now in lavender mist. The capsule was still out there. She felt the old jump in her stomach, but this time her hands were busy checking the knot.

When the painted mark began to catch the first light, Selin stood beside the anchor point, ready to try again—not certain, but prepared.

The painted sun-and-cloud mark caught the first light. At once, the nearest windmill’s sails slowed and settled in one direction.

Selin checked the knot at the stone anchor. Then she held the folded sailcloth edge-on against her side. It did not billow. The wind was calm enough.

A pale cloud curled out from the island’s edge. More clouds joined it, widening into the first firm step of the bridge. Selin waited until three steps had formed. She placed one boot on the nearest step and felt it dip softly, like a mattress made of mist.

She did not leap. She moved one careful step at a time.

The rope ran from her belt back to the stone anchor. When a little puff of wind pressed against her shoulder, Selin turned the sailcloth edge-on. The puff slid past. She kept her eyes on the windmill sails and her feet on the whitest, thickest parts of the bridge.

Behind her, the cloud steps began to thin.

Selin did not look back for long. The bridge had formed during its brief hour, and that hour was already passing. She shortened her steps, kept her knees bent, and reached toward the neighboring island’s grassy edge.

Her fingers caught the turf.

Selin pulled herself onto solid ground just as the last cloud step faded into loose wisps below. The rope stayed stretched behind her to the anchor on the first island, a pale line across the empty air.

She took one breath, then looked around.

The neighboring island sloped toward a tall windmill. Beneath its outer wooden braces, something blue flashed in the morning light. Selin crouched and saw the message capsule wedged between two beams, hanging above a steep drop. A gust could push it loose—or pull her toward the edge if she reached carelessly.

She tested the ground with her boot. The grass near the braces crumbled away in a narrow strip.

“No grabbing,” Selin whispered. “Not until I know where my feet and rope will hold.”

She stayed well back from the drop and studied the windmill base, the braces, and the turning sails. The capsule was close enough to see, but not safe enough to touch. The next step would need just as much care as the crossing.

Selin began with the rope. She unclipped its end from her belt and drew it in carefully, keeping well away from the crumbling strip of grass. At the windmill’s stone base, she found a thick wooden post sunk between two stones. She wrapped the rope around it twice, pulled hard, and tested the knot with both hands.

The post did not shift.

“Good,” Selin said, though her stomach still fluttered. Brave did not mean pretending the drop was small.

She watched the sails. They turned slowly from east toward north. A thin gust brushed the grass and made the blue capsule knock softly against the wooden braces. Selin waited. The sails kept turning. Then, just as she had seen on her own island, they settled for a little while.

The grass stopped rippling.

Selin held the folded sailcloth edge-on. It stayed nearly still. She tucked one corner under her arm and looped the rope around her waist, leaving enough length to lower herself but not enough to swing far. Then she checked the knot once more.

The capsule hung below the lowest brace, just beyond the solid ground. Selin lowered one foot, then the other, keeping her body close to the windmill’s stonework. The rope tightened around her waist and held. She did not look down at the empty air. She looked at the brace, the capsule, and the sails.

A sudden puff swept around the tower.

Selin turned the sailcloth edge-on. The gust struck the cloth’s narrow edge and slid away instead of pushing her outward. The capsule swung, tapping the brace.

“Not yet,” Selin whispered.

She waited until the sails showed the lull had returned. Then she lowered herself another arm’s length. With one hand gripping the rope and one foot pressed against the stone, she reached the capsule. Her fingers closed around its metal loop.

It was stuck.

Selin did not yank. She held the capsule steady with the sailcloth, shielding it from the next little gust, while she felt along the loop with her free hand. A thin strand of dried vine had curled around it. She worked the vine loose, one twist at a time.

The windmill sails began to turn again.

Selin pulled once, firmly and carefully. The vine snapped free. The blue capsule came into her hand.

She held it against her chest and climbed back up the rope, stopping whenever the wind stirred. At last her boots reached solid grass. Selin untied the rope, secured the capsule inside her pack, and looked toward the fading bridge.

The crossing back would have to begin soon.

Selin looked from the blue capsule to the cloud bridge. Its far end was already thinning into wisps.

“Time to go,” she said.

She coiled the rope over one shoulder, checked that the capsule was snug inside her pack, and folded the sailcloth edge-on beneath her arm. At the windmill, the sails turned from east toward north. Selin waited beside the stone base until they settled. Only then did she step onto the bridge.

The cloud surface dipped softly under her boots. She kept the rope looped around her waist, not pulling against it, just as she had before. The sailcloth stayed narrow against the wind. One step. Then another.

Halfway across, the sails began to turn again.

A crosswind brushed over the bridge. Selin stopped at once and lowered herself into a crouch. She held the sailcloth edge-on and waited. The bridge trembled, but it did not crumble. When the windmill sails slowed, she stood and continued.

Behind her, the neighboring island grew smaller. Ahead, her own island's windmill came closer. The last stretch of cloud had begun to fade, so Selin moved steadily, never rushing. Her boots touched the familiar stone edge just as the bridge loosened into shining threads behind her.

She pulled in the rope, gathered the sailcloth, and hurried to the painted sun-and-cloud mark. The capsule felt cool in her pack. Selin sat beside the windmill and opened it carefully.

Inside was a tightly rolled strip of silver paper. She smoothed it with her thumb.

“Cloud bridges observed at the marked hour,” she read. “One bridge also appeared beyond the western cloud line, after moonrise, at an hour not found in any chart.”

Selin read the sentence twice. Then she looked west. Far away, beyond the familiar clouds, a thin pale line gleamed and vanished.

She did not run after it. She did not even leave her pack on the ground. She tucked the message safely away and began coiling her rope.

Now she knew what courage felt like: not a leap without thought, but a brave step made with the wind watched, the hour counted, and the right tools ready.

The distant bridge was still a question. Selin smiled at it. Someday, she would learn which hour it obeyed.
