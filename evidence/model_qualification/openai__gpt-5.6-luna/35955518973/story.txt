The Signal in the Mist

Ada liked the coast best when the evening mist folded softly around the cliffs. It slipped over the grass, curled past the old waystones, and floated above the tidal caves below. Everything looked close and far at once.

She stood near the cliff path with her little sketchbook open. The sea was calm, though a thin wet line shone across the rocks where the tide had reached. Ada drew it carefully, then marked the nearest waystone: a tall, weather-smoothed stone with a faded crescent cut into one side.

She was about to draw the caves when a pale light blinked across the coast.

Ada looked up. The glow appeared near the misty shoreline—a soft, silvery flash, not bright enough to hurt her eyes. She counted quietly as it shone, faded, and shone again.

“One, two, three…”

For three minutes, the light blinked through the dusk. Ada watched its position, the shape of the mist, and the dark line of the cliffs. She did not decide what it was. A lantern? A reflection? Something else? Each idea needed evidence.

She hurried a few steps along the path, but the light vanished before she could get nearer. The coast became only gray mist, quiet water, and shadowed cave mouths.

Ada waited, but the glow did not return. So she added it to her sketch instead: three small marks beside the caves, with an arrow showing where she had seen it. Below, she drew the tide line. Beside that, she copied the waystone’s crescent.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would compare all three things—the signal, the stones, and the water. Then she closed her book and carefully followed the path home.

The next evening, Ada returned to the cliff path with her sketchbook tucked under her arm. Mist drifted over the grass, and the old waystone stood where she had drawn it, its faded crescent pointing nowhere that she could yet tell.

Ada studied her sketch. The signal had appeared near the coast, but perhaps that was only where she had noticed it. Far across the water, a dark headland rose like a long, flat shadow.

“A lighthouse could be there,” she said. “Its beam might be reaching this way.”

It was a sensible idea, she decided. So she watched the headland instead of the caves. The evening grew dim. The tide slipped lower, leaving the rocks shining below the cliff.

Then the pale signal blinked.

Ada looked quickly toward the headland. It stayed dark. No lamp appeared. No beam swept over the water. She checked her sketch, then looked back at the headland. Still dark.

The flash came again, soft and silvery.

This time, Ada noticed something below her. A wet wall inside one of the tidal caves brightened for an instant, as if a stripe of moonlight had touched it. When the signal faded, the wall became dull and gray again.

Ada leaned forward, careful to keep both feet on the dry path. The cave wall flashed a second time. The headland remained dark.

“So it isn’t coming from the headland,” she said. “The cave is catching the light—or the light may be starting there.”

She crossed out her lighthouse idea, but did not erase it completely. Instead, she drew a small note beside it: headland dark during the flash. Then she sketched the bright patch on the cave wall and marked its position below the cliff.

Ada had changed her mind because she had seen something, not because the first idea had been foolish. The cave was now the likeliest direction, though it was not yet the answer. She still needed to learn which cave received the flash and whether the tide would let her reach it safely.

Before leaving, she compared the shining cave wall with the wet tide line beside the waystone. Tomorrow, she would look more closely at both.

The next morning, Ada returned while the mist still rested on the cliffs. Her sketchbook held yesterday’s notes: the dark headland, the bright cave wall, the crescent-marked waystone, and the wet tide line.

She followed the cliff path and found two more old waystones half hidden among the grass. Ada placed her sketchbook on a flat rock and compared all three. Each stone had a faded crescent. Beside each crescent was a tiny dot, almost rubbed away by salt and weather.

She did not decide that the marks were a secret message. First, she drew them exactly as she saw them. Then she looked at where each crescent-and-dot pair faced.

The first waystone pointed toward a narrow shelf above the water. The second pointed along a bend in the cliff. The third pointed toward a cave whose lower stones were beginning to dry.

Ada walked closer, staying well above the water. The first cave entrance was dark and glossy with seawater. At the second, small pools still trembled in the cracks. But beneath the third waystone’s direction, a strip of pale stone showed dry for the first time. Drops slid from it and fell into the sea.

Ada glanced at the tide line she had drawn the evening before. The water had been nearly level with that line then. Now the sea had pulled away below it. The path toward the third cave was not always open; it appeared as the tide fell.

She added arrows to her sketch. Three marked stones, one drying entrance, and a lowering tide made a route—not a mysterious command, but a pattern supported by what she could see.

Ada checked the sun’s position. There was plenty of time before evening, but she would not hurry into the cave. She would return when the tide was falling and reach the entrance before sunset, leaving herself time to watch from the dry stones.

“First the safe path,” she said, closing her sketchbook. “Then I can learn what the light is doing.”

The cave still kept its answer inside, but Ada now knew which way to begin.

Ada returned that evening with her sketchbook tucked safely beneath her coat. The tide was still falling, just as her notes predicted. She followed the three crescent-and-dot waystones, stepping onto the pale stones that had dried beneath the third marker.

The cave entrance waited between two cliffs. Inside, the air smelled of salt and smooth rock. Ada held her lantern low, not to search far ahead, but to watch where she placed her feet. On the wall beside the entrance, she found another faded crescent. A tiny dot sat beside it, worn almost flat. She copied the mark before moving on.

The floor sloped gently inward. Thin streams of seawater slipped back toward the entrance, and little pools became smaller and smaller. Ahead, a narrow opening appeared between two shelves of rock. Water trickled through it, then paused, as if the sea were taking a slow breath.

Ada crouched near the dry edge and drew the opening. Beyond it, something pale caught the lantern light. It was a smooth, polished stone set into the far wall. Not a pebble, she noticed. Its surface was broad and carefully rounded, unlike the rough rocks around it.

She checked the time and looked toward the strip of sky above the entrance. Evening was near.

Then the brief signal began.

A pale beam slipped across the narrow opening. It touched the polished stone, which flashed softly and sent a bright patch skimming over the wet cave wall. Ada watched the beam’s direction and marked it with an arrow. The cave wall was not glowing by itself. Something outside the opening was sending light inward—but Ada could not yet see exactly what.

For a moment, she wanted to squeeze through and find out. Then a thin wash of seawater curled around the nearest stone. The little pools began to join. The opening was narrowing as the tide turned.

Ada closed her sketchbook. She took one last look at the polished stone, the opening, and the returning water.

“Not by rushing,” she whispered. “I have enough to test.”

She backed along the dry stones and followed the marked route out. Behind her, the cave grew dim again, keeping its answer just beyond reach.

The next evening, Ada returned before sunset, when the tide was falling just as her notes predicted. She followed the three crescent-and-dot waystones in order. The first marked the cliff path. The second pointed toward the drying stones. The third led her to the cave entrance, where the water had pulled away.

This time, Ada did not hurry. She checked the tide line against yesterday’s drawing and waited until the lowest stones were dry. Then she went inside, keeping to the route the marks had shown her.

Little streams slid seaward. The narrow inner opening stood clear, and the polished stone waited beyond it. Ada placed her sketchbook on a dry ledge and watched the strip of sky above the entrance. She remembered how this same direction had sent a pale patch across the cave wall before, though she had not yet understood what made it appear.

At sunset, a thin golden beam entered through the opening. It struck the polished stone exactly where she had seen the pale beam land before. The smooth stone caught the light and reflected it across the wet wall. From her earlier position on the cliff path, Ada knew that bright patch would be visible outside as the brief signal.

She counted softly. “One minute. Two minutes. Three.”

The light vanished.

Ada waited, but the wall stayed dim. A narrow line of sunlight still rested on the polished stone; as the sun lowered, that line moved away from it. The signal could not appear again until a later evening, when the sun reached the same angle. The falling tide had opened the passage for her observation, while the returning water would soon close it again.

Ada drew the beam’s path from the opening to the stone and from the stone to the cave wall. She added the tide line and the three waystones.

“It is not coming from the headland,” she said. “The waystones show where to go, and the tide notes show when to arrive. The falling tide opens the passage. Then the sunset light strikes the polished stone, and the stone sends the signal through the cave.”

She had seen the wall brighten before. She had seen the water return before. She had followed the marks on earlier visits and now followed them again. The clues fitted together: an old coastal signal device produced its brief flash when the tide exposed the opening and sunset reached the polished stone at the right angle.

Water curled around the nearest stone. Ada closed her sketchbook and stepped back along the dry path. The cave had not hidden a mysterious answer. It had shown her one slowly, in pieces, through repeated observations.

The next morning, Ada sat on a flat rock above the cave and opened her sketchbook to a clean page. She drew the whole route once more: the first waystone on the cliff path, the second beside the drying stones, and the third beside the cave entrance.

Beside the route, she added the tide window. “Go while the water is falling,” she wrote. “Leave before it returns.” Then she drew the narrow opening inside the cave, the polished stone, and the golden beam that had crossed from the sunset to the stone and then flashed across the wet wall.

At the bottom of the page, Ada wrote three minutes.

Another explorer could follow these notes without guessing. The waystones showed where to go. The tide showed when the path was open. The cave and polished stone explained the light.

Ada closed her pencil case and stood to leave. As she passed the third waystone, the morning sun slid across its rough face. A faint line appeared beneath the familiar crescent-and-dot mark—so faint that she had missed it before. It curved once, then ended in a tiny angle pointing away from the cave, toward the cliffs beyond the path she knew.

Ada leaned close. She did not brush the stone or declare what the mark meant. She copied the small line exactly in the corner of her sketchbook and added a question mark.

“Not part of the signal,” she said. “Just another thing to observe.”

The sea breathed quietly below. Behind her, the cave kept its polished stone and its three-minute secret, now understood and carefully recorded. Ahead, the faint mark waited beneath the weathered one.

Ada tucked the sketchbook safely into her pack. She would follow that other direction another day, with fresh paper, a patient eye, and enough time to look closely.

For now, she walked home along the familiar cliffs, leaving the old waystones exactly as she had found them.
