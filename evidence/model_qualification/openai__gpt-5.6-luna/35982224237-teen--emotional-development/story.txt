The Space Between Words

The media room at Eastwood Community Center smelled faintly of warm plastic and the cinnamon tea Hana kept forgetting beside the printer. Deniz knew every uneven floor tile, every lag in the old design computer, and the exact drawer where Leila stored the good scissors. On Thursdays, they worked there together on *Side Street*, the youth zine they had been making for almost a year.

“Your title is still three pixels too close to the edge,” Leila said, leaning over Deniz’s shoulder.

Deniz nudged it inward. “Happy?”

“Deeply.”

They grinned at each other. The printer clicked behind them while the afternoon light turned the windows pale gold. Their deadline was Friday, and most of the pages were already in the ready folder on the shared drive.

Deniz opened the poem file one last time. It was about the afternoon everything had gone wrong: the blue mug with the chipped handle, the bus shelter smelling of wet wool, and someone saying, “Don’t make this bigger than it is.” Deniz had changed the names and never used the person’s exact words, but the details still felt sharp and alive. Maybe too alive. That was part of why the poem mattered.

Leila had helped adjust the line breaks earlier. She had even said the ending landed hard.

So Deniz exported the final version and dragged it into the ready folder.

“There,” Deniz said. “Now it’s real.”

Leila looked at the screen. Her expression changed—not dramatically, just enough for Deniz to notice. She clicked the file, opened it, and read without speaking.

Then she moved it out of the ready folder.

Deniz stared at the empty space where the file had been. “What are you doing?”

Leila closed the laptop halfway. “This could hurt someone.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know. I mean—I need to check something.”

She picked up the laptop and crossed to Hana’s desk near the supply shelves. Hana was sorting permission forms. Leila bent close and spoke quietly. Hana glanced once toward Deniz, then back at Leila. Neither of them raised their voices.

Deniz’s face heated. The room suddenly seemed too bright, too open. Leila had removed the poem without asking, then taken it to Hana as if Deniz were a problem that needed handling.

A minute later, Leila returned the laptop but not the file.

“I’ll explain,” she said.

“When?” Deniz asked.

Leila hesitated. “Soon.”

Soon sounded like a door closing.

Deniz wanted to say that the poem was theirs, that Leila had approved the layout, that they were supposed to finish the zine together. Instead, Deniz looked at the nearly complete pages on the screen. The cover still needed a final check. Three captions needed trimming. The work was not finished, and despite the hot embarrassment and anger pressing behind Deniz’s ribs, abandoning it felt wrong.

“Fine,” Deniz said, too flatly. “I’ll finish my other pages.”

Leila nodded, but neither of them looked at the other.

As Deniz turned back to the computer, one thought settled heavily beneath the anger: maybe Leila wanted the poem out. Maybe Leila wanted Deniz out too.

The next afternoon, Deniz arrived at Eastwood Community Center early, before the media room filled with the scrape of chairs and printer noise. The poem was still not in the ready folder. Deniz had checked twice on the bus, as if the file might return on its own.

At the computer, Deniz opened a blank document and typed:

What happened:
Leila moved the poem out of the ready folder.
Leila spoke quietly with Hana.
Leila said she would explain soon, then did not.

Deniz paused before the next heading.

What I told myself:
Leila thinks the poem is embarrassing.
Leila wants me out of the project.
Everyone can tell I messed up.

The cursor blinked. Deniz added a third heading: Feelings.

Embarrassed. Angry. Worried.

For a moment, Deniz wanted to delete the whole thing. Naming the feelings made them seem both more real and less like instructions. Anger could push Deniz toward sending a message that would make Friday’s deadline—and everything else—worse.

So Deniz typed one more sentence: Rejection is an interpretation, not something I know yet.

That sentence looked annoyingly reasonable. It also refused to disappear.

Deniz opened the shared project log. The document tracked every page, edit, and person responsible. Under the layout section, Leila had written: “Deniz—final spacing and title placement.” She had left Deniz’s name there, even after removing the poem.

Deniz scrolled further. The poem’s file was listed under “held for review,” not “deleted” or “rejected.” Beside it, Leila had typed: “Do not circulate until concern is checked.”

That was evidence too. Leila had not sent the poem to the other contributors or dropped it into the public draft folder. She had kept it from spreading.

It did not erase the secret conversation. It did not explain why Leila had waited. But it made the story in Deniz’s head less solid.

Hana was at the supply shelves, untangling a box of charging cables. Deniz carried the laptop over, then stopped a careful distance away.

“Can I ask something?”

“Sure.” Hana set down the cable. “About the project?”

“About yesterday. Did Leila ask you whether the poem needed someone’s permission?”

Hana’s expression stayed kind but firm. “She asked about consent for stories with recognizable details. I can tell you that much.”

Deniz’s stomach tightened. “Did she say she wanted me off the zine?”

“No. I can’t speak for Leila, and I shouldn’t repeat a private conversation. You’ll need to ask her what she meant and what worried her.”

The answer was not the explanation Deniz wanted. Still, it mattered. Leila might have been trying to protect someone. Or she might have decided Deniz had crossed a line and used consent as a reason to shut the poem down. Both possibilities fit the facts so far.

Deniz looked back at the screen, where the project log still credited the work. “I’m not going to tell anyone what she said.”

“I appreciate that.”

“I still want to finish the zine.”

Hana nodded toward the unfinished captions. “Then keep working on what you can. And ask Leila directly about the part you can’t.”

Deniz returned to the computer. The embarrassment remained, and so did the anger. But beside them now were questions that had not yet hardened into accusations. Deniz trimmed a caption, saved it, and left the poem exactly where it was: outside the ready folder, still waiting for an answer.

The next morning, Deniz kept the poem open on one side of the screen and the media room’s consent checklist on the other. The checklist was taped beside the printer, its corners softened from being handled so often.

Before sharing a story, it asked:

Could someone recognize the person from the details?
Could the event be identified by a place, object, or exact words?
Has the person involved agreed to its use?

Deniz read the poem again. The chipped blue mug was not unique by itself. Neither was the bus shelter. But together, with the line about the “blue-hour promise” the person had once made, they formed a shape someone from the neighborhood might recognize.

Deniz highlighted those two parts: the mug beside the shelter, and the distinctive statement. The marks did not mean the poem was wrong. They meant the poem needed a closer question.

At the next computer, Deniz opened the message app. Leila’s name sat near the top of the project group, but Deniz did not tap it immediately. The first version of the message arrived too quickly:

Why did you take my poem away and talk about me behind my back?

Deniz deleted it. It described the hurt, but it also turned an assumption into a fact.

The second version was calmer but still carried a sharp edge:

You removed my poem because you think I crossed a line. Explain.

Delete.

Deniz looked at the checklist again, then typed a third message.

Yesterday you moved my poem out of the ready folder and said it could hurt someone. I noticed you spoke with Hana afterward, and I still don’t know what concern you saw. I marked two details that might make the person recognizable. Can you tell me what you were worried about? I want to understand before we decide what happens to the poem.

Deniz read it twice. It named what had happened. It asked for context. It did not claim to know Leila’s intention.

The send button waited at the bottom of the screen.

Sending it would not guarantee a good answer. It would not make the embarrassment disappear. But keeping the question locked inside Deniz’s head was not protecting anything either.

Deniz pressed send.

For the next fifteen minutes, the message showed no reply. Deniz worked on the caption edits, though the cursor kept wandering to the corner of the screen. Around the room, the printer clicked and released a page with a crooked margin. Someone in the recording booth tested a guitar chord, stopped, and tried it again.

Then Leila replied.

I wasn’t laughing at you, Deniz. I’m sorry it looked that way. I also wasn’t trying to exclude you from the zine. I wanted to talk face to face because I didn’t want to explain badly over messages. Can we meet after the room closes? I’ll bring the poem and the checklist.

Deniz let out a breath that had been caught somewhere behind the ribs. One story in Deniz’s head had weakened: Leila had said directly that ridicule and exclusion were not what she intended.

But the reply did not answer everything. It did not explain why Leila had removed the poem before speaking to Deniz, or what exactly she had shown Hana. It did not erase the feeling of being left outside the decision.

Deniz typed, I can meet after closing.

Then, after a pause, added: I still want us to finish the zine responsibly.

Leila’s answer came a minute later.

Me too.

Deniz copied the two highlighted details into a fresh note and saved it beside the checklist. The poem stayed outside the ready folder. Nothing had been secretly rewritten, deleted, or published. For now, there was only a question, evidence on both sides, and a meeting waiting at the end of the day.

After the media room closed, the familiar noise drained away: no printer clicks, no guitar from the booth, only the hum of the ceiling vents. Deniz and Leila sat across from each other at the long editing table. Between them lay the poem, the consent checklist, and the note with the two highlighted details.

Leila touched the poem but did not pick it up. “I didn’t show Hana the whole thing,” she said. “Just the lines you marked—the mug and the bus shelter, and the blue-hour promise.”

Deniz looked at the yellow marks. “Why not tell me first?”

“I should have.” Leila’s fingers tightened around the edge of the paper. “I was worried that if I asked you in front of everyone, you’d feel pressured to defend it. Then I thought I’d ask Hana privately and come back with a clear answer.” She gave a small, unhappy laugh. “Instead, I made it look secretive.”

“It did look secretive,” Deniz said. The words came out sharper than intended, but Deniz kept going. “You moved it out of the ready folder. Then you talked to Hana in the corner. I saw that and felt embarrassed. Like you’d decided I was a problem and didn’t want me in the project anymore.”

Leila nodded, though her face tightened. “I can understand why you thought that. I wasn’t trying to exclude you. But I can’t say your feeling came from nowhere.”

Deniz glanced at the checklist. “The details could identify them.”

“Maybe,” Leila said. “The mug and the shelter together are specific. And the exact phrase makes it worse. I was afraid someone would read it and know immediately. But I also made a decision about your poem without you. That wasn’t fair.”

The apology did not make the hurt vanish. Deniz could still remember the empty space where the poem had been, as if the folder itself had announced a verdict. At the same time, the marked lines now looked different. Not wrong. Just more connected to a real person than Deniz had wanted to admit.

“I don’t want to erase what the poem is about,” Deniz said. “The point is what it felt like when that promise changed. If we strip everything out, it becomes meaningless.”

“I don’t want to erase that either.” Leila slid the checklist closer. “Could we test a version instead of arguing about whether it’s safe in theory?”

Deniz studied her. “What would the test be?”

“We make a copy. We change the details that could identify them, but keep the feeling and the shape of the memory. Then we read it against each question here.” She tapped the checklist. “Not just ask whether we think it’s fine. Check what’s actually on the page.”

“And then ask the person?”

“Yes. Before it goes anywhere. We tell them what the poem is, what we changed, and ask if they’re comfortable with it being in the zine.”

Deniz let the silence settle. It was not the same as trusting Leila completely. It was a plan with steps, which felt steadier than guessing at what either of them meant.

“I want to do that together,” Deniz said. “No showing parts to someone else first. If there’s a concern, tell me before moving anything.”

Leila met Deniz’s eyes. “Agreed. And if I’m worried, I’ll say what I’m worried about instead of assuming I’m protecting you by keeping quiet.”

Deniz pulled a blank copy of the poem from the printer tray. The original stayed between them, its highlighted lines waiting. They had not revised anything yet, and they had not asked permission. But both opened their laptops, ready to begin with the evidence in front of them rather than another story about what the other person intended.

They worked from the blank copy while the media room lights clicked into their evening setting.

Deniz kept the original poem beside the laptop. “Before we change anything,” Deniz said, “I want to say this clearly. I felt pushed out when the poem disappeared. I saw it leave the ready folder, and I saw you talking to Hana. I assumed that meant you thought I was a problem, or that you wanted me out of the zine.”

Leila nodded. “What I intended was to keep someone from being recognized before we checked with them. But I didn’t explain that. I made a decision about your work without you, then left you to guess why. I understand how that looked.”

The hurt still pressed against Deniz’s ribs, but hearing the pieces separated made it easier to hold. “My feeling was real,” Deniz said. “My assumption wasn’t proof.”

“No,” Leila said. “And my good intention wasn’t proof that I handled it well.”

They opened the poem. The first risky image became a rainy street beside a closed bakery instead of the bus shelter. The chipped blue mug became a paper cup with a bent lid. They kept the poem’s center: the moment a promise changed, the silence afterward, and Deniz’s sense of standing beside someone who had already begun to leave.

The distinctive statement was harder. It had mattered because the person had actually said it. Deniz tried three versions, deleting each one.

“That line carries the turn,” Leila said. “But it’s almost word for word.”

Deniz stared at the blinking cursor. “What if I write, ‘You said the kind of thing that makes a promise fold in half’?”

“It keeps the feeling,” Leila said. “It doesn’t give someone the exact sentence.”

They read the new version against every question on the checklist. Could the person be recognized from the details? Not from the changed setting or cup, they thought. Could the event be identified by a place, object, or exact words? The place and object were different, and the exact words were gone. Had the person agreed to the story’s use? Not yet.

Deniz opened the message thread and paused. “I’m nervous.”

“Me too,” Leila said. “We can say exactly what we changed.”

Deniz typed: I wrote a poem about the day our promise changed. Some details came from what happened between us, and I changed the place, object, and exact words because I realized they might identify you. Would you read it and tell me whether you’re comfortable with it being in the youth zine? You can say no, or ask for more changes.

After sending it, Deniz put the phone face down. They finished two captions, checked the cover credits, and waited through seven minutes that felt longer than the whole editing session.

The reply arrived.

I’m glad you asked. I knew it was about me before, even without my name. The new details are less obvious, but the line about the promise still makes me feel exposed. I’m okay with the poem if that part becomes less like the exact moment. Also, don’t use anything that makes it sound like I agreed to be included before today.

Deniz read the message twice. “They’re not saying no.”

“They’re not saying yes to this version,” Leila said. “They’re giving us conditions.”

Deniz changed the line again: Some promises bend until neither person knows how to hold them. Then Deniz sent the revision and waited. This time the reply came more quickly.

This feels like the truth without putting my words on display. Yes, you can publish it in the zine. Thank you for asking first.

Deniz let out a breath. Permission did not feel like a stamp that made everything harmless. It felt like another person had been allowed into the decision.

They marked the checklist together: details changed, exact words removed, permission received. Leila read the poem aloud while Deniz listened for anything that sounded too close to the original. The feeling was still there—raw, awkward, unfinished—but the person’s private edges were no longer exposed.

Deniz dragged the revised file into the ready folder. This time Leila did not move it alone. They both watched the folder accept the file.

The rest of the work became quick but not careless. Deniz corrected the poem’s spacing. Leila finished the captions. They argued briefly about whether the cover needed more white space, then solved it by printing two versions and comparing them on the wall. Near midnight, they sent the completed zine to the center’s youth-media printer.

Leila closed her laptop. “I’m glad we did it together.”

“Me too,” Deniz said. The trust between them was not magically smooth. Deniz could still remember the empty space in the ready folder. But now there was another memory beside it: Leila naming what she had worried about, Deniz naming what had hurt, and both of them changing the work instead of guessing at each other.

They saved the finished files, switched off the media room lights, and left the ready folder shared between them.

By Saturday afternoon, the boxes from the youth-media printer had arrived at Eastwood Community Center. The fresh zines sat in a stack on the media-room table, their covers bright beneath the fluorescent lights.

Deniz ran a thumb along the top copy. “It still feels strange that it’s finished.”

“Finished enough to hand to people,” Leila said. “Which is the dangerous stage.”

They carried a bundle downstairs to the community lounge, where the center’s monthly open house was beginning. People they knew drifted between the notice boards, the snack table, and the folding chairs near the windows. Hana took three copies for the front desk but did not make a speech about them. She only said, “The cover looks good,” and went to help someone find the lost-and-found box.

Deniz and Leila placed the rest beside the sign-in sheet.

For a while, readers flipped through the zine without saying much. Deniz tried not to watch every person who paused at the poem. The title was printed near the middle, above the changed lines and the small byline. The rainy street beside the closed bakery was still there. So was the paper cup with the bent lid. Nothing pointed straight back to the original afternoon.

A girl from the photography group read the poem, then looked up. “This part is uncomfortable,” she said. “But in a good way. It feels like when someone changes their mind and you’re the last person to know.”

Deniz glanced at Leila.

“Yeah,” Deniz said. “That’s what I was trying to keep.”

The girl nodded and turned the page. “It works.”

Later, two younger members argued quietly over which comic to read first. A volunteer laughed at one of the captions Deniz had trimmed. Someone from the music group said the zine felt more personal than the last issue, even though they could not tell who the poem was about.

That comment stayed with Deniz. The poem had not needed the exact place, object, or sentence to remain true. It had needed the feeling—and enough distance for another person’s privacy to remain theirs.

When the crowd thinned, Deniz and Leila returned to the media room. The shared project log was still open on the laptop. Beside the next issue’s blank planning page, Leila typed a short note.

“Too formal?” she asked. “Proposed team habit: concerns go first to the person involved. Bring what we observed, ask questions before deciding what it means, and say when something is a feeling.”

Deniz read it. “Add that the person gets to answer before we change their work.”

Leila added the sentence.

“Good,” Deniz said. “And maybe we don’t need to use it like a rule every time someone forgets a comma.”

Leila smiled. “Evidence: missing comma. Feeling: personally betrayed.”

“Exactly.”

They saved the note in the project folder, where both of them could see it. It was a small action, almost boring. That made Deniz trust it more than a dramatic promise.

On the walk back downstairs, Deniz stopped beside the display table. The first day came back in pieces: the empty space in the ready folder, the heat in their face, the thought that Leila wanted them gone. The embarrassment had not vanished because the poem was now printed. Neither had the anger. But naming those feelings had made room for a question. The question had led to evidence, and the evidence had made conversation possible.

Leila had also changed something real. She had not simply said she meant well. She had shown the marked lines, listened to the person in the poem, revised the work, and stayed beside Deniz while they finished it.

At the door, Leila held out the last copy. “Take it?”

Deniz looked at the poem, then at their friend. Trust still felt less like a locked box than a shared file: something they had to keep opening, checking, and working on together.

“Yeah,” Deniz said. “I’ll take it.”

They left the media room with the zine between them, while the center filled with voices and the next blank page waited in the project folder.
