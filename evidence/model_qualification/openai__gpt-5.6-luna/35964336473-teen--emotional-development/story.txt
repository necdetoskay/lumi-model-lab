The Space Between Words

The community media room smelled faintly of dust, coffee, and the warm plastic of equipment that had been running all afternoon. Deniz knew every part of it: the blue chairs stacked by the wall, the strip of colored tape marking the microphone cables, the editing computers that took forever to wake up. They and Noor had spent three Saturdays here building their neighborhood audio piece from interviews, bus brakes, basketballs, and the old fountain outside.

Deniz pushed open the door expecting Noor to call, “You’re late,” even though Deniz was only two minutes late.

Noor looked up from the nearest computer. She usually grinned or slid one of the rolling chairs over. This time she only said, “Hey,” and turned back to the screen.

Deniz set their backpack down. “Ready to finish the middle section?”

“Mostly,” Noor said. “The showcase deadline’s Friday.”

On the monitor, Deniz’s waveform ended abruptly. The personal recording—the one Deniz had made about moving neighborhoods and missing the apartment they used to live in—was gone from the working timeline. Not muted. Not shifted to another track. Gone.

Deniz clicked through the project folders. The folder they and Noor normally called **Fountain Piece—FINAL-ish** wasn’t on the desktop. It had been moved into a folder labeled **Noor’s Edits**.

For a second, Deniz just stared at the words.

“Where’s my section?” they asked.

Noor’s fingers paused above the keyboard. “I’m still sorting it.”

“Sorting it where?”

Noor glanced toward the hallway. “I have to check something in the workshop. We can talk after, okay? The deadline is close.”

She stood before Deniz could answer and carried her notebook out of the room. The door swung shut behind her with a soft click.

On the desk, Noor had left a message in the project notes: **Need to make choices soon.** Under it was an older message Deniz barely remembered seeing: **Are you sure you want the personal section in the public version?**

Deniz read it twice. The question could have been ordinary. It could also have been the kind of question people asked when they had already decided the answer.

Heat climbed into Deniz’s face. Their recording had been the one part that felt honest, and now it had been cut without even a conversation. Irritation prickled beneath the embarrassment. Worry pressed harder: maybe Noor wanted a cleaner project, a different partner, someone less complicated.

Deniz opened the audio files, then closed them again. The obvious thing would be to pack up and leave before Noor came back. If Noor didn’t want Deniz involved, staying would only make it worse.

But the piece was unfinished. Deniz still wanted to hear the fountain and the basketball court and their own voice fit together. More than that, they wanted Noor to look at them the way she had last week.

So Deniz stayed long enough to save a copy of the unfinished project. Then they put on their backpack and left, carrying the uncomfortable feeling that every ordinary thing Noor had done might now mean rejection.

At home, Deniz dropped their backpack beside the desk but did not unpack it. The saved copy of the audio project sat on their laptop, its file name ending in “unfinished,” which felt less like a description and more like an accusation.

They opened the shared messages with Noor.

There was Noor’s question from two days earlier: **Are you sure you want the personal section in the public version?** Deniz had replied several hours later, after school and a long bus ride: **Maybe leave it out for now. I don’t know.**

Deniz remembered the uncertainty behind those words. They had meant, Maybe don’t decide yet. Or maybe let’s try it without the section and see how it sounds. They had not meant, Remove it without telling me.

Still, the message looked different now. It looked like permission that Deniz had failed to give clearly.

They scrolled up. Noor had sent a shorter message that afternoon: **Deadline’s close. Can we make choices today?** Deniz had not answered. Then came the silence, the missing waveform, and the folder moved into **Noor’s Edits**.

Deniz opened a new message and typed:

**Seriously? You cut my recording and moved our whole project without even asking. If you wanted to do it alone, you could have just said so.**

They read it once. The words made their chest loosen for half a second, as if anger could push the problem back where it belonged. Then the last sentence made them wince. It was not exactly what they knew. It was what they feared.

Deniz deleted “If you wanted to do it alone.” They replaced it with “Why did you do that?” Then deleted the whole message. Their thumb hovered over Noor’s name.

The feeling had a name, though Deniz resisted it at first: embarrassment mixed with fear of being replaced. The recording had been personal, and now Deniz felt foolish for believing it mattered to Noor. They also felt irritated—at Noor for saying almost nothing, and at themselves for writing a message that could make everything worse.

They grabbed a sheet of paper and drew a line down the middle. On the left, they wrote **What I know**.

- My personal recording was cut from the timeline.
- The shared folder was moved into “Noor’s Edits.”
- Noor gave me a short message and said the deadline was close.
- Noor had asked whether the personal section should stay.
- The showcase deadline is Friday.

Deniz stared at the list. None of it proved that Noor wanted them gone. None of it proved the opposite, either.

On the right, they wrote **What I’m guessing**.

- Noor thinks my recording is embarrassing.
- Noor wants a better partner.
- Noor decided the project would be easier without me.

The guesses looked dramatic in their own handwriting. That did not make them disappear. The folder had still been moved. Noor had still left without explaining. Deniz still felt shut out.

They picked up the phone again and opened the unsent draft. Instead of sending it, Deniz saved it in notes and locked the screen.

Not yet. They wanted the project finished. They wanted Noor back on their side. But before asking either of those things, Deniz needed to know what Noor had actually meant.

The next afternoon, Deniz went back to the community media room before the workshop started. They told themselves they were only checking the project. They were not ready to message Noor, and definitely not ready to send the angry draft still sitting in their notes.

The room smelled faintly of dust and warm plastic. Two microphones rested in their foam cradles, and the laptop at the editing station displayed the shared project folder. Deniz opened the edit history, expecting to find only the latest version.

Instead, several saved versions appeared underneath it.

The first was called **neighborhood_piece_v8**. The next was **Noor’s Edits**. Beneath that was a version marked **private—do not publish**.

Deniz’s shoulders tightened. They clicked the version only because it was part of the shared project record, not a hidden account or a private message. The waveform appeared, and there was Deniz’s recording—the whole thing, including the section Noor had removed from the public timeline.

For a moment, Deniz felt a sharp, ridiculous disappointment. So Noor had not erased it. That did not mean she wanted Deniz in the project.

Then Deniz noticed the note attached to the cut section.

**Consent unclear. Hold before public export.**

They read it twice.

The words did not sound like *This is embarrassing* or *Deniz is out*. They sounded almost careful. Maybe Noor had kept the recording safe because Deniz had said, **Maybe leave it out for now.** Maybe she had understood that as permission to remove it from the public version but not permission to delete it.

That interpretation fit the evidence.

So did the other one. Noor had moved the working folder without warning. She had sent only a short message about the deadline. She had not said, *I saved your recording in a private version because I wasn’t sure what you wanted.* Deniz had been left to discover the protective part by clicking through the records.

Both things could be true: Noor might have been trying to protect Deniz’s choice, and Deniz might still have been reasonably hurt by being shut out of the decision.

Deniz opened the message log attached to the project. Their earlier reply sat beside Noor’s question:

**Are you sure you want the personal section in the public version?**

**Maybe leave it out for now. I don’t know.**

Underneath, Noor had added a comment two hours later: **Need definite choice before Friday export.** There was no reply from Deniz. The comment had been waiting there while Deniz imagined that Noor had already decided everything.

Deniz leaned back, rubbing a thumb over the edge of the desk. The facts still did not answer the important questions. Why had Noor avoided explaining? Did Noor think Deniz had abandoned the project after not answering? And what exactly needed to be decided now—the entire recording, one excerpt, or nothing at all?

They took out the sheet from yesterday. Under the list of facts and guesses, Deniz wrote two questions:

- **What did you understand from my message?**
- **What decision do we still need to make?**

They almost added *Why did you cut me out?* Then crossed it out. It was honest, but it carried an answer inside it.

Deniz opened a new message to Noor and typed the first question. Their finger paused above send. The edit history had made the situation less simple, not less painful. Noor’s choice might have been protective. It might also have been a bad handoff. Deniz could not decide which mattered more from a screen.

They saved the two questions beside the project notes, closed the laptop, and went to look for Noor in the workshop room. Not to accuse her. Not yet to forgive her. Just to ask what the records could not say.

Noor was at the workshop table, coiling a microphone cable while two younger volunteers argued cheerfully over a speaker stand. When she saw Deniz waiting by the doorway, her hands stopped.

“Can we talk somewhere quieter?” Deniz asked.

Noor glanced toward the media room. “Yeah.”

They went back through the short hallway. The media room felt familiar—the same microphones in their foam cradles, the same scratched editing desk—but Deniz noticed the empty chair beside Noor’s station as if it had been placed there for someone else.

Deniz opened the laptop but did not sit. “I found the private version. The one marked ‘do not publish.’”

Noor nodded. “Okay.”

“I saw that you kept my full recording. I also saw that you cut the personal part from the working timeline, moved the folder, and left a note saying consent was unclear.” Deniz’s throat tightened. “And then you only said the deadline was close and left. I felt excluded. Embarrassed, honestly. I started thinking you didn’t want me on the project anymore.”

Noor looked down at the cable in her hands. “I didn’t mean that.”

“I know that’s possible,” Deniz said, more sharply than intended. “But I couldn’t tell what you meant.”

Noor took a breath. “I cut it because I didn’t know if you wanted strangers hearing that part. You said, ‘Maybe leave it out for now.’ I thought that meant you weren’t sure, so I kept the full recording private instead of deleting it. The showcase export is public, and I didn’t want to decide for you.”

“Then why didn’t you tell me?”

“I should have.” Noor’s voice was quiet, but not defensive. “I thought the label would explain it. Then I saw your message later—well, the message where you didn’t answer my question about making a definite choice. I figured you were stepping back from the project.”

Deniz frowned. “I didn’t say I was stepping back.”

“You didn’t say you weren’t.”

The sentence landed between them. Deniz looked at the project notes on the screen. Noor’s comment was still there: **Need definite choice before Friday export.**

“I wrote, ‘Maybe leave it out for now. I don’t know,’” Deniz said. “That was vague. I can see how it sounded like I was giving up or making you wait. But when you moved everything into ‘Noor’s Edits,’ it looked like you’d taken over.”

Noor winced. “I can see that. I moved it because I was cleaning up the versions, but I should have told you. And I should have explained the consent note instead of leaving you to find it.”

Deniz sat down at last. The relief of hearing that was real, but it did not erase the hot, embarrassed feeling from the day before. “I kept treating every ordinary thing as proof you were done with me.”

“I treated your silence as proof you were done with me,” Noor said. “Neither of us checked.”

Deniz almost smiled, but stopped short of it. “That sounds simple when you say it.”

“It wasn’t simple.” Noor rubbed the cable between her fingers. “I was annoyed too. I thought I was carrying the deadline alone.”

That mattered. Noor had not been secretly calm and perfectly careful; she had made a choice while frustrated, then failed to explain it. Deniz’s fear was not the whole truth, but it had not appeared from nowhere either.

“So we still need to decide what happens to the recording,” Deniz said.

“Together,” Noor replied. “Can we listen to it now? We can mark the parts that feel too personal and decide what, if anything, gets used before Friday.”

Deniz looked at the waveform, then at Noor. Trust did not snap back into place because Noor had apologized. Still, Noor was sitting beside the same project instead of guarding it from across the room.

“Okay,” Deniz said. “But while we listen, say what you’re changing. No silent edits.”

“No silent edits,” Noor agreed.

They opened the preserved version. The recording waited on the screen, unfinished and still theirs. Neither of them pressed play yet. The decision was not made, but for the first time, they were facing it from the same side of the desk.

Deniz pressed play.

Their voice filled the media room, younger-sounding than it felt in their memory. They talked about the neighborhood basketball court, the broken lights, and the way people still gathered there after dark. Then the recording shifted. Deniz described their mother moving out of the apartment the previous winter and how the court had become the one place that did not ask questions.

Deniz reached for the space bar.

“Stop.”

Noor paused the track. “That part?”

“The sentence about my mother. And the one after it.” Deniz stared at the waveform. “I thought I wanted it in there when I recorded it. But hearing it with the rest… it feels too personal for strangers at the showcase.”

Noor nodded, but did not touch the editing controls. “What did you hear in my message when I asked whether the personal section should stay?”

Deniz considered the question. “At first? I heard that you thought it was a problem. Then, when you cut it without saying anything, I heard that you wanted the project without me.”

Noor winced. “I was asking because I didn’t know what you wanted. I heard ‘Maybe leave it out for now’ as ‘don’t use it yet,’ not as a final no. Then when you didn’t answer, I thought you were done deciding.”

“That’s not what I meant,” Deniz said. “But I can see how it sounded that way.”

They played the section again. Deniz listened with one hand resting on the laptop, ready to stop the track. The words about the court stayed. The words about their mother did not.

“Use the part from ‘The lights are broken’ through ‘people still come anyway,’” Deniz said. “Only that. I’m giving permission for that excerpt in the public version. Not the rest, and not the full recording.”

Noor repeated it back. “Public version: that excerpt only. The personal section stays private.”

“Yes.”

“Do you want your name in the credits for the voice and the excerpt?”

Deniz felt a small knot tighten, then loosen. “Yes, but write that I chose the excerpt. I don’t want it to look like you decided what I was willing to share.”

“I’ll write exactly that.”

This time Noor moved the playhead slowly. “I’m trimming here, after ‘broken.’ I’m leaving half a second so the sentence doesn’t sound chopped. Then I’m fading into the street sounds. Does that work?”

“Play it.”

Noor did. The edit sounded clean. More important, Deniz could follow every choice as it happened. Nothing disappeared silently. No folder shifted while they watched from somewhere else.

The relief arrived carefully, not like a switch. Deniz still remembered the empty chair and the phrase **Noor’s Edits**. The hurt had not vanished just because the track sounded right. But the evidence in front of them was different now: Noor was asking, repeating the boundary, and showing the changes before saving them.

“I’m relieved,” Deniz said. “And still a little annoyed.”

“That seems fair.” Noor saved the excerpt as a new file: **court_excerpt_DENIZ_APPROVED**. “I’m still annoyed too. I felt like I was carrying the deadline alone.”

“Next time, say that before moving the whole project.”

“Next time, answer the question before disappearing into your own head.”

Deniz glanced at her. “Also fair.”

They checked the final piece from the beginning. The neighborhood voices came first, then the court recording, then the music they had built together from a busker’s permission-cleared sample. Deniz listened for the place where their excerpt entered. It belonged there without exposing more than they had chosen.

At the end, Noor opened a blank document and titled it **Friday Showcase Handoff**.

Deniz dictated while Noor typed:

“Public audio includes Deniz’s approved excerpt only. Full personal recording remains private. Noor will upload the final export. Deniz will check the credits and opening levels. Noor will not make consent-related edits without telling Deniz. Deniz will answer project questions clearly. Final export checked by both before the showcase.”

Noor turned the screen toward them. “Anything missing?”

“Add the file name.”

She did. They both read the note once, then Deniz checked the credits and Noor checked the levels. When they agreed, Noor uploaded the finished version to the showcase folder. The confirmation appeared beneath the file.

Submitted.

Deniz looked at the word. The project was done, and the friendship was not magically repaired into its old shape. It felt more deliberate than that—less like guessing and more like having something real to stand on.

Noor closed the laptop. “Same time tomorrow for the presentation setup?”

Deniz hesitated only long enough to notice the hesitation. “Yes. And if you change a file, tell me.”

“If you’re unsure about a message, ask me what I heard.”

Deniz nodded. “Deal.”

They left the approved excerpt and the handoff note open in the shared folder, where both of them could see them.

The community center’s main room looked different with the chairs lined in rows, but Deniz still recognized the scuffed table near the sound booth and the poster board covered in old workshop flyers. People they knew from the neighborhood drifted in carrying paper cups and folding chairs.

Noor stood beside the laptop, holding the printed handoff note. She read the line about the opening levels, then checked the audio meter on-screen. She did not reach for the controls.

“Deniz,” she said, “the first voice comes in a little louder than it did in the room yesterday. Can you check the opening?”

Deniz sat down. The old reaction arrived before the thought: a quick tightening under their ribs, the expectation that something had been changed without them. Then Deniz looked at the screen. The file name was the one they had approved. The handoff note was open beside it. Noor was waiting.

“Did you change the opening level?” Deniz asked.

“No. I only opened the submitted file. I wanted you to check it.”

Deniz played the first twenty seconds through the small monitor. The neighborhood voices sounded clear, just more noticeable in the nearly empty room.

“It’s fine,” Deniz said. “The room is quieter than the media room. Leave it.”

Noor nodded and wrote a short check mark on the note. “Okay. Opening checked by Deniz.”

The event coordinator called them over. “You two are up after the youth choir. Bring the piece up when I point to you.”

During the choir’s last song, Deniz watched people settle into their chairs. A little kid whispered to his father. Someone near the back laughed softly when a chair squeaked. The ordinary noise made Deniz’s stomach jump in a different way. Their recording would be heard by all these people. The excerpt was only about the broken lights and the court, but it still belonged to them.

Noor leaned closer. “Want me to handle the play button?”

Deniz glanced at the handoff note. “I’ll start it. You watch the level?”

“Sure.”

When the coordinator pointed, Deniz pressed play.

The piece began with voices from the neighborhood, then the soft rhythm they had made from the permission-cleared busker sample. The court recording entered exactly where it should.

“The lights are broken,” Deniz’s voice said. “People still come anyway.”

On the screen, the level moved steadily. Noor did not adjust anything. She simply watched, one hand resting beside the trackpad. Deniz listened to the rest of the piece without bracing for a missing sentence or an unexpected cut. At the end, the room filled with applause—not huge or dramatic, but warm and real.

Afterward, while people gathered near the refreshments, Deniz and Noor carried the laptop back to the sound booth. For a moment neither spoke. The awkwardness had not disappeared with the applause. It sat between them, smaller than before but still present.

“I still think about that day,” Deniz said. “The empty chair. The folder called ‘Noor’s Edits.’ I know what you meant now, but it still felt bad.”

Noor folded the handoff note. “I know. And I still remember feeling like I was carrying the deadline alone. I don’t want either of us filling in the blanks next time.”

“Then ask directly,” Deniz said. “If a message or edit is unclear.”

“And answer directly,” Noor replied. “Even if the answer is, ‘I need time to decide.’”

Deniz smiled a little. “That would have helped.”

Noor held out the note. “Should we keep this habit for the next project?”

Deniz took it and slid it into the shared folder. “Yes. Not because nothing will get awkward again. Just so awkward doesn’t get to decide what everything means.”

They returned to the main room, where neighbors were still talking about the piece. Noor checked the next presentation time. Deniz checked the credits on the program and told her they were correct. Neither had to guess what the other meant. The space between words was still there, but now they knew how to cross it.
