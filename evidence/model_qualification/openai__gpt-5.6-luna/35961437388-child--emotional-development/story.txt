The Space Between Words

Every Thursday after school, seven-year-old Deniz walked to the community library by themself. Ms. Narin knew Deniz well. She always waved from the art table, where cardboard, paper tubes, glue, and bright paint waited.

Ece was already there. “You made it!” she said.

Deniz dropped their backpack beside the table. Together, they worked on their cardboard town. The tiny houses stood along a painted road. A paper-tube tower leaned beside the river. Today, they were finishing the bridge.

Deniz held the two sides steady while Ece brushed blue paint across the top. “It needs one more stripe,” Ece said. “Then cars can cross.”

Deniz smiled and reached for a yellow paper car.

Just then, Ece picked up a box of craft sticks. The box bumped the bridge. One cardboard side bent with a soft crack.

“Oh!” Ece set the box down quickly. She pressed the bridge flat, but a support underneath had folded.

Deniz stared at it. “We can fix it.”

Ece shut the little notebook beside her elbow. “Not yet,” she said.

She lifted the notebook and hurried over to Ms. Narin. The two of them bent close and whispered. Deniz could not hear the words. Ms. Narin glanced toward the bridge, then back at Ece.

Deniz looked at the bent road and the closed notebook. A few minutes ago, Ece had been laughing and sharing the blue paint. Now she was whispering without Deniz.

The library was still warm and familiar. The art table was still full of supplies. But the space beside Ece felt different.

Deniz tucked the yellow paper car into their backpack. On the way out, they glanced back once. Ece was still talking quietly to Ms. Narin.

Maybe Ece did not want Deniz’s help anymore.

The next Thursday, Deniz came back to the library with the bridge plan tucked under one arm. The paper showed every street in the cardboard town, including the bridge that was supposed to carry the yellow car across the river.

Ece was at the art table, but she was not sitting by the bridge. She had moved to the other side, where she sorted paper scraps into little piles.

Deniz chose a chair farther away. Their chest felt tight. They looked at the feeling carefully.

Hurt, Deniz thought. I feel hurt.

It was not the same as being angry. Deniz still wanted to build the town. They still wanted Ece to be their friend. But Ece had closed her notebook, whispered with Ms. Narin, and said, “Not yet.”

Ece glanced at the bridge, then quickly looked down. She carried a small box to Ms. Narin.

“Could I have some tape?” Ece asked.

Ms. Narin handed her a roll. Ece held it close to her shirt and spoke in a low voice. Deniz heard only a few words.

“I can fix something before Deniz sees it.”

Deniz bent over the bridge plan. The words landed like a pebble in their shoe. Before Deniz sees it. Why did Ece not want Deniz to see?

When Ece went to the supply shelf, Deniz looked at the bridge from their chair. One support underneath was bent, and a strip of tape had been pressed along its edge. It had not held. A smear of blue paint marked Ece’s fingers as she reached for another piece of cardboard.

Deniz kept both hands on the plan. They wanted to ask, What are you hiding? But the question felt sharp, and Ece had not spoken to them yet.

Maybe Ece had damaged the bridge and was trying to fix it. Maybe she thought Deniz would be upset. Or maybe the secret meant Ece truly wanted to work alone.

Deniz did not know which thought was true. They watched Ece return to the table and stayed quiet, holding the bridge plan close.

Deniz waited until Ece carried the paper scraps to the other side of the table. Then Deniz walked to the bridge.

They did not touch the town. They only leaned close, the way they leaned close to read tiny labels on library shelves.

The bridge had more than one problem. A fresh strip of tape clung to the side, wrinkled at one end. The left support was loose. Under the bridge, something white stuck out from behind a cardboard tree.

Deniz pulled it out carefully.

It was a small folded piece of paper. Deniz opened it just enough to read the words inside:

Fix before Deniz sees.

Deniz read the sentence twice. The words made their stomach wobble. Ece had been hiding something. That much seemed true. But hiding a repair was different from hiding a friendship.

Deniz folded the note again and placed it where it had been. Then they went to Ms. Narin, who was cutting windows for the cardboard houses.

“Did Ece borrow tape?” Deniz asked.

“Yes,” said Ms. Narin. “She asked for some a little while ago.”

“Did she say why?”

Ms. Narin set down the scissors. “She wanted to use it for a project.”

Deniz waited, but Ms. Narin did not say anything else. She did not tell Deniz what Ece had planned. Maybe she did not know. Maybe it was Ece’s story to tell.

Deniz returned to the bridge and sat with the plan in their lap.

What I saw, Deniz thought: Ece bent the bridge, closed her notebook, said “Not yet,” asked for tape, and wrote a note about fixing it.

What I guessed: Ece did not want me to help anymore.

Deniz looked at the wrinkled tape and the loose support. The clues could fit that guess. They could also fit another one. Maybe Ece felt embarrassed because the bridge had been damaged. Maybe she was trying to make it better before showing Deniz.

Deniz still felt hurt. The hurt had not vanished. But it no longer pointed in only one direction.

Across the table, Ece glanced over. Deniz almost asked her about the note. The words crowded together, then stopped.

Only Ece could explain what she meant. Deniz held the bridge plan close and decided that guessing could wait, but asking could not wait forever.

Deniz watched Ece stack the paper scraps. The question was still there, bumping around inside Deniz’s head.

Guessing could wait, but asking could not wait forever.

Deniz found a small piece of scrap paper and wrote three reminders. The first said, “What happened?” The second said, “I felt left out.” The third said, “Ask about the bridge.”

The words looked plain and steady. Deniz held the paper in one hand and walked to Ece.

Ece was putting tiny cardboard windows into a box. When she saw Deniz, her fingers stopped.

Deniz took a breath.

“I felt left out when you said, ‘Not yet,’” Deniz said. “Can we talk about the bridge?”

Ece looked at the bent bridge. Then she looked at the small note in Deniz’s hand.

“Did you find that?” she asked.

Deniz nodded. “I did. I didn’t read anything else.”

Ece pressed her lips together. She looked worried, not cross.

“I was trying to help,” she said. “But I haven’t explained everything.”

“Help with the bridge?” Deniz asked.

Ece nodded slowly. “Yes. With the bridge.”

Deniz’s fingers tightened around the reminder paper. That answer helped a little, but it did not fill the empty space. What had Ece been helping with? Why had she closed the notebook? Why had “Not yet” sounded so final?

“I thought maybe you didn’t want me to work with you anymore,” Deniz said. The words felt scary to let out, but they did not break anything.

Ece’s eyes grew wide. “No. I didn’t mean that.”

She reached toward the bridge, then pulled her hand back.

“I still need to tell you the whole thing,” she said. “Can we talk after we put away the scraps and paint?”

Deniz looked at the crowded table. The blue paint jars needed lids. The paper scraps belonged in the bin. The talk would have to wait a few minutes, but now it had a place to happen.

“Okay,” Deniz said. “After the supplies.”

Together, they began sorting the table. They worked quietly, with the bent bridge between them. The questions were still there, but neither child was carrying them alone.

When the last paint jar was closed, Deniz and Ece carried the bent bridge to a clear part of the table. The cardboard town waited nearby, with its tiny houses and paper trees.

Ece sat down. Deniz sat beside her, leaving the bridge between them.

“I should tell you first,” Ece said. She touched the wrinkled tape. “When I moved the box of craft sticks, my elbow bumped the bridge. This side folded.”

Deniz looked at the loose support. “You broke it.”

Ece nodded. “I did. I felt embarrassed. You worked hard on it, and I thought you would be upset.”

“So you closed your notebook,” Deniz said, “and said, ‘Not yet.’”

“I was drawing a way to fix it,” Ece said. “I didn’t want you to see the broken part before I made it better. But my first tape did not work.” She glanced at her fingers. “Then I asked Ms. Narin for more tape.”

Deniz picked up the small repair note. “I saw this too. It said, ‘Fix before Deniz sees.’”

“I meant to show you after I fixed it,” Ece said. “I should have told you I had bent it.”

Deniz rubbed the edge of the bridge. “When you hid it and said ‘Not yet,’ I thought you didn’t want me to help anymore. The closed notebook and the secret tape made that seem true.”

Ece’s shoulders lowered. “I’m sorry I made you feel left out.”

“I’m sorry I guessed instead of asking,” Deniz said. “But I was hurt.”

“I know now,” Ece said. “And I was embarrassed.”

The empty space between them did not feel so big anymore. Ece took a fresh strip of tape, and Deniz found a clean piece of thick cardboard from the scrap box.

“Could this be a new support?” Deniz asked.

Ece held the bridge steady. “Yes. Put it under the middle.”

Deniz slid the cardboard into place. Ece taped one side, then Deniz taped the other. They pressed the tape flat with their thumbs. The bridge still had a wrinkle, but it stood straighter than before.

They tested it with the yellow paper car. Deniz pushed the car across. It rolled over the middle and stopped beside a cardboard bakery.

“It works,” Ece said.

“Stronger than before,” Deniz said.

Ece smiled a little. “Next time, I’ll tell you before I move our work.”

“And I’ll ask what happened before I decide what it means,” Deniz said.

Together, they carried the repaired bridge back to the town.

The repaired bridge stood between the cardboard bakery and the little paper park. The yellow car rested on the other side, as if it had just finished a trip.

Deniz and Ece added the last roof to a tall cardboard tower. Ece held the tower with both hands.

“Should I move it next to the bridge?” she asked.

Deniz looked at the town. “Yes. Put it beside the bakery, but leave room for the car to turn.”

“Here?” Ece asked, pointing to a clear spot.

“Maybe one finger more to the left.”

Ece moved it carefully. Deniz watched the bridge, the bakery, and the road made from a strip of brown paper. When the tower was in place, the town looked bigger.

“There’s a space between the tower and the bakery,” Ece said.

Deniz found a small white scrap. “We could put a garden there.”

“Or a tiny library,” Ece said.

Deniz printed three words in careful block letters: ASK FIRST. Ece helped tape the sign to the scrap. They placed it in the empty space.

Ece read it aloud. “Ask first.”

Deniz grinned. “The best rule in the whole town.”

“Even for tiny librarians?”

“Especially tiny librarians.”

They laughed, and Ece made a paper bench for the little library. Deniz folded two blue scraps into chairs. Soon the empty space had a bench, two chairs, and the sign standing between them.

Across the room, Ms. Narin began putting away the colored paper. She turned off one lamp, then another. The library grew quiet except for the soft slide of cardboard and tape.

“Five more minutes,” Ms. Narin called. “Then it is time to pack up.”

Deniz placed the yellow car at the top of the bridge. “Ready?”

“Ready,” said Ece.

They watched it roll down the stronger middle and stop beside the new little library.

Deniz put the car in their backpack. Ece carried the tower box, and Deniz carried the town map. Together, they looked at their cardboard streets one last time.

The bridge was stronger. The town had a new library. And between its buildings was a small space for asking first.

Ms. Narin held the door open. Deniz and Ece walked out side by side, already talking about where the next paper tree should go.
