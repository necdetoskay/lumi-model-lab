The Space Between Words

By the time Deniz reached Common Ground, the front windows were glowing against the gray afternoon. The community space was already in its usual rhythm: the espresso machine hissed behind the café counter, someone tested a microphone in the small performance room, and two regular volunteers argued cheerfully over whether the folding tables belonged in the library corner or the hallway.

Deniz knew most of the people there well enough to borrow a charger, interrupt a conversation, or leave a bag behind without worrying. At twenty-one, they had a part-time job, rent to keep track of, and enough experience at Common Ground to be trusted with more than carrying chairs. For the past month, they and Mina had been preparing a listening wall for the neighborhood open house. Visitors would write short responses to prompts such as What helps you feel at home here? and pin them to a large display near the entrance.

Deniz carried a stack of revised labels under one arm. They had tightened the wording, enlarged the headings, and added a small note explaining where the responses would be displayed. The labels had taken most of Tuesday night, squeezed between a closing shift and an early appointment.

Near the welcome desk, a printed roster was taped to the wall. Deniz slowed as they passed it.

Mina’s name was beside Welcome Table: Saturday, 10–12.

Deniz’s name was not.

They read the list twice. Their name appeared under Setup and Materials, but the welcome-table slot—the one they and Mina had planned together—had been crossed out in blue pen. Someone had written Noor beside it.

Mina stood three steps away, sorting colored clips into a plastic tray. She glanced up. “You brought the labels?”

“Yeah.” Deniz held up the stack. “I fixed the contrast on the gray ones.”

“Great. Put them by the wall.” Mina looked toward the roster. “I changed the roles a little. Noor can cover the table, and you’re better with the display anyway.”

The explanation was brief, almost casual. Before Deniz could ask what had prompted the change, Mina turned toward the half-finished wall and called, “Sam, do we have another roll of mounting tape?”

Sam answered from the supply closet. Mina crossed the room to check.

Deniz remained beside the roster, the labels pressing awkwardly against their ribs. They could have followed Mina. They could have said, I’m not asking about the display; I’m asking why you moved me. Instead, they set the labels on the nearest table and watched Mina discuss tape lengths with Sam.

“Better with the display,” Deniz repeated silently.

It might have meant exactly what Mina said. The display did need work, and Deniz had designed most of it. But the sentence landed differently beside the crossed-out shift. Mina had not asked whether Deniz agreed. She had simply replaced them.

A familiar, embarrassing thought arrived before Deniz could examine it: Mina did not want them at the front anymore. Maybe she had stopped trusting them to represent the project. Maybe this was a quiet way of saying Deniz was no longer part of the partnership in the same way.

Across the room, Mina laughed at something Sam said. Deniz looked down at the unfinished labels. The open house was four days away, and there was still a consent form they had meant to check. They gathered the stack, suddenly unwilling to leave it there, and headed for the back worktable without saying anything more.

Deniz came back to Common Ground the next afternoon with the labels in a canvas folder and an hour before their shift at the bookstore. The place felt familiar in the way it always did: the same chipped mug near the café register, the same volunteer playlist drifting from the performance room, the same two people arguing over whether the library-corner chairs could be called furniture if they folded.

They nodded to Noor at the welcome desk but did not mention the roster. Noor was answering an email, and Deniz was not ready to explain why seeing her name beside Saturday’s shift made their stomach tighten.

At the back worktable, Deniz opened the shared project notebook on their laptop. They had planned to finish the display labels, but the blank document seemed to wait for a different kind of work. After a moment, they opened a new page and typed two headings:

WHAT HAPPENED

WHAT I’M ASSUMING

Under the first heading, they wrote:

Mina changed the roster.
My name moved from Welcome Table to Setup and Materials.
She said I was better with the display.
She was brief and turned away before I could ask more.

Deniz stared at the last sentence. It was an observation, technically. It was also carrying more weight than the others.

Under the second heading, they wrote:

She is tired of working with me.
She does not trust me with the project.
She wants to push me out without saying so.

The words looked dramatic on the screen, but not ridiculous. That was the uncomfortable part. Deniz had not invented the roster change or Mina’s clipped explanation. They had built a conclusion around those facts, then started reacting to the conclusion as if Mina had announced it.

They leaned back and tried to name what was happening without softening it into “a weird mood.” The feeling was embarrassment mixed with fear of being unwanted. Embarrassment because they might have failed at something obvious. Fear because the project had become one of the places where Deniz felt useful, known, and expected.

The shared folder held the project schedule, the prompt list, and a running set of notes. Deniz clicked through the entries from the past week. There it was, in a line they had somehow avoided reading closely:

Consent forms: Deniz to review by Tuesday evening. Confirm public-display permissions and flag anything unclear.

Below it, Mina had added a note from Wednesday morning: Covered consent explanation for staff check-in. Still need second privacy review before labels are finalized.

Deniz checked the date. Tuesday evening had been the night of their closing shift. They had meant to review the forms afterward, then gone home too tired to keep their eyes open. They had not told Mina they were behind. Mina had apparently explained the process to staff herself instead.

A flush moved up Deniz’s neck. There was evidence here for Mina being frustrated. Not proof that she wanted Deniz gone, but enough to make the roster change less arbitrary than it had felt beside the welcome desk.

Then Deniz noticed Mina’s earlier note, marked with a yellow star: Please do a second privacy check on any response that might identify a person indirectly. Some neighbors used specific workplaces or family details. Let’s separate those before display.

Deniz read it twice. Mina had been worried about the project’s responsibility to the people who had contributed, not only about who stood at the front table. The note could explain the reassignment. It could also sit alongside frustration about the missed deadline. It did not answer the personal question.

Deniz added a third line to the edge of the page: Ask, don’t fill in the silence.

They nearly deleted it. Instead, they saved the document, closed the laptop, and pulled the unfinished labels from the folder. The feeling had not disappeared. Neither had the uncertainty. But now the facts occupied one place, and the story Deniz had made from them occupied another.

Deniz opened the consent folder again, this time with the privacy note beside it. The forms were scans of handwritten pages and short online submissions, each matched to a response number rather than a name. Most had a clear yes beside “May this response appear on the listening wall?” A few did not.

One response was marked anonymous, but the writer had mentioned being the only nurse on the overnight shift at a particular clinic. Another described “my daughter’s violin lesson at the blue house on Mercer.” In a neighborhood this small, anonymous did not necessarily mean unidentifiable. A third form had the permission box left blank altogether.

Deniz sat back. The wall was supposed to give people room to speak without turning their words into clues about who they were. If those responses went up beside the bright new labels, the project could expose more than the contributors had agreed to share.

Mina had not invented a concern. She had noticed a real problem, and Deniz had been assigned to catch it before the deadline. The fact of the missed review was still uncomfortable. So was the fact that Deniz’s mind had taken that mistake, combined it with Mina’s briefness, and built a much larger story: Mina was done trusting them; Mina wanted them out.

The story had started with something real. It had simply traveled farther than the evidence allowed.

Deniz sorted the questionable forms into a temporary folder and gave it a plain name: HOLD—PERMISSION TO CONFIRM. They did not delete anything or decide on their own that every unclear response should be excluded. The forms belonged to the contributors, and the project needed an agreed process.

For several minutes, Deniz considered writing to Mina. The first draft came out defensive: I found the privacy issues you were worried about. Then they deleted it. The sentence sounded like a point scored.

They tried again.

I’m reviewing the consent forms. A few anonymous responses don’t have clear permission for public display. Should I separate those from the labels for now?

Deniz read it twice. It asked about the work. It did not smuggle in the roster, the missed deadline, or the fear underneath both. They sent it before they could turn a practical question into a performance of being fine.

Mina’s reply arrived a few minutes later.

Yes, and we need to talk.

Deniz looked at the message until the screen dimmed.

The first half was clear. Separate the forms. There was a task to do, and Deniz could do it carefully. The second half refused to settle into one meaning. Mina might want to discuss the privacy problem. She might want to discuss the missed review. She might want to discuss the roster. All three, perhaps. The message did not say that Mina was ending the partnership, but it did not reassure Deniz that nothing had changed either.

Their stomach tightened again, though the feeling was different from the day before. It was still embarrassment, still fear of being unwanted, but now it had company: the uneasy knowledge that a real conversation was approaching.

Deniz typed, Okay. I’ll separate the unclear ones and leave the labels unfinished until we decide what to do with them. After a pause, they added, When would be a good time to talk?

Mina answered that she would finish evening setup at Common Ground and could meet in the small workroom afterward.

Deniz moved the flagged forms into the temporary folder, then placed the folder beside the revised labels. The two piles looked ordinary: paper, scans, unfinished text. Neither could explain Mina’s intention. For that, Deniz would have to ask her in person—and listen to an answer that might be more complicated than the story they had first told themselves.

By late afternoon, Common Ground had settled into its in-between hour. The café counter was still busy, but the performance room stood empty, and the library corner held only two people working quietly at a shared table. Deniz knew the rhythms of the place well: the delivery driver would arrive before six, Noor would take over the welcome desk, and Mina would finish evening setup in the small workroom near the storage shelves.

Deniz carried the flagged forms and the unfinished labels there, then chose a table beneath the narrow window. The materials waited in two uneven piles. One represented what still needed doing. The other represented what they did not yet know.

They opened a blank document and typed:

I saw the roster change, felt pushed aside, and started assuming you were done trusting me.

Deniz read the sentence several times. It made their chest tighten, but not because it sounded unfair. It sounded accurate. The feeling had not been only anger. It had been embarrassment, then fear of being unwanted, with irritation layered over both. The roster had changed. Mina had been brief. Those were the events. The rejection story had been Deniz's interpretation.

They added another line.

I don't know whether that was your intention. I want to understand what you meant, and I want to tell you how the change affected me.

That was better. It left room for Mina to answer instead of forcing her into an explanation Deniz had already written.

For a moment, Deniz considered listing every detail that had hurt: Mina turning toward another task, the empty space beside Noor's name, the way Deniz had carried the labels to the back table without being asked. But the list began to sound like evidence in a case. Deniz deleted most of it.

The facts they could own were harder to remove.

I missed the Tuesday review. I left permissions unclear. You had to cover the consent explanation with staff. You wrote that the forms needed another privacy check.

Deniz paused over the last sentence. Mina's note had been practical, but it had also been the clearest sign that the project had a problem before Deniz noticed it. If Deniz wanted context, they had to bring the whole picture, not only the part that made them feel abandoned.

They typed one more paragraph:

I want to ask what you intended when you changed the roster. If the project needs a safer process, I can rebuild the consent file tonight. We could separate the unclear responses, contact contributors where needed, and review the final display together before anything goes up.

The proposal felt concrete enough to be useful and small enough not to pretend that one evening's work would repair everything. It was not an apology disguised as a task. Deniz still felt hurt. Completing the file would not make that feeling imaginary. But responsibility and hurt could occupy the same conversation.

A chair scraped in the corridor. Someone laughed near the café, and a familiar voice called goodbye to Noor. Deniz closed the document, then opened it again. They did not want to walk into the workroom clutching a speech. They wanted a beginning they could actually say.

On a scrap of paper, they wrote four words: observation, feeling, responsibility, request.

Then they crossed out the labels.

“Can we talk about the roster?” Deniz said quietly to the empty room. “I saw that my name had been removed, and I felt pushed aside. I started assuming you were done trusting me. I know I missed the consent review, and I see why the privacy issue matters. I want to know what you intended—and I want to help fix what I left unfinished.”

The words sounded less polished aloud. That helped. Mina would not be answering a document. She would be answering Deniz.

Deniz gathered the forms and labels into one folder, leaving the unclear responses marked for review rather than deciding their fate alone. Then they walked toward the small workroom, where Mina was finishing the evening setup. The conversation was still unresolved. So was the project. But Deniz was carrying questions instead of conclusions.

Mina was at the workroom table, sorting binder clips into a shallow tray. The open-house signs leaned against the wall behind her, and a roll of kraft paper rested under one chair. She looked up when Deniz entered.

“Hey,” she said. “Did you bring the forms?”

“I did.” Deniz set the folder on the table. Their hand stayed on it for a moment. “Before we start, can we talk about the roster?”

Mina nodded, though her shoulders tightened. “Okay.”

Deniz pulled out the chair across from her. The room felt familiar—the humming fluorescent light, the faint smell of coffee from the café, the storage shelves crowded with supplies—but sitting there felt different from any ordinary project meeting.

“I saw that my name had been removed from the welcome-table roster,” Deniz said. “You told me the roles had changed, and then you turned back to setup. I felt pushed aside. I started assuming you were done trusting me.”

Mina looked down at the clips. Deniz kept going before the sentence could become an accusation.

“I know that assumption went beyond what I actually knew. I missed the Tuesday consent review. I left permissions unclear, and you had to explain the project to staff. I saw your note asking for another privacy check. I understand why that mattered. But I still need to know what you intended when you changed my role.”

Mina was quiet long enough for someone to pass in the hallway. Then she rubbed one thumb over the edge of the tray.

“I intended to keep the welcome table from becoming another thing you had to manage,” she said. “That was part of it. But mostly, I was worried about the forms.”

Deniz waited.

“On Tuesday, staff asked whether we had clear permission for every response on the wall. I realized I did not know. You were supposed to flag the unclear ones, and you hadn’t finished the review. Then I found the responses that could identify people even though they were marked anonymous.” Mina exhaled. “I covered the explanation because I was there, but I was resentful. I thought, if Deniz is at the welcome table, I’ll have to keep wondering whether the display is safe.”

The words stung, but they were specific. They gave the hurt a shape other than rejection.

“So you moved me because you didn’t trust my project work,” Deniz said.

“I didn’t trust the process at that moment,” Mina replied. “And I was angry that I had to cover for it. I should have said that instead of making a new roster and pretending ‘better suited to the display’ explained anything.”

Deniz looked at the empty space between them. “When you didn’t explain, I took the change personally. I’m sorry I missed the deadline. I should have told you I was behind instead of letting the forms sit.”

“I’m sorry I changed your role without talking to you,” Mina said. “You had a right to understand what was happening. And being brief with you afterward probably made it look like I was withdrawing from you, not just dealing with the project.”

“It did,” Deniz said. “I was embarrassed, and then afraid you didn’t want me involved anymore.”

Mina met their eyes. “I do want you involved. I was avoiding a conversation because I felt resentful and didn’t want to say something sharp.”

“That makes sense,” Deniz said. “It still affected me.”

“I know.”

Neither apology erased the awkwardness. It did make the room less crowded with guesses.

Deniz opened the folder. “Can we fix the file now? Not as a way to skip over this. Just because it needs fixing.”

“Yes,” Mina said. “Let’s do it together.”

They spread the forms across the table. First they made three sections: approved for display, permission needing confirmation, and do not display. Mina read each response aloud while Deniz checked the original consent marks and noted any detail that could identify the writer. When a response had no clear permission, they moved it to the middle section rather than arguing themselves into an answer.

One form belonged to a neighbor who had given permission for a written response but not for any detail about their family. Deniz caught the problem before it reached the label draft. Mina circled the identifying sentence and added a note: contact contributor before display. Another response had clear consent but included a street name that made the writer obvious. Together they shortened the displayed text and recorded why.

After an hour, the file was no longer a jumble of question marks. Every response had a documented choice: display as written, display with an agreed edit, hold until the contributor replied, or keep private. The held forms went into the marked folder. The safe ones returned beside the labels.

Mina closed the binder. “For the rest of the project, you’ll handle the consent tracker and display labels. I’ll do the second privacy review. Neither of us approves a response alone. We check the file together before anything goes up.”

“And I’ll still help at the welcome table?” Deniz asked.

“Yes. You’ll cover the first hour with Noor, then switch to the display when the visitor flow picks up. I’ll be nearby for the review and the questions.”

Deniz nodded. “That works.”

Mina slid half the labels toward them. “Can we start again with these?”

Deniz picked up a pen. The trust between them was not magically untouched; the afternoon had left marks. But Mina was sitting across the table now, explaining her choices, and Deniz was no longer trying to fill the silence with the worst possible answer.

“Show me which labels you changed,” Deniz said. “I’ll check them against the new file.”

Mina turned the first page toward them. “Together?”

“Together.”

By Saturday morning, Common Ground had changed shape. Folding chairs filled the performance room, the café counter was serving coffee faster than usual, and the listening wall stretched across the library-corner partition in neat bands of color.

Deniz stood on a step stool, fastening the last revised label beneath a row of responses. The wording was cleaner now. A family detail had been removed from one answer, and every response on the wall matched a decision in the consent file.

Mina came past with a roll of blue tape. “The welcome table is ready,” she said. “Noor’s covering the first half-hour with you.”

“Good. I’ll check the display before people come in.”

Mina handed over the tape without looking worried or hurried. “I’ll do the second review after the first group arrives.”

It was an ordinary exchange, which was part of what made it matter. They had followed the agreement all week. Deniz updated the tracker; Mina checked the privacy notes. When one of them was unsure, they left the response in the hold folder instead of making a confident guess.

At the bottom of the wall, Deniz paused over a short card titled “What makes a place feel like yours?” The response said, “When the people here know what happened on Maple.” It had been marked anonymous, and the writer had checked permission for the words to be displayed. But Maple Street was small. Someone who knew the neighborhood might recognize the story.

Deniz held the card between two fingers and looked toward Mina, who was arranging sign-in sheets near the doorway.

“Can you check this one with me?” Deniz asked.

Mina crossed the room. Deniz showed her the card and pointed to the note in the tracker.

“I’m not sure the permission covers the identifying detail,” Deniz said. “The words are approved, but this might still narrow it down.”

Mina read the response twice. “I agree. Let’s hold it until we can ask the contributor. We have enough for the wall without it.”

Deniz placed the card in the marked folder. “I was tempted to leave it up because the wording was already approved.”

“Thanks for stopping,” Mina said. “That’s exactly the kind of thing the second review is for.”

She returned to the sign-in sheets. Deniz looked at the empty space where the card would have gone. It did not feel like failure. It felt like a choice they had made together, with the information they had.

The doors opened at ten. Neighbors drifted in carrying tote bags and paper cups, greeting people they knew. A child pressed both palms against the edge of the kraft-paper wall. An older man read three responses, then went back to the first one. Deniz welcomed visitors with Noor while Mina moved between the display and the café counter.

A woman stopped near the privacy note at the end of the wall. “Why are some responses shortened?” she asked.

Deniz pointed to the small sign Mina had helped write: Responses are displayed only with permission. Details may be edited or held if they could identify someone. “Some people agreed to share an answer but not every detail around it,” Deniz said. “We wanted the wall to keep the meaning without making anyone recognizable by accident.”

The woman nodded. “That makes sense.”

Later, Mina joined Deniz at the welcome table during a lull. Noor had gone to help in the performance room. Mina passed Deniz a stack of visitor cards, and their hands met briefly over the pile.

“Your explanation was clear,” Mina said.

“So was the sign.”

They smiled, then turned as another visitor approached.

By afternoon, the wall was surrounded by quiet conversations. People added new responses to the blank section, and Deniz checked each one before placing it in the review tray. Once, a volunteer asked whether a newly written card could go up immediately. Deniz felt the quick pull to decide before anyone had to wait.

They named it silently: impatience, not certainty.

“I’ll check the permission first,” Deniz said. “Mina, can you take a look with me?”

Mina came over. Together, they read the card, confirmed the choice, and placed it on the wall.

The event continued around them—the scrape of chairs, the hiss of the coffee machine, voices rising and folding into one another. Deniz still noticed pauses in conversations. They still sometimes felt the urge to turn a changed tone into an answer. But now there was room between what happened and what their mind supplied. In that room, they could ask.

Mina lifted the next card from the tray. “Ready?”

Deniz checked the tracker, then nodded. “Ready.”
