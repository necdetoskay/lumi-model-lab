The Signal in the Mist

Ada liked the misty coast best when it was quiet. The cliffs stood above the sea like great gray steps, and below them, waves whispered in and out of the tidal caves.

For two evenings, just before sunset, Ada noticed a pale light blink beside the cliffs.

Blink. Blink-blink.

Then it vanished.

She did not decide that it came from a lantern, a boat, or a cave. Those were guesses, and Ada wanted evidence first. She took out her small notebook and wrote the time. The light had appeared for only a few minutes each evening. She counted the flashes carefully: one, then two close together, then one more after a pause.

On the second evening, she stood in the same place and watched again. The flashes appeared in the same part of the coast, though the mist softened their edges. Ada held up her pencil and lined it with the light. In her notebook, she drew an arrow toward the shore instead of writing what she thought the light was.

“Direction first,” she murmured. “Answer later.”

Near the edge of the cliff path stood an old waystone, rounded by wind and salt. Its carved face pointed toward the lower shore. Farther along, Ada spotted a low cave opening between two rocks. It was wide enough to see the gentle shine of water inside.

Below the cliff, a dark tide line marked how high the sea had reached. Ada sketched that, too, adding a note: Check tide before going lower.

The light blinked once more through the mist, then disappeared.

Ada closed her notebook. Tomorrow, she would compare the signal with the waystone and the tide line. She still did not know what made the light—and she was pleased that she did not know yet.

The next evening, Ada returned to the cliff path with her notebook tucked safely in her pocket. Her eyes went first to the tallest waystone, the one that rose above the others like a stone finger.

“The highest stone must hold the light,” she said. “It can see the farthest.”

She climbed to it and waited. Its carved face stayed gray and dull in the mist. Then, below and across the water, the pale signal blinked.

Blink. Blink-blink.

Ada looked from the light to the tall stone. The direction did not match. The signal shone lower, near the shore, just as her first sketch had shown.

She was not disappointed. A guess was useful when it could be tested.

Ada followed the slope downward, stopping beside a smaller waystone near the edge of the lower path. A fresh wet streak shone on its seaward face. It began near the top and ran toward the cave opening, exactly along the direction of the signal.

Ada held her pencil beside the streak. Then she held it toward the blinking light.

“They point the same way,” she whispered.

The lower stone did not seem to make the light. But it might mark the route toward whatever did.

She drew the stone in her notebook and added a bold arrow. Lower waystone. Wet mark faces cave.

Before taking another step, Ada crouched to study the tide line. The sea had crept higher since yesterday, leaving a shining band on the rocks below. Small waves slid toward the cave mouth and retreated again.

Ada counted the space between the water and the dark mark. There was room to reach the cave now, but not much time before the tide rose farther.

“Check the tide before going lower,” she read from her earlier note.

So she marked the present water level, watched one more flash from the shore, and turned back before the path became wet.

The lower waystone pointed toward the cave. The tide decided when that route was safe. Tomorrow, at low tide, Ada would follow both clues carefully.

At the next low tide, Ada returned with her boots tied firmly and her notebook wrapped in oilcloth. The lower waystone pointed toward the cave, just as it had the evening before. She checked the water twice, then stepped down the dry part of the path.

The cave was small and calm. Soft daylight entered through the mouth, and the pale mist outside made the walls glow silver. Ada could see every step.

Near the entrance, she found a row of shallow marks in the stone. Each one curved like a little wave. They led inward, toward the back wall.

“Another direction,” Ada said, and copied the marks into her notebook.

Farther inside, a thin notch crossed the wall at knee height. It looked like a line someone had carefully scratched into the stone. Ada compared it with her tide notes. The line was higher than the water outside now, but it matched the dark tide line she had seen below the cliff.

“Tide-height notch,” she wrote. “Leave before water reaches here.”

Beyond the notch lay a smooth, pale patch in the floor. At first it looked no brighter than a seashell. Ada dipped two fingers into a small puddle and touched the patch. Dampness spread across it, and the pale surface shone more clearly, like a pebble beneath clear water.

Ada leaned closer, but did not touch it again. The patch was interesting. It was not an answer yet.

Then she noticed a narrow opening high in the back wall. It pointed toward a slice of evening sky. Through it, Ada could see a strip of blue-gray air and one bright edge of cloud.

She drew the opening beside the wave marks and the pale patch. The lower waystone pointed here. The tide left a mark here. The cave held a pale surface and an opening aimed outward.

Perhaps the clues worked together. Perhaps they did not. Ada would need to watch them at the right time.

A small wave whispered outside. Water touched the first stone near the entrance.

Ada closed her notebook and checked the notch once more. The tide had not reached it, but it was rising.

“Tomorrow, I compare,” she said.

She followed the wave marks back out, leaving the cave safely before the water reached the marked stones.

The next evening, Ada returned before the sky turned gold. She carried her notebook, a short piece of chalk, and the careful route she had drawn: lower waystone, wave marks, tide notch, pale patch.

She checked the lower waystone first. Its wet streak faced the cave, and its edge lined up with the narrow opening high in the back wall. Ada placed a small chalk mark on the path so she could compare the direction again.

“Direction alone is not enough,” she said. “I need the tide and the time, too.”

She followed the wave-shaped marks into the cave, stopping well before the water reached the notch. Outside, the evening signal had not yet appeared. Mist drifted past the cave mouth in soft ribbons, hiding and revealing the far shore.

Ada watched her little tide-clock, then looked at the notch.

When the rising water reached the scratched line, it spread across the lower stones. At the same moment, the puddle beside the pale patch grew wider. The smooth surface darkened with dampness, and its brightness changed from a quiet gleam to a clear, silvery shine.

Ada looked from the patch to the waystone and then to the sky-facing opening. All three directions met.

A thin glimmer slid across the wet stone. It moved in a narrow line, brightened near the pale patch, and slipped toward the cave mouth. Outside, the familiar signal flashed once through the mist.

Ada held her breath—not from fear, but from careful attention. The light was not coming from the patch itself. It was a reflection, and reflections needed a bright thing somewhere else. Yet the mist covered the far end of the line, and the cave wall hid the angle beyond the opening.

She marked the time and drew the glimmer’s path. Then she checked the water again. It had risen to the notch, so Ada stepped back along the wave marks and left the cave before the marked stones disappeared beneath the tide.

On the cliff path, she studied her notes. The waystone gave the direction. The notch gave the safe moment. The opening and wet patch carried the changing light. But what reflected it remained hidden.

“Tomorrow,” Ada decided, “I will arrive just before the alignment and follow every clue together.”

Ada returned the next evening with her notebook, tide-clock, chalk, and four careful sketches. She reached the lower waystone before the water began to rise. Its wet seaward streak pointed toward the cave, just as it had before.

She followed the wave-shaped marks, counting them as she went. Before the tide reached the notch, Ada stepped inside and settled beside the smooth pale patch. From there, she could see the narrow opening aimed at the gold evening sky.

“Waystone for direction,” she whispered. “Wave marks for the path. Notch for the right height. Opening for the light.”

She checked the tide-clock. The water crept over the lower stones, but it had not yet reached the scratched line. Ada waited, watching the opening and the damp patch together.

When the water touched the notch, a bright strip of evening light slipped through the opening. It landed on something set into the far wall: a small, flat tile of old sea glass, green-blue and smooth from years of water and wind.

The tile caught the sunlight and sent it across the damp pale patch. The wet surface carried the glow onward in a narrow silver path. It traveled over the stones, through the cave mouth, and out into the mist.

Outside, the familiar signal flashed.

Ada smiled and quickly drew the tile, the opening, and the path of reflected light. Then she watched for the next flash. It came when the sunlight struck the sea glass again, not before and not after. The pattern repeated exactly.

“The tile is the source of the reflection,” Ada said. “But it needs every other clue. The waystone shows which cave to follow. The wave marks lead to the tile. The tide notch tells when the wet stone will shine. The opening lets in the evening light.”

She touched the edge of the sea-glass tile without moving it. It was not glowing by itself. It was simply catching and returning the light at the right moment.

Ada made one last note: SIGNAL = EVENING LIGHT + SEA GLASS + DAMP STONE + TIDE HEIGHT.

Then the water began to cover the lowest marked stone. Ada tucked her notebook safely away and followed the wave marks back out. She crossed the lower path before the tide rose farther, pausing only to see the signal fade as the sun moved behind the mist.

On the cliff path, she looked back at the calm cave. The mystery was no longer a guess. It was a pattern she had tested, observed, and understood.

Ada climbed the cliff path while the last light rested softly on the mist. Below, the cave mouth looked calm and ordinary again. The tide moved in its steady rhythm, covering the lowest stones exactly as her notes had predicted.

At a flat place beside the path, Ada opened her notebook for one final drawing. She redrew the whole route in order: the lower waystone pointed toward the cave; the wave-shaped marks led inside; the tide notch showed when it was safe to enter; the sky-facing opening let in the evening light; and the sea-glass tile sent that light across the damp stone.

She added the times beside the tide mark and drew a small arrow showing where the signal flashed outside.

“Patient looking made the pattern,” Ada said. “No single clue could tell me everything. I needed the direction, the tide, the opening, and the tile together.”

She compared the drawing with the coast once more. Nothing had changed except the colors of the evening. The cave was peaceful. The wet stones shone gently, and the sea-glass tile was hidden safely inside, waiting for the next proper alignment. Ada closed her notebook, pressed its pages flat, and tucked it securely away.

Before leaving, she walked to the final waystone at the edge of the explored path. Its familiar face held the marks she already knew. Then Ada noticed something faint near its lower edge: a thin line scratched into the stone, pointing beyond the cliffs where the path became pale with mist.

She knelt to look more closely. The mark was real, but weather-worn. It did not match the directions she had followed, and she could not yet tell where it led.

Ada did not guess.

Instead, she copied the small line into a fresh corner of her notebook and wrote, “Examine another day—in clear light and with the tide checked first.”

Then she followed the known path home as evening settled quietly over the coast, leaving the faint mark waiting for a careful explorer another day.
