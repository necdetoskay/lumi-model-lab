The Missing Pattern

At nine o’clock, Deniz pushed open the glass door of the Civic Discovery Room. Deniz was ten, and today the room supervisor had trusted Deniz to check the Pattern Path station before the afternoon visitors arrived.

“May I handle the cards and the tools?” Deniz asked Ms. Vale, who was arranging pencils at the welcome desk.

“Yes,” said Ms. Vale. “Keep everything with the station, and tell me if anything is missing.”

Deniz hurried to the long table. The Pattern Path board had six square spaces, but one space was empty. Five cards lay in a row beside it. Each card had a colored corner, a small map symbol, and a station number.

“Oh,” Deniz murmured. “That explains why the path won’t work.”

Without the sixth card, visitors could not follow the activity from one station to the next. Deniz’s job was to find the missing card and make the station usable before the visitors came.

The empty space sat between a card with a red triangle in its corner and a card with a red triangle too. That made one idea seem easy: perhaps the missing card also had a red triangle. But the station numbers did not look like a simple row. The map symbols changed from card to card—a park, a road, a bridge, and something that looked like a bus stop.

“Maybe the corners make the pattern,” Deniz said. “Or maybe the map symbols do.”

Deniz looked around for things that could help test the ideas. A large neighborhood map covered one wall. Nearby, an object shelf held labeled models of places in the neighborhood. A daily schedule was posted beside a digital timer, with a ten-minute pause marked at 3:20. Under the table stood a normal tool drawer containing rulers, tape, and magnifiers. Small labels named each station and reminded visitors where to return the objects.

Deniz picked up a pencil and a clean observation sheet, but did not move any cards yet. First, Deniz drew the five visible cards in order, copying their corners, symbols, and numbers.

The red triangles seemed to point one way. The map symbols seemed to point another.

Deniz tapped the pencil against the sheet. “I need to check, not just choose the pattern that looks nicest.”

Deniz studied the two red triangles again. They sat on either side of the empty space like matching bookends.

“Then the missing card probably has a red triangle too,” Deniz said. “That is my first guess.”

Deniz wrote it on the observation sheet instead of placing a card that was not there. Under the guess, Deniz added: Check the other clues.

The ruler lay beside the board. Deniz used it to draw a straight line beneath the station numbers. The visible cards read 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6. The empty space was clearly between 2 and 4.

“So the missing card should be station 3,” Deniz said. “That part fits the space. But does the red triangle tell me anything else?”

Deniz copied the corner colors into a second row. The red triangles were easy to notice, but the colors did not make a complete order. One color appeared again later, while another changed without warning. The corners could describe the cards, but they did not explain the path.

Next, Deniz copied the small map symbols. The first card showed a park. The next showed a road. Farther along were a bus stop and another neighborhood place. Deniz checked the large wall map and traced the symbols with one finger, careful not to smudge the observation sheet.

The symbols followed the stops on the neighborhood route. Between the road at station 2 and the next marked stop, the map showed a bridge. The empty place on the board was station 3.

Deniz drew a small bridge in the blank row and circled it. Then Deniz compared the result with the red-triangle guess.

“If I use the corners, I just notice that two cards look alike,” Deniz said. “If I use the map symbols and station numbers, I can predict both the place and the symbol.”

That was a better test. The red-triangle idea had been reasonable, but it had not survived the evidence.

Deniz did not move the cards. Instead, the observation sheet now showed:

Station 3 — bridge symbol.

Deniz looked toward the labeled object shelf. Several small models rested there, each beside a station label. If the board was pointing to a bridge and station three, the next clue might be among those objects.

Deniz picked up the sheet and ruler, leaving every card exactly where it was. “I’ll check station three first,” Deniz said, and walked toward the shelf.

Deniz reached the object shelf and found the station labels taped along its front. A small bridge model stood behind the label marked STATION 3. It was made of painted wood, with two short sides and a road running over the top. Deniz had seen it during earlier visits, but today it looked different because of the clue on the observation sheet.

“Bridge. Station three,” Deniz whispered. “That is what the board predicted.”

Deniz checked the model’s label. Below the words STATION 3, a sentence was printed in smaller letters:

FOLLOW THE SCHEDULE’S PAUSE.

Deniz read it twice. The label did not say where the missing card was. It gave another step to test.

“First, the board led me here,” Deniz said, tapping the circled bridge on the sheet. “Now the bridge tells me to look at the schedule. So I should not just choose a place because it sounds right.”

The daily schedule hung on the wall a few steps away. It listed the room’s activities in rows: map practice, object sorting, a short pause, and the afternoon visitor session. Deniz ran a finger down the times until finding the pause.

3:20 — TEN-MINUTE PAUSE

Deniz looked back at the bridge model, then at the sheet.

“The bridge clue and the schedule agree on three-twenty,” Deniz said. “The station number is three, and the useful time is 3:20. That is more than one clue pointing in the same direction.”

The red-triangle idea had only come from two cards looking alike. It had not explained a station, a map stop, or a time. Deniz drew a box around 3:20 on the observation sheet and wrote: bridge label → schedule pause.

Near the schedule, the digital timer showed 3:20. Its numbers were still, as if someone had paused it during the ten-minute break. Deniz leaned closer but did not press any buttons. The timer sat above the shallow tool drawer, where Ms. Vale kept rulers, tape, and magnifiers for station work.

“Maybe the pause is the next place to check,” Deniz said. “But I need to use the tools carefully and leave the timer alone unless Ms. Vale says otherwise.”

Deniz glanced toward the Pattern Path board. The five cards remained in their places, and the empty space still waited between stations 2 and 4. The bridge-and-three result had not found the missing card yet, but it had narrowed the search without a guess.

Holding the observation sheet, Deniz walked toward the paused timer and the nearby tool drawer. The next clue, if there was one, would have to fit the board, the bridge, and the schedule together.

Deniz stopped beside the digital timer. The numbers still read 3:20, just as they had on the schedule. Below it, the shallow tool drawer held the rulers, tape, and magnifiers used for station work.

Deniz looked toward Ms. Vale, who was sorting papers at the welcome desk. “I’m checking the pause clue and the tool drawer,” Deniz called. “May I open it?”

“Go ahead,” Ms. Vale answered. “Keep the tools in their places.”

Deniz pulled the drawer open only as far as needed. A ruler lay on top, then a roll of blue tape and two magnifiers. Nothing looked like a card. Deniz did not dump the drawer onto the table. Instead, Deniz used the ruler to lift the back edge of a folded cloth beneath the tools.

Something white showed behind the paused timer’s base, where the drawer met the wall.

“An envelope,” Deniz said.

The envelope was tucked in the back of the drawer, not hidden inside the timer. Its front had three words written in neat block letters:

CHECK BEFORE PLACING.

Deniz carried it to the Pattern Path table and opened it carefully. Inside was a stiff card with a slightly worn corner. The card showed a blue bridge, the number 3, and a thin route line curving from one edge to the other.

Deniz held it beside the empty space on the board. The bridge and station number matched the result on the observation sheet. The route line also looked like the line drawn on part of the neighborhood map.

But the worn corner made the card difficult to place. It could slide into the empty space between stations 2 and 4, where the board had been waiting. It could also fit the neighboring space below the route sequence. Both spaces had a corner shape that seemed close enough.

Deniz tried neither one.

“Check before placing,” Deniz read aloud. “That means the card is not ready just because it has the right bridge and number.”

Deniz turned the card over. The back held no answer, only a pale square where an old label had been removed. The route line on the front stopped at the card’s edge, so it might continue onto the map—or onto another card.

Deniz placed the card beside the board instead of forcing it into either space. Then Deniz set the ruler next to its worn corner and opened the observation sheet.

“The board led to the bridge,” Deniz said, touching the circled symbol. “The bridge led to station three. Station three led to the schedule’s pause. The pause led here. All of that found the card, but it still has to tell me where it belongs.”

The timer continued to show 3:20. The empty board space waited. So did the map, with its route lines and labels.

Deniz picked up the card and observation sheet. The next test would have to use all three: the board, the card, and the map.

Deniz carried the card to the neighborhood map and laid the observation sheet beside it. First came the board. The cards on either side of the empty space showed stations 2 and 4. The card in Deniz’s hand showed 3, just as the station-three label and the bridge model had promised.

Deniz lined up the card’s route edge with the board. The worn corner slid neatly against the corner of the space between 2 and 4. In the lower space, the corner looked close, but the route line turned the wrong way and the station numbers would no longer run in order.

Deniz checked again with the ruler. “This space fits the corner,” Deniz said. “It also keeps 2, 3, and 4 together. The bridge symbol matches the board pattern, and the schedule’s pause led me to this card. The other space only fits if I ignore some of the clues.”

Deniz placed the card in the space between stations 2 and 4, but did not press it down yet. The route line on the card continued toward the map. Deniz followed it with one finger, passing the bridge symbol and station three. Near the edge of the map, the line ended at a small printed label.

STAIRS TO THE NORTH DOOR.

Deniz frowned. The map showed a staircase there, but the room’s neighborhood model had a blue ramp beside that same building. Deniz walked over to the model, then checked the real hallway through the open doorway. A ramp sign stood farther along the hall, while the map’s stairs label looked faded and old.

“So the card belongs here,” Deniz said, looking back at the board, “but its route label is not ready to use. The card’s line matches the map, yet the map tells visitors to use stairs. The ramp is the clear route now.”

Deniz did not peel off the old label or move the card without asking. Instead, Deniz took a piece of blue tape and a blank strip from the tool drawer, then brought them to Ms. Vale at the welcome desk.

“May I make a temporary direction sign?” Deniz asked. “I tested the card in the station-three space. Its corner, bridge, numbers, schedule clue, and map line all agree. But the map still says stairs. The ramp is the route we should show visitors.”

Ms. Vale came to look. Deniz pointed to each piece of evidence: the bridge model’s station label, the boxed 3:20 pause, the card’s worn corner, and the map line. Ms. Vale compared the faded stairs label with the ramp in the hall.

“You’re right to stop before changing anything,” she said. “Make the sign, and I’ll report the outdated label for replacement.”

Deniz wrote RAMP THIS WAY in large letters, added a blue arrow, and placed the sign beside the route display with tape. Then Deniz pressed the card firmly into the space between 2 and 4. The board’s symbols now ran in a complete pattern, and the arrow pointed visitors toward the tested route.

The timer still showed 3:20, but the missing card was no longer missing from the activity. Deniz set the observation sheet beside the board for Ms. Vale to check. The afternoon visitors would have a working pattern—and a route that matched the building as it was now.

The first visitors arrived just before the afternoon session began. Two children stopped at the Pattern Path table while their grandfather looked toward the hallway.

Ms. Vale stood beside Deniz and checked the observation sheet. She traced the notes from the board to the bridge model, then to the 3:20 pause and the card’s worn corner. Finally, she followed the route line to the map.

“Every step agrees,” she said. “The card belongs between stations 2 and 4, and the blue sign gives the current route. I’ll send the old stairs label to maintenance so it can be replaced.”

Deniz glanced at the card. It was pressed firmly into place. The bridge symbol connected to station 3, and the numbers ran smoothly from 2 to 4. Beside the route display, the sign still pointed toward the ramp.

Ms. Vale turned to the visitors. “Would you like to test the pattern?”

The younger child studied the board. “The corners look like they might repeat,” she said, pointing at two red triangles.

“That was my first idea too,” Deniz replied. “Try checking the station numbers.”

The children leaned closer. One used a finger to follow the numbers. “Two, three, four!”

“And what symbol is at three?” Deniz asked.

“The bridge.” The child found the matching symbol on the card. “So this card goes here.”

“Now test the route,” said Ms. Vale.

The visitors followed the line across the neighborhood map. At the old stairs label, the grandfather paused. Deniz pointed to the blue sign beside the display.

“The map label is old,” Deniz explained. “We checked the building, and the ramp is the route visitors can use now.”

The group followed the arrow toward the hallway. From the table, Deniz could see the ramp sign standing clearly ahead. No one had to guess which way to go.

More visitors gathered around the board. Each group tried the station numbers, matched the bridge, and checked the route before moving on. When someone reached for the red-triangle idea, Deniz did not snatch the card away. Instead, Deniz handed over the ruler.

“Let’s see what the spaces actually do,” Deniz said.

The children measured, compared, and corrected themselves. The board stayed complete, the card stayed in its tested place, and the blue arrow continued to point toward the ramp.

Deniz felt a warm, quiet pride. The first guess had been quick, but the checked answer helped real people choose where to walk. At the welcome desk, Ms. Vale marked the old label for replacement. Then she returned to help the next group test the pattern.
