The Space Between Words

Every Tuesday after school, Deniz knew exactly where to go in the community center. Past the front desk, past the rack of bright umbrellas, and into the after-school room, where the old heater clicked and the snack cupboard smelled like cinnamon crackers.

Lale was already at the long table. She was ten too, and she knew the room’s routines as well as Deniz did. They had spent many afternoons making the neighborhood-story display together. They knew which drawer held the colored tape, which printer needed a gentle tap, and which corner of the display board leaned slightly forward.

“Look,” Lale said, spreading three photographs across the table. “This one has the baker’s window, but the reflection makes it look like there are two moons.”

Deniz leaned closer. “That one. Definitely.”

Beside it, they placed a poem they had found in the center’s folder of neighborhood writing. It was about bicycle bells, wet pavement, and the sound of Mrs. Aras sweeping her shopfront in the morning. The last line was still blank, marked with a pencil star.

They pinned the photograph to the blue display board and tested two places for the poem. Then Lale began writing a label on a strip of cream card.

“Name, title, and where we found the story,” she said. “We can finish it together.”

Deniz nodded, but kept looking at the poem’s last line. One word felt wrong. The poem needed a quiet ending, not a loud one.

“Wait,” Deniz said, touching the edge of the paper. “Don’t show it yet, okay? I want to check the last line.”

Lale paused with the marker above the label. “Okay. I’ll wait.”

Deniz slid the poem into a blue folder so nobody would bend it. The folder went beside the label on the table. The photograph stayed safely under a paperweight shaped like a yellow bus.

At that moment, the center’s bell rang. Deniz’s art club was starting in the next room.

“I’ll be back tomorrow,” Deniz said, grabbing a backpack.

“Tomorrow,” Lale agreed.

Deniz hurried away, pleased that their display was almost ready. It did not occur to Deniz to explain whether Lale should wait before showing the poem, writing the names, or changing anything on the board. Deniz believed Lale understood.

Behind them, the blue folder rested on the table, holding the unfinished poem and the question neither friend had thought to ask.

The next afternoon, Deniz hurried into the after-school room with a sharpened pencil and two ideas for the poem’s last line.

Then Deniz stopped.

The blue folder was not on the long table. It sat on the community-center coordinator’s desk near the sign-in book. The display board looked different too. The photograph of the baker’s window was still pinned beneath the yellow-bus paperweight, but the cream label now said only:

NEIGHBORHOOD STORY
Poem and photograph

Deniz’s name was gone. So was Lale’s.

Before Deniz could move closer, Lale came from behind the storage shelves. She held a sheet covered with brown paper and both hands pressed tightly around it.

The coordinator glanced up. “Lale, you can put that with the other materials.”

Lale looked toward the door. “Deniz shouldn’t see it yet,” she said.

Her voice was low, but the room was quiet enough for Deniz to hear every word.

Lale noticed Deniz. Her eyebrows jumped. Then she turned and stepped toward the hallway, carrying the covered sheet against her chest.

For one second, Deniz could not even remember the two last lines in the pencil case.

The blue folder on the coordinator’s desk. The changed label. The covered poem. Lale’s words.

A hot, pinching feeling spread from Deniz’s stomach up to their face. Deniz knew its name: hurt. Embarrassment came with it, prickly and bright, as if everyone in the room could see that Deniz had been left out.

Maybe Lale had shown the poem to the coordinator after all. Maybe she had decided Deniz’s name did not belong on the display. Maybe “Don’t show it yet” had sounded silly, or maybe Lale simply did not trust Deniz with the work anymore.

Deniz stared at the blank space beneath the title. Those thoughts felt solid, but Deniz caught them before they became facts. They were guesses. The only things Deniz truly knew were that the folder had moved, the names were missing, and Lale had said Deniz should not see the covered sheet.

“Are you staying for the craft group?” the coordinator asked gently.

Deniz looked at the shelves, the familiar snack cupboard, and the table where Lale had once spread out three photographs. This was still the community center. Lale was still somewhere nearby. Walking out forever would not explain anything.

“I’m going to put my things away,” Deniz said.

Instead, Deniz stepped into the hallway and sat on the bench beneath the bulletin board. The hurt was still there, but so was a small, stubborn wish to learn what had happened before saying something that could not be taken back.

After a few minutes, Deniz stood up. Sitting on the bench had not made the guesses disappear, but it had made room for another thought: if the poem was still in the room, Deniz could look at what had actually changed.

The display table stood beside the blue board. A stack of photographs leaned against the wall, and the yellow-bus paperweight waited near the empty spot where the poem had been. Deniz reached for it, hoping to see whether the poem was underneath.

A sour, sweet smell rose from the table.

Deniz froze. Beneath the paperweight, the corner of a plastic sleeve stuck out. Brown juice had dried across it in a wide, sticky stripe. The poem showed through the plastic, its pencil lines blurred at the edge. A torn piece of sleeve lay on the floor nearby, curled like a thin clear leaf.

“Oh,” Deniz whispered.

The stain had not been there yesterday. Or maybe it had been hidden under the paperweight. Deniz remembered Lale carrying the covered sheet against her chest. Covering the poem suddenly made sense. If the juice had soaked through, Lale might have been trying to keep the paper from tearing or smearing more.

But the missing names still sat on the label like two little erased doors.

The coordinator came over with a cloth. “I wondered where that smell was coming from.”

“Did Lale ask for a new sleeve?” Deniz asked.

“She did,” the coordinator said. “She brought me the old one and asked if we had a clean replacement. I gave her one from the supply drawer.” The coordinator picked up the torn plastic. “I don’t know why the name label changed, though. Lale didn’t explain that part to me.”

So even the coordinator did not know everything.

Deniz glanced toward the storage shelves. Lale was there, holding a clear sleeve that shone in the afternoon light. She saw Deniz looking and stopped. For a moment, neither of them spoke. Then Lale turned sideways, as if deciding whether to come closer, and disappeared behind the shelves.

Deniz’s chest tightened again. The covered poem could mean protection. It could also mean Lale wanted to keep the work away. The missing names still looked like exclusion. One piece of evidence could fit more than one story, Deniz realized. That made the story harder, not easier.

Then Deniz remembered yesterday’s message, the words Deniz had left for Lale before hurrying to art club: “Wait until I check the last line.” Lale had asked, “Wait—what do you mean?” but Deniz had already been gathering the pencils. Deniz had never said whether “wait” meant the poem, the name, or the whole display.

Maybe Lale had guessed wrong. Maybe Deniz had, too.

Deniz touched the edge of the stained sleeve but did not lift the poem. The paper needed the clean one. More importantly, the guesses needed something they could not get from a table or a hallway. Deniz would have to ask Lale what she had understood, then explain why the missing name had hurt.

Deniz looked toward the shelves again. A direct conversation was the only clue left.

Deniz found the message in the community-center group chat and read it again: “Wait until I check the last line.”

The words looked different this time. Yesterday, Deniz had meant, Wait before we show the poem to anyone. But the sentence did not say that. It could also mean, Wait before you change the poem. Or wait before you show the display. Lale might have heard a much bigger instruction than Deniz had intended.

Deniz locked the phone and pulled a small notebook from a backpack pocket. Drawing usually helped when thoughts bumped into one another. On one page, Deniz sketched the blue folder. Under it, Deniz wrote:

The folder was on the coordinator’s desk.
The label had no names.
Lale said I shouldn’t see the covered sheet yet.

Deniz stared at the three sentences. They were things Deniz had actually seen or heard. On the next line, Deniz wrote one feeling:

I felt left out and embarrassed.

A fourth thought tried to squeeze underneath: Lale doesn’t trust me anymore. Deniz almost wrote it down, then stopped. That was a guess, not an observation. The same was true of Lale showed the poem to someone else and Lale wanted to take over the display.

Deniz drew a crooked line between the facts and the guesses. The line did not make the hurt disappear, but it kept the guesses from pretending to be facts.

Lale appeared near the storage shelves, holding the clean sleeve. She looked at the notebook, then at Deniz. “Are you writing about me?”

“About what happened,” Deniz said. The answer came out more sharply than intended. Deniz took a breath. “I want to ask you about it without calling you names.”

Lale’s fingers tightened around the sleeve. “Are you going to say I was being mean?”

“No. I don’t know what you meant yet.” Deniz glanced at the display table. “I want to say what I saw, what I felt, and what I guessed. Then I want to hear your side.”

Lale looked toward the doorway, where voices from the art club drifted past. “Right now?”

“If you’re ready. Beside the display table?”

Lale nodded, though she did not move at once. Deniz could see that she was nervous too. Maybe Lale expected blame. Maybe Deniz did as well.

Deniz practiced the question silently, then said it aloud. “I felt left out when I saw my name missing. What did you understand?”

Lale looked down at the clean sleeve. “Okay,” she said. “But don’t decide what I meant before I explain.”

“I’ll try not to,” Deniz said.

They walked to the display table together. The stained sleeve waited there between them, and the unfinished label still had two blank spaces where their names had been. Neither child touched anything yet.

Neither child touched the display.

Deniz held the notebook against the table. “I saw the blue folder on Ms. Nuri’s desk. Then I saw the label without either of our names. You said I shouldn’t see the covered sheet yet.” Deniz’s stomach tightened again, but the words stayed steady. “I felt left out and embarrassed. I guessed you had shown the poem to someone else, or that you didn’t want me working on it anymore. What did you understand?”

Lale looked at the stained sleeve. “I thought you wanted the poem kept private.”

Deniz blinked. “Why?”

“Because you wrote, ‘Wait until I check the last line.’ Then you left so quickly. I called after you, but you were already going toward art club.” Lale rubbed one corner of the clean sleeve. “I thought maybe you didn’t want anyone seeing the unfinished poem. I put it in the blue folder so nobody would look at it.”

Deniz glanced at the brown mark across the poem’s corner. “But the stain—”

“That happened when I was carrying my juice.” Lale lifted the old sleeve carefully. “The cup tipped when someone bumped the table. I wiped the poem, but the plastic tore. I covered the sheet because I didn’t want the stain to spread or the paper to get bent.”

She looked at Deniz. “I took the names off while I was fixing the label. I thought if the poem was private for now, the names should be private too. And when you came back, I said you shouldn’t see it yet.” Her face tightened. “That sounded secretive, didn’t it?”

“A little,” Deniz said. “It sounded like you were hiding something from me.”

“I was hiding the stain,” Lale said. “Not the poem from you. I should have said that.”

Deniz looked down at the message in the notebook. “I should have written, ‘Please don’t show the poem to anyone until I check the last line.’ I didn’t say what ‘it’ meant. And I left without explaining.” Deniz took a breath. “I’m sorry I made the request confusing, and then left you to guess why.”

Lale’s shoulders lowered. “I’m sorry I moved everything without asking you. I could have told you about the stain.”

For a moment, the room sounded ordinary again: chairs scraping in the art club and the soft hum of the old radiator.

“Can we fix it together?” Deniz asked.

Lale nodded. “Together.”

They slid the poem out of the torn sleeve. The dried juice had left a pale brown crescent, but the words were still clear. Lale held the paper flat while Deniz eased it into the clean sleeve. This time, neither hurried.

Then Deniz found the label. The blank spaces looked strangely large. With a fresh marker, Deniz wrote “Poem by Deniz and Lale” across the top. Lale checked the spelling of both names, then pressed the label onto the board.

The last line still waited beneath its pencil star. Deniz read the first half aloud. Lale tried three endings. The third one fit the rhythm, but Deniz changed one word, and Lale agreed. Together they wrote the final line in dark blue ink.

The poem, photograph, and names stood side by side again. The blue folder rested underneath the table, empty now except for the old sleeve.

Deniz looked at the restored display. The stain had left a faint mark, and the old sleeve was still torn, but the poem was protected and both names were there. Lale stood beside Deniz, close enough that their shoulders touched.

Neither of them had guessed perfectly. This time, they had asked, listened, and checked what they could see. The display was repaired, and so was the space between them.

By evening, the community center had filled with familiar sounds. Chairs scraped across the floor, the kettle clicked in the small kitchen, and people from the neighborhood wandered through the open room to see the displays.

Deniz and Lale stood together beside the blue board. The photograph of the baker’s window caught the warm light from the hallway. Beneath it, their poem rested safely in its clean sleeve, with both names written across the label.

Ms. Nuri stopped in front of it. “This looks ready,” she said. “Would you like to tell everyone about it?”

Deniz felt a tiny flutter in their stomach, but Lale was already smiling.

“The poem is about the smells and sounds near the bakery,” Lale told a group of neighbors. “We wrote it together.”

“And the last line took a while,” Deniz added. “It wasn’t finished when we first brought it here.”

Lale glanced at Deniz. “There was also a juice stain.”

“A very dramatic juice stain,” Deniz said.

The neighbors laughed, and Lale explained how the old sleeve had torn. Deniz explained that the missing names had made the situation confusing. They did not tell every detail, but they did not pretend the display had appeared perfectly. Ms. Nuri listened, then gave the restored sleeve an approving nod.

When the group moved on, Lale noticed a small card beside the photograph. “The card still says, ‘Final line coming soon.’”

Deniz looked at the poem. “That needs one last change.”

Lale picked up a marker, then paused. “Wait. Do you mean we should change the card now, or write it together after the open room?”

Deniz smiled. “Good question. Let’s change only the card now. The poem stays as it is. Ms. Nuri and the visitors can see the card, but we’ll check the wording together before we put it up.”

“When?” Lale asked.

“After everyone leaves? We can meet here at five tomorrow and read it once before sticking it down.”

Lale nodded. “So the card can change now, and the final version waits until tomorrow at five. Both of us check it.”

“Exactly.”

They crossed out the old words and wrote, “Last line completed by Deniz and Lale.” Lale held the card flat while Deniz checked each letter. Then they placed it beside the photograph, leaving the poem untouched.

Across the room, Mr. Cem, who ran the evening art table, called, “That display is getting popular!”

Deniz looked at Lale. Nothing was covered. Nothing was missing. The words on the card matched what they had actually done, and the plan for tomorrow was clear.

The old tight feeling in Deniz’s chest loosened. Lale bumped Deniz’s shoulder gently, and Deniz bumped back. Together, they turned to welcome the next visitor.
