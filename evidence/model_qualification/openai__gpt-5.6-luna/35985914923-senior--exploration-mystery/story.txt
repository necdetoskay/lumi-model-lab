The Quiet Signal

On Thursday evening, 14 September, Deniz walked through the public garden toward the library. At seventy, Deniz still preferred to walk whenever the weather allowed. The sea was calm beyond the low stone wall, and the air carried the clean smell of salt and damp earth. In one hand, Deniz held a walking stick—not because it was needed, but because it was useful on uneven paths. In the other was a small notebook.

Near the rose beds, a faint white flash appeared on the far garden wall.

Deniz stopped.

After about half a minute, a second flash crossed the same patch of pale stone. A third followed at nearly the same interval. Each one was brief, no brighter than sunlight caught on a window. Then the wall was still again.

Deniz did not decide what it meant. A light might come from a passing car, a window, or some piece of equipment. It might also be nothing more than a reflection seen at an unusual angle.

First, Deniz examined the wall. The stones were dry and unmarked. There was no lamp, wire, or notice attached to it. Deniz followed the nearby paths, looking between the clipped shrubs and along the iron fence. No one stood there holding a light. No movement suggested an active warning.

The old observation point stood above the garden on a rocky rise. Deniz walked to its entrance and looked up at the quiet structure. Its railings were empty. Beyond it, the roof of the town workshop showed dark against the brightening sky. The workshop was closed, and no light shone from its windows.

From the observation point, Deniz turned back toward the garden wall. The angle was different, and the flashes did not return. That was worth noting too.

Deniz sat on a bench beneath an olive tree and opened the notebook. The first line read:

“14 September, 7:18 p.m. Three faint white flashes on the garden wall. About thirty seconds apart.”

Below it, Deniz added:

“Weather: calm, clear, mild breeze from the sea. Light: low sun behind the observation point. Position: west path, beside the rose beds.”

For a moment, Deniz considered writing, “Possible warning.” Instead, Deniz crossed out the phrase before finishing it. There was not enough evidence for that conclusion.

The library would open for another hour. Its local history shelves might contain plans or photographs of the observation point. The workshop could explain some ordinary equipment, if equipment turned out to be involved. For now, though, the flashes remained only a repeating fact.

Deniz closed the notebook and looked once more at the quiet wall.

“What makes you appear?” Deniz murmured—not expecting an answer.

Then Deniz walked toward the library, carrying the question and the careful record together.

The next evening, Deniz returned to the public garden with the notebook in a coat pocket. The question from the night before had not become an answer. Three flashes could still mean a lamp, a moving reflection, or some piece of equipment working badly. Deniz wanted observations before opinions.

At 7:12 p.m., Deniz stood on the west path beside the rose beds, the position recorded in the notebook. The sea breeze was light, and the branches of a tall tamarisk tree stirred above the wall. A flash touched the familiar patch of pale stone. Thirty seconds later, another appeared, followed by a third.

Deniz wrote the times carefully: 7:12, 7:12:30, 7:13. Then came a fourth flash, though it was partly hidden by the tree's moving leaves.

“That is different,” Deniz said. “Not the wall. The view of the wall.”

Deniz moved to a bench near the fountain, about twenty paces east of the first position. From there, the tree stood directly between the garden path and the flashing patch. For several minutes nothing appeared. The branches swayed, but the wall remained blank.

At 7:20, Deniz walked to the gravel path near the garden gate, west of the rose beds. The tree no longer blocked the view. Almost at once, three brief flashes crossed the same part of the wall, still separated by about half a minute. They did not strike the fountain, the gate, or any other stone.

Deniz noted the change in position rather than treating it as a failure. The flashes had not moved. Visibility had changed.

A thin cloud then passed over the low sun. The wall lost its warm brightness, and the flashes stopped. Deniz waited. The cloud's edge drifted away, but another grey layer followed it. No flash appeared. The breeze continued to lift the tamarisk branches, yet the regular marks of light were gone.

At 7:36, the cloud thinned. Sunlight returned in a narrow, golden band, and the branches began to turn and settle in small, uneven movements. A flash appeared. After thirty seconds, a second crossed the wall. A third followed.

This time Deniz watched the direction of the light instead of only its destination. The bright line seemed to come from above the garden, passing near the dark roof of the town workshop and continuing toward the old observation point on the rocky rise. The roof and the point lay along the changing line, but that alignment proved only that they might be involved. It did not prove which, if either, was responsible.

Deniz sat beneath the tamarisk and opened the notebook. The new entry read:

“14 September, 7:12 p.m. West path: three flashes, about thirty seconds apart; fourth partly hidden by branches. Fountain bench: no flashes; tree blocked the wall. Garden gate: same wall area, three flashes. 7:20–7:36: light breeze moved branches. Thin cloud covered low sun; flashes stopped. At 7:36, flashes returned when sunlight reached the garden. Possible line between workshop roof and observation point. Source unknown.”

Deniz read the last two words twice. Source unknown. They were more useful than a confident guess.

The breeze could be moving something. The low sun could be supplying the light. The tree and the different viewpoints explained why the flashes appeared or vanished. Still, no one had seen the reflecting surface itself.

Deniz closed the notebook and looked toward the library. Old plans or photographs might show what stood at the observation point, while the workshop could account for ordinary materials or equipment. The pattern had become clearer, but its meaning had not. With the notebook in hand, Deniz set off to find evidence that could separate possibility from fact.

The library was quiet except for the soft turning of pages and the low hum of the heating system. Deniz carried the notebook to the local archive table, where folders of town plans stood in shallow drawers. The flashes had become a pattern, but a pattern was not yet an explanation.

Deniz searched the index under “observation point,” “coastal signals,” and “garden wall.” The last heading led to photographs of paths and buildings. The first two led to a thin folder marked “Harbor Watch, 1898–1936.”

Inside was a black-and-white photograph mounted on stiff paper. The old observation point stood on the rocky rise, its stone platform clear against the sky. Beside the railing was a round, bright disk fixed to a short frame. A caption beneath the photograph read:

“Polished signaling mirror, used to send simple daylight signals toward the harbor and lower town.”

Deniz leaned closer. The disk was not large, perhaps the width of a dinner plate, but its surface was pale enough to shine even in the old photograph. A second note explained that watchkeepers had turned the mirror by hand. One flash meant that a boat had been sighted. Two or three flashes could mark a change in watch or weather conditions. The signals were practical, not secret, and were recorded in a daily log.

Deniz read the note again. The old mirror gave the flashes a possible history, but history did not prove that the mirror still existed, or that anyone was using it now. It also did not explain why the present flashes appeared in sets of three at roughly thirty-second intervals.

Another sheet in the folder was a folded plan of the town. It showed the observation point, the workshop, the public garden, and the library. A dotted line ran from the mirror's frame toward the lower town. It ended near the garden wall, close to the patch where Deniz had seen the light.

Deniz placed the notebook beside the plan. The garden positions from the previous evening were written in the margin: west path, fountain bench, garden gate. Using a pencil, Deniz marked each position with a small cross. Then Deniz followed the dotted line with a ruler.

From the observation point, the line passed over the workshop roof before reaching the garden. From the west path and the gate, the marked wall area lay in the same direction. From the fountain bench, the tamarisk tree would stand between the line and the wall. That fitted the observations, but a good fit was still only a good fit.

Deniz added a note beneath the crosses: “Archive direction agrees with observed target. Other reflective surface remains possible.”

The possibilities now had clearer edges. The old mirror might still be at the observation point. Another bright surface on the workshop roof might catch the low sun. A window, a piece of metal, or even an unrelated light could still account for the flashes. The breeze might be turning branches, or it might be moving a reflective object somewhere along the line.

Deniz looked again at the photograph. The mirror's historic purpose made the flashes seem less mysterious, but it did not make them intentional. No present-day message had been found. No record said that the old signaling system was still in use. The archive offered context and a direction, not a conclusion.

In the notebook, Deniz wrote the date and a fresh entry:

“Library archive: photograph shows polished signaling mirror at old observation point. Plan shows former light direction crossing workshop roof and ending near garden-wall target. Mirror once carried simple daylight signals. Present flashes still unconfirmed. Need to inspect the point and compare the actual arrangement.”

Deniz closed the folder carefully and returned it to its place. Daylight would be best for examining the rocky rise. At the observation point, the mirror might be gone, covered, or damaged. It might also prove to be nothing more than an old record with no connection to the present light.

With the notebook under one arm, Deniz left the archive table. The question had narrowed, but it had not been answered. The next step was not to announce a signal or dismiss it. It was to look at the evidence itself.

The next morning, Deniz walked to the old observation point while the tide was low. The path across the rocky rise was firm and dry, and the daylight showed details that evening shadows had hidden.

The stone platform remained, though salt had whitened parts of its railing. Beside the railing stood the round signaling mirror from the archive photograph. It was smaller than Deniz had imagined. Its surface was clouded with a thin film of dust and salt, and its frame leaned several degrees toward the sea.

Deniz did not touch it at once. The frame rested on a short metal support, and the support ended in a pivot. A narrow gap showed between the pivot and its socket. When a breeze moved across the platform, the mirror trembled. It did not swing far, but it shifted enough to catch the sun and then lose it.

“That could account for movement,” Deniz said, “but not yet for the flashes.”

The mirror was evidence, not a conclusion. Deniz opened the notebook and drew the support, the direction of the sun, and the line toward the garden. The drawing agreed with the plan from the library, although the actual mirror was tilted more loosely than the one shown in the old photograph.

A sign on the platform warned visitors not to climb beyond the railing. Deniz respected it. There was no reason to handle a loose metal frame, and certainly no reason to approach the old wiring that ran along the base of the observation point. The question could be tested without touching either.

The workshop stood below the rise, beside the path that led toward the garden. Its side door was open for daylight, and a broad sheet of discarded cardboard rested in the materials bin. Deniz took one clean piece, along with a small hand mirror from the shelf of reusable tools. These were ordinary workshop materials, and Deniz planned to return them exactly as they had been found.

At a clear space near the workshop wall, Deniz cut no wires and moved no equipment. Instead, the cardboard became a temporary map. With a pencil, Deniz marked three places: the observation point, the workshop roof, and the patch of garden wall recorded in the notebook. A short line showed the low evening sun. Another line showed the path of reflected light.

Then Deniz set the hand mirror upright against a cardboard fold. The mirror was loose enough to turn when the breeze touched the cardboard, but not so loose that it could fall. Deniz stood at the marked position for the west path and angled the mirror toward the bright sky. A white spot appeared on the cardboard wall. When the breeze shifted the mirror a little, the spot slid away. A second small movement brought it back.

Deniz repeated the arrangement from the garden-gate mark. The spot reached the same drawn target. From the fountain-bench mark, Deniz placed another piece of cardboard where the tamarisk tree would stand. The tree's position blocked the spot, just as it had blocked the evening flashes.

The model was simple, but it gave a result that could be seen and repeated. Breeze moved the mirror. Low sunlight supplied the brightness. The viewing position determined whether the reflected spot reached the garden target or disappeared behind branches.

Deniz watched the hand mirror turn through a small arc. Three brief bright spots appeared on the marked wall area before the mirror settled. They were not perfectly timed, because cardboard could not imitate the old metal frame exactly. Still, the model predicted the important features: three short flashes, the same target, and no flash when the tree blocked the line.

Deniz wrote carefully: “Daylight model agrees with recorded path. A loose mirror could produce three brief flashes as wind turns it toward the setting sun. Model is not proof. Compare the real mirror with the real garden target.”

The last sentence mattered. A model could show what might happen; only a direct observation could show what was happening. Deniz folded the cardboard without tearing it, returned the hand mirror to the workshop shelf, and put the remaining piece back in the bin.

Before leaving, Deniz looked once more at the clouded mirror. It was a strong possibility now, but not a settled answer. At the recorded evening time, the real mirror would have to be watched from the garden. Deniz would mark the target, compare the flashes, and decide whether the town needed an alarm or a maintenance report.

For now, the notebook held a prediction. The decisive test still lay ahead.

That evening, Deniz returned to the public garden a little before 7:18. The air was mild, and a light breeze came from the sea. Above the garden wall, the sky held the last clear brightness of the day.

Deniz opened the notebook to the page marked with the garden target. The page showed three small circles, each beside a note: “same patch of wall,” “about thirty seconds,” and “breeze from the sea.” Deniz placed the page against the wall at the recorded spot and weighed its lower edge with the notebook. The paper stayed flat enough to show the circles clearly.

From there, the old observation point stood above the garden. Its mirror was too far away to show every detail, but Deniz could see the pale round surface and the dark frame around it. The workshop roof lay between the point and the garden, just as the archive plan had shown.

Deniz checked the time. It was 7:18 p.m.

Nothing happened at first. The mirror remained dull. Then a breeze crossed the rise. The loose frame shifted a little on its pivot, and a white spot appeared on the marked page.

The spot vanished.

Nearly half a minute later, it returned. A second brief brightness crossed the first circle and slipped away. Deniz watched without moving, resisting the urge to call the pattern complete too soon.

Another interval passed. The breeze touched the mirror again. A third flash landed on the page, close to the third circle.

Deniz looked from the page to the mirror and back again. The prediction from the cardboard model had been specific: three brief flashes, the same garden target, caused by a mirror turning slightly in the breeze toward the low sun. The real observation matched it.

Still, one more test was necessary. A matching pattern could be strong evidence, but Deniz wanted to check the light itself.

The notebook page remained weighted on the wall. Deniz carried the notebook up the path toward the observation point, keeping to the public side of the railing. The sign still warned visitors not to climb beyond it. From the safe side, Deniz could reach the edge of the mirror’s reflected path without touching the frame or its old wiring.

The mirror had settled into shadow for a moment. Then the breeze moved it, and a fourth bright spot appeared on the garden wall below. Deniz waited until the light faded. Holding the dark wool coat by its shoulders, Deniz raised it between the mirror and the setting sun. The coat did not touch the equipment. It simply blocked the mirror’s surface from the light.

The bright spot disappeared.

Deniz lowered the coat. The mirror caught the sun again, and a flash returned to the marked page. Deniz raised the coat once more. The flash stopped at once. Lowering it brought the reflection back.

There was no need to add a meaning that the evidence did not support. The flashes were not a warning from a person, and they were not an active emergency signal. Sunlight struck the old signaling mirror. Its loose pivot let the breeze turn it. The moving reflection reached the garden wall in three short bursts.

Deniz wrote the result in the notebook:

“7:18 p.m. Real mirror produced three brief flashes on marked target. Shielding the mirror stopped the reflection; removing the shield restored it. Source: sunlight reflected by loose historic mirror.”

The wording mattered. Deniz wrote “source” only after the shielding test, not before. The records had supplied the history. The garden observations had supplied the timing and direction. The model had supplied a prediction. The direct test had connected all three to the real mirror.

At the library, Deniz used the town maintenance number listed beside the public-building notices. The report was brief but complete. It named the old observation point, described the clouded mirror and loose pivot, and explained that the flashes were reflected sunlight rather than an emergency. Deniz requested that the pivot be secured and that the town provide a short public explanation before anyone mistook the reflections for a warning.

After sending the report, Deniz returned to the garden. The page was still resting against the wall, its three circles visible in the fading light. Deniz lifted it, folded it back into the notebook, and looked once more toward the observation point.

The mirror was no longer a question without shape. It was an old signaling device, still capable of doing what it had once been built to do, though now by accident and in need of repair. Deniz had not guessed the answer or dismissed the flashes. The answer had become clear one observation at a time.

Two days later, Deniz returned to the public garden at about 7:18 p.m. The evening had the same mild air, and a light breeze came from the sea. The tamarisk branches moved softly above the wall, their narrow leaves making a dry, whispering sound. The familiar path looked unchanged, yet Deniz noticed it differently now. Each place had become part of a careful record.

Deniz looked first toward the old observation point. The mirror was still there, but its frame had been secured. The loose pivot no longer shifted in the wind. A plain weatherproof cover rested over the polished surface, and a small sign had been fixed to the railing below it. The cover was simple and practical, just as the maintenance report had requested.

Deniz waited.

The breeze moved the branches. The workshop roof held the last sunlight. The garden wall remained evenly lit. Deniz watched the same patch where the three flashes had appeared before and compared it with the marked positions in the notebook.

No flash appeared.

Deniz checked the time and waited several minutes more. The light grew lower. The mirror stayed covered and still. The wall showed no sudden white spots, no brief signals, and no repeated pattern waiting to be interpreted. The quiet was not an empty result. It was the result the evidence had predicted.

At the library, a notice had been placed beside the entrance. It was short enough to read without stopping for long:

“NOTICE: The old observation point contains a historic signaling mirror. Its loose pivot recently reflected sunlight onto the public garden wall, creating three brief flashes. The mirror has now been secured and covered. The flashes were accidental reflections, not an emergency signal.”

Deniz read the notice twice. Its wording was plain, and that mattered. It explained what had happened without making the old mirror seem mysterious or pretending that the flashes had carried a message. Anyone who had seen them could now understand their source. The explanation also made clear why the flashes had stopped, linking the public notice to the repair rather than to a lucky change in the weather.

Inside the library, Deniz opened the notebook to the final page. The earlier entries filled several sheets: times, positions, cloud cover, wind direction, the garden target, the archive plan, and the sketch of the loose pivot. The last entry had been written after the shielding test.

Deniz added a new line beneath it:

“14 September, 7:18 p.m., after repair: no flashes observed. Mirror secured and covered. Garden quiet.”

For a moment, Deniz considered crossing out the first question—What creates the repeating flashes?—but left it where it was. The question belonged to the investigation. So did the answer that followed it.

The first observation had shown only a pattern. The garden tests had connected that pattern to sunlight, breeze, and viewpoint. The library records had supplied the mirror’s history. The daylight inspection had revealed the loose pivot. The model had predicted the path, and the direct test had proved it. Finally, the quiet wall had shown that securing the mirror changed the result. No single observation had been enough, but together they formed a clear explanation.

Deniz closed the notebook and returned it to the coat pocket. Outside, the public garden was settling into evening. The stone paths held the day’s fading warmth, and a few windows shone along the street, but none sent a moving beam across the wall. The old observation point stood above the town, no longer making accidental signals.

Deniz walked home at an unhurried pace. The uncertainty had not disappeared because someone had guessed well. It had changed because each careful observation had given the next one a firmer place. By the time the garden grew dark, the flashes were no longer a warning, a message, or a mystery. They were sunlight, a loose pivot, and an old mirror—understood at last.
