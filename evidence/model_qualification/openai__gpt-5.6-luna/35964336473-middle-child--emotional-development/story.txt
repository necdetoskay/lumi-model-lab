The Space Between Words

On Thursday afternoon, the community room smelled of cardboard, glue, and the cinnamon rolls someone had brought for the volunteers. Deniz knelt beside the word bridge, fitting a blue block under the word *proud*.

“Try this,” Deniz said, handing Samir a small notebook. “I wrote more label ideas.”

Samir took it with a grin. “You wrote twelve?”

“Fourteen.”

“That is extremely Deniz.”

Deniz smiled. Samir had been their friend since the beginning of the school year, and they usually worked well together. For the weekend display, they were building a bridge from wooden blocks. Each block would carry a word about feelings, with a short label explaining it. The bridge was supposed to show how one feeling could lead to another.

Deniz hurried to the supply shelf for silver paper. When they came back, Samir was no longer beside the bridge. He was carrying it toward the far wall, one hand under a shaky row of blocks. The notebook was tucked against his chest.

Ms. Aylin stood near the window. Samir leaned toward her and whispered, “The labels…”

Deniz stopped behind a stack of poster boards.

Samir shifted the bridge higher. Two blocks wobbled. He caught them with his elbow, still holding the notebook. Then he glanced toward the doorway, as if he needed to get somewhere quickly.

Deniz’s stomach tightened. Heat climbed from their neck to their cheeks. Their fingers curled around the silver paper until it made a loud crinkle.

Samir noticed them. “Deniz—”

“Here,” Deniz said, holding out the paper.

He reached for it, but the bridge tipped again. “Not now!”

The words landed harder than the falling blocks would have. Deniz pulled the paper back.

They saw the bridge being moved. They saw Samir holding their notebook. They heard him whisper about the labels. Those things were real. But the thought rushing through Deniz’s head felt real too: Samir disliked the labels. Maybe he thought the whole exhibit was a bad idea. Maybe he did not want Deniz working on it anymore.

Deniz set the silver paper on a table. “Fine.”

Samir turned toward them, but Deniz was already stuffing the notebook’s empty pencil into their pocket. They walked out before their face could show how embarrassed they felt, leaving the word bridge unfinished behind them.

The next afternoon, Deniz came back to the community room. The word bridge waited near the far wall, leaning slightly toward the window. Samir was not there yet.

Deniz chose a chair at the work table and pulled a scrap of poster paper close. They wrote:

WHAT HAPPENED

Samir moved the bridge.
Samir had my notebook.
He whispered, “The labels…” to Ms. Aylin.
He said, “Not now.”

Deniz stared at the list. Their stomach gave the same small twist it had given yesterday.

Under it, they wrote:

WHAT I THINK IT MEANT

He thought my labels were bad.
He wanted to change everything.
He did not want me helping.

The last sentence pressed so hard into the paper that the pencil nearly tore through it. Deniz crossed it out, then wrote it again in smaller letters.

Only the first list was certain. That was annoying. It would have been easier if the second list were certain too.

Deniz tapped the pencil against the table. “Embarrassment mixed with anger,” they muttered. Saying the feeling out loud made it less like one giant hot lump, but it did not make it disappear.

The door opened.

Samir stepped in, carrying the notebook. A few pages stuck out at odd angles. He saw Deniz and stopped.

“I brought this back,” he said.

Deniz reached for it. “Thanks.”

They took the notebook without looking at his face and set it beside the poster paper. Samir stayed there for a moment.

“Are you still making the labels?” he asked.

Deniz picked up a marker. “I’m working.”

The words came out flatter than Deniz meant them to. Samir’s eyebrows pulled together. He looked as if he had another question, but instead he nodded and moved to the other side of the room.

Deniz felt a sharp pinch of guilt, followed by anger at the guilt. Samir had started this. Probably. Maybe. Deniz bent over the labels and wrote *lonely* in large blue letters.

A few minutes later, they tried fitting the bridge pieces together. One wooden block leaned crookedly. When Deniz touched it, the whole middle row shivered.

They caught it before it fell.

The bridge had been moved. The notebook had been carried. The labels had been mentioned. Those were facts. Samir’s opinion was still a guess. So was the idea that he wanted Deniz gone.

Deniz looked across the room. Samir was quietly sorting blocks, not looking over.

They could ask him. The thought made their cheeks warm again. They could also keep guessing, which had not made anything better.

Deniz straightened the crooked piece but did not glue it. First, they needed more context. Then, maybe, they would decide what to say.

Deniz waited until Samir carried a box of small blocks to the storage corner. Then they went to Ms. Aylin, who was checking the display table.

“Can I ask something about the bridge?” Deniz said.

Ms. Aylin looked up. “Of course.”

“Why did it get moved to the far wall?” Deniz tried to keep the question plain. “I mean, did someone ask Samir to move it?”

“I did,” said Ms. Aylin. She pointed toward the window. A faint dark patch showed on the sill. “There was a leak during the rain. I didn’t want the cardboard base getting wet, so I asked him to carry the bridge away from it.”

Deniz glanced at the bridge. The crooked middle piece made more sense now. Carrying the whole thing would have been difficult, especially with its little wooden blocks balanced along the top.

“And the notebook?” Deniz asked.

“The pages were coming loose.” Ms. Aylin touched the corner of her own clipboard. “I saw some sticking out, so I asked Samir to hold it while he moved the bridge. He was trying not to drop either one.”

Deniz pictured Samir from the other day: one arm around the bridge, the notebook pressed against his side, pieces wobbling as he hurried across the room. The picture did not prove that Samir had been kind. It did prove that carrying everything had been tricky.

“Did he say anything about my labels?” Deniz asked.

Ms. Aylin paused. “He mentioned them to me, yes. But you’ll need to ask him what he meant. I don’t want to guess for him.”

Deniz’s fingers tightened around the edge of the table. That was not the answer they wanted, though it was probably the answer they needed.

So far, the facts were these: Ms. Aylin had asked Samir to move the bridge. The window had leaked. The notebook pages had been loosening. Samir had been carrying both things.

None of those facts explained “Not now.” None explained why he had whispered instead of speaking normally. None explained why Deniz had felt as if the work—and maybe the friendship—was being taken away.

Deniz looked toward Samir. He was kneeling by the bridge, pressing one wooden piece carefully against another. He did not look over.

The new information made Deniz’s first guess wobble. Maybe Samir had not been rejecting the exhibit. Maybe he had been trying to help. But a practical reason could explain where the bridge went without fixing the hot, embarrassed feeling that had started when Samir shut them out.

Deniz thanked Ms. Aylin and returned to the table. They picked up the pencil, then set it down again. The missing answer was still Samir’s. This time, guessing felt less useful than asking.

Deniz sat at the work table with the notebook open, though the page stayed blank. Across the room, Samir knelt beside the word bridge. He pressed the crooked middle piece into place, lifted it when it leaned, and tried again.

Deniz watched his hands. Samir had been hurrying that day, yes. He had also tried to return the notebook. Deniz remembered the way Samir had held it out before they took it without speaking. Maybe he had been trying to help. Maybe he had still thought the labels were bad. Both guesses felt possible, which was almost worse than having one.

Deniz pulled a scrap of paper from the notebook and wrote:

I saw Samir moving the bridge and holding my notebook.
I heard him whisper about the labels.
He said, “Not now.”

Below that, Deniz began another list.

He thinks my labels are awful.
He wants to do the exhibit without me.
He was trying to help.

Deniz stared at the last three lines. The first two felt sharp and hot. The third felt annoying because it might be true.

They turned the paper over and tried to write what they could say. At first, the words came out wrong.

“You were careless, and you made me feel terrible.”

Deniz scratched that out. It sounded like a door slamming.

Next they wrote, “I saw you move the bridge, and I felt embarrassed and angry. Why were you talking about my labels?”

That was closer, but it still sounded as if the answer had already been decided.

Deniz tapped the pencil against the table. “I saw… I felt… I want to know…” they whispered.

They tried again: “I saw you moving the bridge and holding my notebook. I felt embarrassed and angry when you said, ‘Not now.’ I want to know what you meant when you talked about the labels.”

The sentence made Deniz’s stomach twist. It was not perfectly calm. Their voice might shake. Samir might look surprised or annoyed. But the question was real, and it left room for an answer.

Across the room, Samir adjusted the bridge again. One block slipped loose. He caught it before it hit the floor, then searched through a small box for glue. He was repairing the bridge alone, just as he had been doing when Deniz first noticed him.

Deniz looked at the two lists. The facts still did not explain Samir’s words. The facts also did not prove the worst guesses. Samir might have caused the confusion and still have been trying to help.

Deniz folded the scrap of paper once, then stood. Their knees felt wobbly, and the question seemed much easier on paper.

“Samir,” Deniz said, taking a step toward the bridge. “Can we talk before we change anything?”

Samir looked up from the bridge. He had a smear of glue on one thumb and a worried crease between his eyebrows.

“Sure,” he said. “What’s wrong?”

Deniz unfolded the scrap of paper, but the words looked smaller than they had at the table. They took a breath.

“I saw you moving the bridge and holding my notebook,” Deniz said. “Then I heard you whispering about the labels to Ms. Aylin. When I came over, you said, ‘Not now.’ I felt embarrassed and angry. I thought you thought my labels were bad. Did you?”

Samir blinked. He set the loose block beside his knee.

“No,” he said. “I didn’t think your ideas were bad. Some of the writing was hard to read, though. I was telling Ms. Aylin that we needed bigger letters on a few labels.”

Deniz looked at the notebook. “You were talking about changing my labels.”

“Changing the size,” Samir said quickly. “Not throwing them out. I liked the one about proud and nervous. I just couldn’t read ‘nervous’ from the other side of the room.”

He pointed to the bridge. “Ms. Aylin told me to move this because the window had leaked. I was trying to carry it before more water came in. Your notebook pages were coming loose, so I grabbed it too. You came back when I was balancing everything.”

Deniz remembered Samir’s arms full of blocks and the notebook pressed against his elbow. The memory did not erase the hot feeling in their chest.

“But you said, ‘Not now,’” Deniz said. “You didn’t tell me any of that. I thought you wanted me to go away.”

Samir’s eyes dropped to the crooked bridge piece. “I was trying not to drop it. I should have said, ‘I’m in a hurry. I’ll explain in a minute.’ Instead, I sounded like I was shutting you out.”

“I also took the notebook and walked off,” Deniz said. “You tried to give it back, and I didn’t say anything.”

“I thought you were mad at me,” Samir replied. “So I worked here by myself.”

For a moment, the room held only the soft hum of the old window fan. Deniz felt the anger loosen, though embarrassment was still there, smaller now but real.

“We both made guesses,” Deniz said.

“Pretty big ones,” Samir said. “Mine was that you didn’t want to work with me anymore.”

Deniz gave a shaky laugh. “That wasn’t true.”

“Neither was mine about your labels being awful.”

They spread the notebook across the table. Together, they chose thicker markers and rewrote three labels in larger letters. Samir held the page flat while Deniz traced each word. Then Deniz passed him the marker for the label about worried and excited.

“Your turn,” Deniz said. “Tell me if you’re changing something.”

“I’m changing the size,” Samir said, and grinned.

They repaired the loose bridge piece with glue and added a strip of cardboard beneath it. Then they carried the bridge farther from the window, to a dry table under the bulletin board. Deniz checked that each block stood firmly before they placed the labels around it.

“If either of us is rushing,” Samir said, “we explain what’s happening.”

“And if I start guessing,” Deniz added, “I’ll ask before deciding.”

Samir held out his gluey hand. Deniz bumped it with a clean fist.

The bridge still needed time to dry, but it stood straight. The exhibit was not finished yet. This time, though, they were facing the same table.

By Saturday afternoon, the word bridge stood straight on the dry table under the bulletin board. The cardboard support was hidden beneath it, and the three new labels were easy to read from the doorway.

Deniz placed the last marker in the supply box. Samir checked the corners of the display, then looked toward the community-room door.

“People are coming,” he said.

A family entered first. The youngest child pointed at the bridge. “What is it for?”

Deniz smiled. “Each block has a word connected to a feeling. But a word can change how a feeling seems.”

Samir picked up the label that said nervous. “Nervous can mean worried about something difficult, like speaking to a group. But it can also mean excited, like waiting for a surprise.”

“So nervous does not always mean bad?” the child asked.

“Right,” Deniz said. “You have to look at what is happening around the word.”

Samir nodded. “And sometimes you have to ask the person who used it.”

Deniz glanced at him. Samir was already explaining the next block to the child’s parents. His voice sounded steady. Their friendship was not exactly the same as it had been before the mix-up. It was less like a thing that could be switched back on and more like the bridge beneath the labels: repaired, supported, and worth checking.

More visitors came. Deniz explained proud and shy. Samir talked about worried and excited. They traded places without bumping elbows, and when one of them forgot a word, the other filled it in.

Near the end, Samir stopped beside the label about angry. He held the card between two fingers and stared at it.

Deniz felt a quick pinch of worry. For half a second, an old guess rose up: He thinks this one is wrong.

Then Deniz looked at the card. The letters were thick and clear. Samir was not frowning at Deniz. He was tracing the edge where the tape had lifted.

“Do you want to change it, or are you checking it?” Deniz asked.

Samir looked up. “Checking it. The tape is peeling.”

“Oh.” Deniz touched the tape. “I thought you didn’t like the label.”

“I can see why,” Samir said. “I should have said what I was doing.”

He pressed the tape down, but one corner popped up again. Together, they replaced it with a fresh strip. Then Samir pointed to the next card.

“Want to check that one with me?”

Deniz read it from the doorway. “Clear enough.”

At closing time, Ms. Aylin thanked them and switched off the room’s extra lights. Deniz and Samir carried the markers back to the shelf and straightened the visitor cards.

The bridge, the labels, and the friendship had all needed more than one small fix. Deniz did not feel completely unworried. They did feel certain about what had happened that afternoon, though: Samir had paused, Deniz had asked, and neither of them had to guess alone.
