The Space Between Words

At seventy, Deniz still arrived early enough to hear the community hub waking up. The automatic doors sighed open, the coffee machine began its cheerful sputtering, and somewhere down the hall a cart rattled over the tiles.

Deniz unlocked the cupboard beside the meeting room and took out the name cards. The cards had been written in large, steady letters the night before: LUCIA, OMAR, JUNE, PETER. Deniz set them around the oval table, leaving room for newcomers. Then they checked the pens, straightened the chairs, and placed the conversation prompts in a neat stack.

The weekly conversation table was one of the hub’s small fixtures, like the noticeboard and the bowl of peppermint sweets. Deniz and Mara had coordinated it together for nearly three years. Deniz welcomed people at the desk and kept track of who needed a quieter seat. Mara handled the schedule, supplies, and the occasional difficult cancellation. They did not need to discuss every detail. Usually, a glance was enough.

Deniz was carrying the welcome roster toward the front desk when Mara came in from the office. She had a folder tucked beneath one arm and a mobile phone in her hand. She gave Deniz a brief nod, then reached for the roster.

“I’ll take that,” Mara said.

She bent over the page. Deniz watched her pencil move down the list. The welcome desk, the first task on the morning schedule, had Deniz’s name beside it. Mara drew one clean line through the name and wrote her own.

Deniz looked at the change. “Is there a problem?”

“Not a problem,” Mara said. She kept her eyes on the paper. “I’ll talk with you about it later. We have a few things to get ready first.”

Her voice was even, but the answer left Deniz standing there with the roster in both hands.

A moment later, Mara carried the folder toward the coordinator’s office. The door was open. Deniz was returning the pens to the table when Mara spoke inside.

“Deniz cannot keep doing this,” she said.

The words were clear. Deniz stopped with one hand on the chair back.

The coordinator murmured something Deniz could not hear. Mara answered, “I know. I’ll handle it.”

Deniz looked at the chairs, the name cards, the carefully stacked prompts. For three years, handling things had been part of what they did together. Now Mara had removed Deniz from the welcome desk and was telling someone that Deniz could not keep doing this.

Perhaps Mara thought Deniz was unreliable. Perhaps she had decided that seventy was too old for the early setup, the lists, the steady stream of questions. The thought arrived quickly, carrying embarrassment with it.

Mara came out of the office. “Deniz—”

“I need to go,” Deniz said.

“But we should discuss—”

“Later, then.”

Mara’s expression tightened, or perhaps Deniz only imagined that it did. Deniz put the roster on the desk, picked up their coat, and walked through the automatic doors.

Outside, the morning air was cool. Deniz had come to the hub ready to arrange the room and welcome the regulars. They left carrying two things instead: what Mara had done, and what Deniz feared it meant.

By the time Deniz reached home, the morning had lost its ordinary shape. The kettle clicked off, but Deniz left the tea untouched while standing at the kitchen counter.

The roster lay beside the fruit bowl. Mara’s pencil mark was still visible: one straight line through Deniz’s name, Mara’s name written below it.

Deniz picked up the page and read it again. That was an observed fact. So was Mara’s sentence in the office: Deniz cannot keep doing this. So was the coolness—or what had felt like coolness—in Mara’s goodbye.

The rest was an interpretation.

Mara no longer trusted me.

Deniz said the thought aloud, then disliked hearing it in the quiet kitchen. It sounded more definite than it had a moment before. They put the roster down, made the tea, and sat near the window with both hands around the mug.

Hurt, Deniz decided. And embarrassment.

The embarrassment was not only about the welcome desk. It came from imagining the coordinator listening while Mara explained that Deniz could not manage the work anymore. At seventy, Deniz knew the difference between needing a hand and being pushed aside. Or believed they did. The trouble was that feelings could blur the difference.

Their phone lit up on the table. A message from Mara waited there.

Could you confirm whether you’ll be at Thursday’s preparation session?

Deniz read it twice. The sensible answer was easy. Yes, I’ll be there. But the question seemed to contain another one: Can you still be relied upon?

Deniz typed, Yes. Then deleted it. They typed, I’ll attend Thursday. That looked too formal. Finally, they sent: Yes. Thursday.

The message went with a soft chime. Almost at once, Deniz wished they had added something warmer. Thank you. See you then. Anything that might tell Mara this was hurt, not indifference.

But the moment had passed. Deniz set the phone face down.

Later that afternoon, Deniz opened the shared hub calendar on the laptop. The page showed the coming week’s tasks. The welcome desk was assigned to Mara. So were setup and supply pickup.

Deniz leaned closer. Those were two jobs they usually handled before the table began. Mara had moved the attendance summary to her own list as well.

There was evidence, Deniz thought, for the conclusion they feared. If Mara had decided Deniz was no longer dependable, taking away the work would make sense.

There was also another possibility. Mara might have been trying to take some weight off Deniz’s shoulders. That possibility was harder to hold. It did not explain why she had changed the roster without asking, or why she had said those words where Deniz could hear them.

Deniz closed the laptop.

At the hub, Mara would now be doing the early work alone. Perhaps she expected Deniz to object. Perhaps she expected no answer at all.

Thursday arrived with no further message. Deniz did not send one. Mara did not send another either.

The silence began to acquire meaning. To Deniz, it felt like exclusion being quietly put into place. To Mara, though Deniz did not yet know this, the clipped reply and the absence of any explanation could look like resentment. Mara saw a closed door where Deniz had meant only to protect a bruised feeling.

On Wednesday evening, Deniz took out the roster once more. The paper had a crease where it had been folded and unfolded. They considered writing to Mara: What did you mean?

Instead, Deniz put the page back beside the fruit bowl.

Thursday’s session was still on the calendar. Deniz could attend, ask, and risk hearing an answer they would not like. Or cancel, and let the silence speak for them.

For the moment, Deniz left both choices open.

Deniz did not cancel Thursday.

Before leaving home, they opened the shared hub calendar again. The entries had not changed. Mara had taken setup, supply pickup, and the attendance summary. Next to each task was a time and a short note.

Deniz looked at the dates rather than the feeling those entries stirred.

On the last three Thursdays, Deniz had stayed late. One evening, they had waited for a new group of speakers to finish sorting their papers. Another time, they had remained after the table to help stack chairs and put away the tea urn. The third late finish was the night the printer stopped working. Deniz had gone home after nine.

The calendar showed something they had not noticed before. On each of those evenings, Mara had added work to her own list.

Setup—Mara.
Supply pickup—Mara.
Attendance summary—Mara.

Deniz opened the task notes. Under the supply entry, Mara had written: I can handle this part.

That was all.

Deniz leaned back from the laptop. The sentence might have meant, You have done enough this week. It might also have meant, I do not want you responsible for this anymore. The words did not settle the matter. They only made both possibilities more visible.

The roster still troubled Deniz. Mara had crossed out Deniz’s name and written her own without asking. That was a fact. So was the overheard sentence: Deniz cannot keep doing this. So was Mara’s cool goodbye, though Deniz admitted that “cool” described an impression, not a measurable temperature.

Deniz almost smiled at that, but the smile did not last.

They printed the calendar and took a sheet of plain paper from the drawer. At the top, they wrote two headings.

FACTS

Mara moved my name from the welcome desk roster.
She said she would discuss it later.
I heard her say, “Deniz cannot keep doing this.”
She covered setup, supply pickup, and the attendance summary.
She wrote, “I can handle this part.”
I replied, “Yes. Thursday,” and did not explain why I was hurt.

GUesses

Mara thinks I am unreliable.
Mara thinks I am too old for the work.
Mara is trying to push me out.
Mara is worried I am doing too much.
Mara changed the roster because she wanted to help.

Deniz paused over the last two lines. The first guesses had arrived quickly and felt heavy. The second set felt possible but unproven. Neither set belonged in the column of facts.

They added a third note in the margin: Ask, do not decide.

The phrase sounded a little formal, like something printed on a meeting-room poster. Deniz left it there anyway. It was useful.

A knock sounded from the apartment next door, followed by the brief rumble of someone moving a shopping trolley down the hall. Ordinary sounds. The day was going on while Deniz sat with one changed roster and several possible explanations.

They folded the paper once, not as tightly as the roster had been folded, and placed it beside the laptop. The list had not made the hurt disappear. It had done something quieter. It had stopped the hurt from pretending to be evidence.

Deniz picked up the phone and opened Mara’s message.

Could you confirm whether you’ll be at Thursday’s preparation session?

The clipped reply sat beneath it: Yes. Thursday.

Deniz could see how that might have sounded. Not angry, exactly. Not welcoming either. If Mara had been trying to help, the message would not have told her so. It might even have suggested that Deniz wanted no further discussion.

That, too, was a fact. Deniz had chosen the words.

They put on a coat and checked the time. There was still enough of the morning to reach the hub before the others arrived. Deniz would look at the roster once more, then ask Mara what she had meant. Not to win an argument. Not to prove that the hurt was justified. The missing fact belonged to Mara, and Deniz could not find it in a calendar.

At the door, they hesitated with one hand on the latch. The question would be uncomfortable. Mara might be defensive. Deniz might hear something painful.

Still, leaving the question unanswered had already begun to damage the work they shared.

Deniz stepped into the hallway, carrying the folded page in a coat pocket and the unresolved question with it.

The community hub was open when Deniz arrived, though the conversation room still stood empty. Morning light fell across the polished floor, catching the wheels of the folded chairs. Near the entrance, the notice board displayed the week’s activities: tax help, a poetry group, a computer clinic, and Thursday’s conversation table.

Deniz knew nearly everyone whose name appeared there. They had helped several of them find the right room, locate a missing umbrella, or work out the new booking system. Familiarity usually made the hub feel easy. That morning, it made the unanswered question harder to carry.

Mara was at the long table, checking the tea supplies against a list. She looked up when Deniz came in.

“Good morning.”

“Good morning.”

The exchange was polite and thin. Deniz set their bag beside the stack of name cards. Their hand rested on the strap a moment longer than necessary.

Mara glanced toward the clock. “The others will be here in about half an hour.”

“I know.” Deniz took out the folded sheet from their coat pocket, then put it back. “Could I ask you for ten quiet minutes before we begin?”

Mara’s expression changed, not dramatically, but enough for Deniz to notice. Her shoulders drew up. She placed the supply list on the table.

“About the roster?”

“Yes.”

Mara looked toward the doorway, where a cart of clean mugs waited to be returned to the kitchen. “All right. Ten minutes.”

Her answer sounded guarded. Deniz felt a small tightening beneath the ribs. Mara seemed to be preparing for criticism, perhaps because Deniz’s brief message had made the request sound formal and cold. For a second, Deniz considered saying that it was nothing urgent. That would have been easier, and untrue.

They moved to two chairs near the windows, far enough from the entrance to speak without being overheard. Deniz sat down but did not open the folded page. The words they had practiced in the hallway now seemed stiff and exposed.

I felt pushed aside when I saw the roster.

That was the beginning. Not, You pushed me aside. Not, You think I am incapable. The first sentence belonged to Deniz and could not pretend to know what belonged to Mara.

Mara sat opposite them, hands clasped loosely in her lap. “You wanted to talk.”

Deniz nodded. “I did. I may not say this perfectly.”

“You usually have a clear way of saying things.”

There was no warmth in the remark, but there was no sharpness either. Deniz could not tell whether Mara meant it as praise or complaint. They let the uncertainty remain uncertain.

“I want to start with what I saw,” Deniz said. “You crossed my name off the welcome desk roster and wrote in your own. You said you would discuss it later. Then I heard you say, ‘Deniz cannot keep doing this,’ and, ‘I’ll handle it.’”

Mara’s mouth tightened. She looked down at the floor.

Deniz continued before courage failed. “Those are the things I know. What I guessed was that you thought I was unreliable, or that you wanted to push me out of the work.”

Mara lifted her eyes. “And what did you feel?”

“Hurt. Embarrassed, too.” Deniz pressed their fingertips together. “I felt pushed aside when I saw the roster. I know that is my feeling, not proof of what you intended. That is why I am asking.”

Mara leaned back. For a moment, neither of them spoke. From the kitchen came the soft clink of cups being arranged for the session.

“What did you intend when you changed the roster?” Deniz asked.

Mara’s guardedness did not disappear. If anything, it became more visible. “I can answer that. But I need to say something first.”

“All right.”

“Your messages made it sound as if you were angry with me. ‘Yes. Thursday.’ Nothing else.”

Deniz looked at the folded page in their pocket. “I was hurt. I did not know how to say it.”

“I assumed you did not want my help.” Mara’s voice remained careful. “So I stopped asking.”

The statement did not explain the roster. It did explain why the silence had grown.

A chair scraped in the hall. Someone had arrived early. Mara glanced toward the sound, then back at Deniz.

“We still have a few minutes,” she said. “I will tell you what I meant.”

Deniz nodded, though their stomach remained tight. The difficult conversation had not gone away. It had only been given a place and a time. That was enough for now. They turned the folded page over in their pocket and stayed in the chair, ready to listen.

“I will tell you what I meant,” Mara said. “After you stayed late three times in a row, I began to worry.”

Deniz kept their hands folded. “Worry about what?”

“That you were carrying too much. On Monday, you stayed to finish the attendance summary after everyone else had gone. On Tuesday, you picked up the supplies, stayed for the full table, and then put away the chairs. Wednesday was the same. You said you were fine, but you looked tired.”

“I remember those evenings,” Deniz said. “I was tired. I also chose to finish the work.”

“I know. That is part of what made this difficult.” Mara glanced toward the kitchen, then back. “I thought if I asked you to give up the welcome desk, you would say no. So I changed the roster myself.”

Deniz felt the old hurt move through them again, sharp enough that they did not want to soften it too quickly. “When you changed it without asking, it felt as if you had decided I could no longer be trusted.”

“I understand why it looked that way.”

“And when I heard you say, ‘Deniz cannot keep doing this,’ I thought you meant I could not do the work.”

Mara winced. “I meant you could not keep doing all of it. I was speaking to myself as much as to anyone else. I said, ‘I’ll handle it,’ because I thought taking over would solve the problem.”

“It solved it without telling me there was a problem.”

“Yes.” Mara lowered her eyes. “That was my mistake.”

The words landed plainly. They did not erase the embarrassment Deniz had carried home, or the lonely certainty that had followed them through the week. Still, the explanation changed the shape of those feelings. The roster had been real. The overheard words had been real. Their meaning had been different from the one Deniz had supplied.

Deniz took a slow breath. “My part was not small, either. I sent you that message—‘Yes. Thursday.’—and nothing else. I was hurt, but I made you guess. Then I stayed quiet because I wanted you to notice.”

Mara gave a rueful smile. “I noticed. I just guessed wrong.”

“I guessed wrong too.”

For a moment, they listened to the sounds gathering in the hub: a cupboard closing, footsteps in the corridor, the low murmur of an early arrival. The conversation table would begin soon. Neither of them seemed eager to leave the chairs, but the quiet no longer felt like a wall.

Mara said, “I am sorry I changed your duties without speaking to you. I was trying to help, but I made a decision about your time without including you.”

“Thank you,” Deniz said. Their voice was steady, though the hurt had not vanished. “I am sorry I answered as if you had insulted me and then gave you no chance to explain.”

Mara nodded. “What would make this work better?”

Deniz reached into their coat and unfolded the fact-and-guess page. The paper had softened at the creases. They placed it between them, not as evidence against Mara, but as a reminder of how much had been left unsaid.

“Let us rewrite the roster together,” Deniz said. “I still want to welcome people. I do not need to give up that desk. But I could stop handling the attendance summary every week. You could take it twice a month, and I can take supply pickup on the other weeks.”

Mara picked up a pencil from the windowsill. “And setup?”

“We do it together unless one of us says otherwise.”

“Good.” Mara drew a line through the old assignments on the spare roster. “And before either of us changes the other person’s duties, we ask. Even if we think we are being helpful.”

Deniz considered the sentence. “Ask first. That belongs on the page.”

Mara wrote it at the bottom in clear block letters: ASK BEFORE CHANGING A DUTY.

Then Deniz added, “A short check-in before the table begins. Five minutes. We can say what we are carrying that day.”

“Five minutes,” Mara agreed. “If one of us is tired, or needs a different task, we say so directly.”

They read the new roster together. It was not a grand arrangement. It was a welcome desk assignment, a supply schedule, an attendance column, and a small square marked CHECK-IN. Yet Deniz felt the practical details holding the apology in place.

Mara folded the old roster and set it aside. “Will you work with me today?”

Deniz looked at her. The question was simple, and answering it still required care.

“Yes,” they said. “I will.”

Mara stood, then waited until Deniz rose. Together they carried the revised roster to the long table. The conversation room was still empty, but the chairs were ready, the name cards stacked, and the first pot of water beginning to warm in the kitchen.

At the doorway, Mara touched the new check-in box with one finger. “Five minutes next week?”

“Five minutes,” Deniz said.

They went to prepare the room side by side, not because the difficult week had become unimportant, but because both of them now knew what had happened—and what they would do before guessing again.

The following Thursday, Deniz arrived at the community hub ten minutes early. The welcome desk stood where it always did, beneath the bulletin board crowded with notices about music evenings, tax appointments, and the neighborhood garden. Their name was printed on the new roster in Mara’s careful handwriting.

Mara was already in the preparation room. She had set out the cups and was sorting the supply box. When Deniz came in, Mara glanced at the clock.

“Five-minute check-in?” she asked.

“Five-minute check-in.”

They stood beside the long table while the hub slowly woke around them. A door opened down the corridor. Someone laughed near the kitchen. Deniz looked at the roster. Setup was marked SHARED. The attendance summary belonged to Mara this week, and supply pickup belonged to Deniz.

“How are you carrying things today?” Mara asked.

“Steady,” Deniz said. “I can do the welcome desk and supplies.”

“And I can finish the attendance notes before everyone arrives.” Mara tapped the folder beside her. “Good.”

The check-in took less than five minutes, but neither of them hurried through it. Then Mara carried the supply box toward the storage room. At the doorway, she stopped. Her hand remained on the box’s handle while she looked back at the roster.

For an instant, unease rose in Deniz. It was the old sensation, quieter now but recognizable: a tightening in the chest, followed by the thought that Mara had changed something without saying so.

Deniz noticed the thought. They did not treat it as a fact.

“Mara,” they said, “I am feeling a little uneasy. Are you about to change the supply task?”

Mara turned. She did not look offended. “I was considering it, but I should have said so. The delivery came earlier than expected, and the boxes are heavier than usual. I can take this one if you would rather stay at the desk.”

Deniz looked at the box. It was not enormous, but it was awkwardly packed, with paper goods pressing against the lid.

“I can still do it,” Deniz said. “But if you are already carrying the attendance notes, perhaps we should switch for today.”

“That seems sensible.” Mara set the box down. “Would you like me to mark it on the roster?”

“Yes. Let us mark it together.”

Mara brought the roster to the table. Deniz crossed out their name beside supply pickup and wrote Mara’s name in the space. Then they added their own beside the attendance folder.

“Today only,” Deniz said.

“Today only,” Mara agreed. “Next week we ask again rather than assuming.”

Deniz nodded. The small exchange had not required an apology. It had required a question while there was still time to answer it.

A few minutes later, Deniz took their place at the welcome desk. The first attendee came through the glass doors carrying a folded newspaper and wearing the green scarf she brought every Thursday.

“Good morning,” Deniz said. “It is good to see you.”

“Good morning, Deniz. Are we discussing the river walk today?”

“We are. Your name card is ready.”

Across the room, Mara arranged the cups and placed the supply box beneath the counter. She looked up. Deniz met her eyes, and Mara gave a brief, easy wave before returning to the table.

The hub filled gradually: familiar voices, the scrape of chairs, the smell of coffee beginning in the kitchen. Deniz welcomed each person and watched Mara move through the room without needing to guess what her movements meant.

The unease had not vanished forever. It had appeared, been named, and met with an answer. Now Deniz turned the next name card face up and greeted the person coming through the door.
