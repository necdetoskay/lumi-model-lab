The Shared Map

Ege spread a wide sheet of paper across a bench in the small market town. Around him, baskets of peaches, ribbons, and bright flower pots made the morning market sparkle.

“Garden Day map!” he announced. “Children will start here, zip past the blue fountain, turn at the sunflower arch, and race all the way to the gardens!”

His pencil hurried over the page, drawing one bold red line between the brightest places. He added a star beside the fountain, a circle around the arch, and three curly arrows.

Lina leaned closer. “Could we mark the sounds, too? The little bells near the herb garden are soft, and the music corner is very—”

“And we need a shortcut!” Ege said, drawing another arrow. “A map should help explorers keep moving. I’ll add a bridge-shaped line from the fountain to the rose gate.”

“Maybe we could show the colors of the flowers,” Lina tried again. “And leave a few quiet pauses for children who want to look—”

“A treasure trail!” Ege burst out. He drew tiny diamonds beside his red line. “Children can follow the diamonds and find every wonderful place before lunch.”

Lina waited until his pencil stopped. Then she pointed gently to the edge of the paper, where her notes had become a small, crowded patch of words: bells, blue petals, quiet pause.

“Those ideas are still here,” she said. “But the map does not feel ready yet.”

Ege looked at the busy red trail. It connected many landmarks, just as he wanted. Still, Lina’s little notes sat outside it, as if they belonged to a different map.

The market hummed around them. Garden Day was only a few days away, and soon children would hold this map in their hands.

Ege folded the crowded sketch under his arm. “Let’s walk the route,” he said. “Then I can make it even quicker.”

They followed the market path past baskets of mint and marigolds. Soon they heard a lively tune jingling from the music corner. Children clapped beside little drums while a gardener hung bright ribbons between two trees.

“I would start here,” said a girl, pointing to the music. “I like places that sound busy.”

Her younger brother covered one ear. “I’d rather see the quiet flowers first.”

Lina knelt so she could hear him clearly. “The flower lane near the blue gate?”

He nodded. “The blue ones are my favorite. And I like stopping to look.”

Ege glanced at his red line. It rushed straight toward the music corner, then turned toward the rose gate. But beside the ribbon trees, he spotted another path curling between beds of lavender. Farther on, the paths joined again beneath a wide picnic tree.

A gardener carrying watering cans smiled when Lina asked which way visitors liked best. “Some children follow the music,” she said. “Others take the flower lane slowly. Families often choose both, then meet under that tree for a snack.”

Ege traced the joining paths with his finger. “So the routes don’t have to be the same. They can still bring everyone together.”

“And children can choose what feels right to them,” Lina added. “Lively sounds for some, quiet colors and pauses for others.”

Ege looked at the sketch again. His single red trail suddenly seemed too bossy for such a many-colored garden. “We found something neither of us saw from the bench,” he said. “A map needs choices, but the choices must stay connected.”

He began to draw two lines in the air, one skipping and one winding. Then he stopped. “How will we show that simply?”

Lina looked from the music corner to the quiet lane and back to the picnic tree. “We’ll have to listen, look, and try an idea together.”

They sat beneath the tree with the sketch between them. The garden hummed on both sides, while the empty paper waited.

Lina spread the sketch on the picnic-tree bench and placed three small pebbles along the paths. “This one is the music corner,” she said, setting a pebble beside the ribbon trees. “This one is the quiet flower lane. Children may want different sounds and different speeds, even when they are exploring together.”

Ege opened his mouth to suggest a shortcut. Then he closed it. He looked at Lina’s pebbles instead.

“Can you show me the routes you heard about?” he asked.

Lina’s face brightened. She moved one pebble slowly along the flower lane and another quickly toward the music corner. “Some children will follow a tune. Some will stop to notice blue petals or listen to bees. They can start together, choose differently, and meet here.” She tapped the picnic tree.

Ege studied his single red arrow trail. “Then I need to change it,” he said. “A single fast route cannot show what you noticed.”

He turned the paper sideways and drew two new lines. A bright yellow line skipped toward the music corner, while a soft green line wound past the blue gate and the quiet flowers. At the place where they joined, both lines curved toward the picnic tree.

“For the quiet path,” Ege said, “let’s add a listening leaf.” He drew a little leaf with curved lines inside it, like a leaf listening to a whisper. “That means pause and notice.”

Lina took the green pencil. Beside the flower lane, she added tiny blue petals and a small ear-shaped mark. Along the yellow line, she drew music notes and red ribbon dashes. “These can help children guess what each path feels like,” she said.

Ege connected the two lines carefully to the picnic tree, then added a round circle there. He frowned at the page. “The circle may mean meeting—or it may just look like a flower.”

“And the listening leaf might look like an ordinary leaf,” Lina said.

They smiled at each other. The map had two choices and one shared destination, but its signs still needed trying. “Tomorrow,” said Ege, “we should let some children follow it without us explaining every mark.”

The next morning, Ege and Lina brought the draft map to three children near the market. Sami chose the yellow line because he liked the music marks. Nara pointed to the green line and said, “I want the flower lane.” Jojo decided to walk with Nara and carry the map.

“Choose the line that suits you,” Ege said. He and Lina followed behind without explaining the signs.

The children found the quiet flower lane and stopped to look at blue petals. Sami skipped toward the ribbon trees, where bells tinkled and music floated through the garden. Soon, both paths curled toward the picnic tree.

At the joining place, the children slowed down.

“Is this round flower the meeting place?” Sami asked.

“Or should we follow around it?” said Jojo.

Nara pointed to the listening leaf. “Does this mean stop here, or listen somewhere else?”

Ege glanced at Lina. The children were not having trouble choosing a path. They were having trouble knowing what the signs asked them to do next.

Lina knelt beside the paper. “They also need to know where the map begins,” she said. “If Garden Day starts at the market, the first mark should be there.”

Ege looked back toward the market stalls and the blue fountain. “A start mark,” he said. “My arrows begin in my head, but the children will begin in the town.”

Together they made a new legend. Ege drew a little gold sun beside the blue fountain and wrote, “START.” At the picnic tree, Lina changed the plain circle into two small children holding hands. Beneath it, Ege wrote, “MEET HERE.”

They kept the listening leaf, but Lina added a tiny ear inside it and the words, “PAUSE AND NOTICE.” She placed music notes beside the yellow line and blue petals beside the green one.

The children followed the revised instructions from the gold sun. They chose their lines, paused at the listening leaf, and gathered beneath the picnic tree. The new marks made the next steps clearer, even though Ege and Lina knew a larger Garden Day group would still give the map its real test.

Ege folded the map carefully. “It is ready for Garden Day,” he said.

Lina tucked the pencil into the map’s fold. “Ready to be used,” she replied.

They carried it back toward the market, its two colored paths bright in the morning light.

Garden Day filled the market with bright awnings, flower baskets, and the cheerful murmur of visiting families. Beside the blue fountain, Ege and Lina spread their finished map on a wooden table. The little gold sun shone beside the word START.

“Which way should we go?” asked Sami, who had come back with his friends.

A girl pointed to the yellow line. “I hear music!”

Another child touched the green line. “I want to see the blue flowers.”

The grown-ups and children looked at Ege and Lina.

“Both ways begin here,” Lina said. “You can choose, and meet again at the picnic tree.”

The lively group followed the yellow path. Music notes on the map led them past ribbon trees, where bells tinkled in the warm breeze. The children listened for each sound and found the music corner glowing with bright scarves.

The calm group followed the green line. Blue-petal marks guided them through the quiet flower lane. When they reached the listening leaf, they saw its tiny ear.

“Pause and notice,” one child read.

They grew still. Bees hummed softly near the flowers. A breeze moved through the tall stems like a whisper. Then the children smiled and continued along the green path.

Ege watched the two colored lines carry the groups through different parts of the gardens. Lina watched the children use the signs without needing a long explanation. At each turn, the music notes, blue petals, and listening leaf gave the map a job to do.

At last, both groups reached the wide picnic tree. The two small figures holding hands on the map matched the children gathering beneath its branches.

“We found each other!” called Sami.

“And we both got to choose,” said the girl from the flower lane.

Ege placed one hand on the map. “The yellow path helped us notice the lively places,” he said.

“And the green path helped us notice the quiet ones,” Lina added.

Around them, children compared the bells they had heard with the flowers they had seen. Some wanted to try the other route next. Ege and Lina looked at the map together as sunlight flickered through the picnic tree’s leaves. Its lines had carried different footsteps to the same bright meeting place.

After the children compared bells and flowers, Lina carried the map to a long table beneath the picnic tree. Beside it lay a smaller copy, made for everyone to add to.

“Can we draw on it?” asked Sami.

“Please do,” said Lina. “Add something you noticed.”

Sami drew three tiny music notes near the yellow path. The girl from the flower lane added blue petals beside the green line. Another child drew a little swirl for the breeze near the listening leaf. Soon the display copy held bright marks from many exploring hands.

Ege watched carefully. When a child reached for a blank corner, he slid the paper closer instead of filling the space himself.

“There is room here,” he said. “What would you like to put there?”

The child added a small purple flower.

As the afternoon light turned honey-colored, Ege rolled up the map that had guided the visitors. “Lina,” he said, “will you keep this with me in the town workshop?”

Lina looked at the map. Her blue petals, listening leaf, and quiet-path notes rested beside Ege’s yellow lines, music marks, and meeting symbol.

“Together?” she asked.

“Together.”

Back at the workshop, Ege pinned the map above their worktable. Under the title, he wrote his name. Then he left a wide, empty space beside it.

Lina picked up a green pencil. “May I add mine?”

Ege handed it to her. She wrote her name in the open space, then drew one tiny blue petal between the two names.

Outside, new explorers gathered near the blue fountain. A child pointed toward the yellow line. Another pointed toward the green one. Their families began along different paths, while bells tinkled near the music corner and soft bees hummed in the flower lane.

From the workshop doorway, Ege and Lina watched the colorful gardens fill with footsteps, voices, and pauses. On the table behind them, the shared map waited, with room for whatever the next explorers would notice.
