The Space Between Words

By six o’clock, Deniz had finished the last of her calls at the housing office, answered three messages from her brother about their mother’s appointment, and driven across town to the Lale Community Hub. She was fifty, old enough to know that a day could change shape without asking permission, and experienced enough not to mistake an interruption for an excuse. Still, she disliked arriving late.

The hub occupied the ground floor of a former library. Its wide windows looked onto the tram stop, and its entrance usually smelled of coffee, dust, and whatever someone had brought in from home. Deniz knew which cupboard held the spare mugs, which radiator clicked in cold weather, and how to coax the old printer through a paper jam. For four years, she and Arda had organized the hub’s oral-history evenings together. The open house on Saturday would introduce recordings from longtime residents: first jobs, neighborhood changes, weddings, strikes, recipes, departures.

She had taken the storeroom key home the previous evening. Her niece had called just as Deniz was locking up, upset about a problem with her landlord. Deniz had gone to help, then fallen asleep before returning the key. She had brought it back first thing in the morning, but Arda had not been there. Recently, he had tightened the hub’s access procedures, asking volunteers to sign keys in and out and to avoid leaving them in the desk drawer.

Now Deniz found him near the reception table, sorting name tags into neat piles. She lifted the key. “I’m sorry this is late. I had a family emergency.”

Arda looked at it, then at the clock. “All right. Put it in the log, please.”

His voice was not sharp. It was also not the easy, teasing voice that usually met her at the door.

Deniz signed the sheet and set the key in its marked envelope. From the corridor, she heard Arda speaking with Mira, one of Saturday’s volunteers.

“We need someone else at the welcome desk,” he said.

The sentence reached Deniz before the rest of the conversation, swallowed by the scrape of a folding table. She stood still, holding a stack of programs against her coat.

A moment later Arda noticed her. “The programs can go on the side table,” he said. “We’re still checking the room layout.”

“Of course.”

Usually he would have asked about her mother, or told her some small absurdity from the morning. Instead, he turned back to the name tags. When Deniz mentioned the microphone batteries, he answered, “They’re in the supply cabinet.” When she asked whether the speakers had confirmed, he said, “Two have. I’m waiting on the others.” Each reply was useful. None invited her into the familiar conversation they had built over years.

Deniz arranged the programs and watched without appearing to watch. Arda checked the storeroom latch twice. He counted the keys before placing the envelope in the locked drawer. Those details could have been ordinary caution. They could also have been what happened when someone no longer trusted her.

The embarrassment arrived first, hot beneath her collar. Then came a quieter, heavier feeling: exclusion. She wanted to ask, right there, whether “someone else” meant someone other than her. But the question, formed in anger, would carry its own answer.

So she said nothing about the sentence. She finished the layout, noted that the welcome table still needed a lamp, and told Arda she would return the next morning to help. She left with the key procedure followed and the partnership apparently intact, though neither felt secure.

Outside, Deniz paused beside the tram shelter. She could not yet tell whether Arda was protecting the hub’s rules or distancing himself from her. Until she had more than one overheard fragment and a changed manner, she decided, she would watch what he actually did.

The next morning, Deniz returned to the Lale Community Hub before the first volunteer arrived. She had slept badly, not because she had decided Arda meant to exclude her, but because she had not decided that he did not.

The distinction mattered. It did not make the uneasiness disappear.

She set her bag beneath the reception table and opened the shared planning folder on the computer. The Saturday schedule had been revised overnight. Near the bottom, between “speaker check” and “tea delivery,” someone had added: ACCESS REVIEW — INSURER, FRIDAY, 10:00–12:00.

Deniz read the line twice. The insurer had requested a walk-through before approving the hub’s updated coverage. That could explain the keys, the latch, the counting. It could also be an ordinary practical matter that had nothing to do with her place at the welcome desk.

She opened the key log. Her name appeared in yesterday’s entry.

STOREROOM — Deniz — returned 9:12 a.m. — marked envelope.

There was no note beside it. No warning. No explanation. The blank space seemed to acquire weight simply because she was looking at it. Arda had recorded the late return and left it there for anyone to see. Perhaps he was documenting a procedure. Perhaps he was documenting her.

At nine-thirty, Mira came in carrying a box of programs. Arda looked up from the printer and smiled.

“You found the revised labels,” he said. “Excellent. Can you put them with the name tags?”

“Of course,” Mira said.

He asked about her train, joked about the printer’s stubbornness, and took the box from her without being asked. A few minutes later, Cem arrived with the portable speaker. Arda greeted him just as warmly, asking about his shoulder and whether he had remembered the correct cable.

When Deniz came back from the supply cabinet, Arda said, “The spare extension cords are on the lower shelf.”

“I know,” she replied. “I put them there last month.”

“All right.” He turned to the speaker.

His words were not rude. That was part of the difficulty. Nothing she could quote would sound like a rejection. Yet the pattern seemed clear: warmth for everyone else, efficiency for her.

Deniz took a sheet of paper from her notebook and drew a line down the middle. On the left she wrote, “What happened.” On the right: “What it might mean.”

She listed the facts first.

Arda had said, “We need someone else at the welcome desk.” He had spoken briefly to her after the late key return. The schedule now showed an insurer’s access review. Her return was recorded without a note. He had been relaxed with Mira and Cem, but practical with her.

On the other side, she wrote possible meanings: He no longer trusted me. He wanted someone else in my role. He was focused on the access review. He was overloaded. He thought I was offended and was giving me space.

The last two possibilities irritated her because they were plausible. Plausibility was not comfort. It only prevented her from turning an interpretation into a fact.

She folded the page and went to the kitchenette, where the kettle clicked off. From the reception area came Arda’s voice, directing volunteers toward the folding chairs. Deniz could hear the strain in his quick instructions, though she wondered whether she was hearing strain or simply searching for evidence that supported her.

She opened her phone and typed:

Hi Arda—could you clarify the welcome-desk plan for Saturday? I’m happy to help; I’d just like to know whether you’re assigning someone else because of the access review or for another reason. No rush today.

She read it once for accusation, once for hidden pleading, and once for plain meaning. It survived all three readings. Then she sent it.

The message showed as delivered. Arda was standing six feet away, but his phone remained face down beside the printer. Deniz returned to the schedule and waited, the two columns open in her notebook and neither one yet entitled to win.

Arda’s reply arrived just before noon.

Let’s discuss after Saturday; I am overloaded.

Deniz read it once, then again. The words were not cruel. They were not even unusual for Arda during the final week before an open house. He was coordinating the speakers, checking the audio equipment, confirming the tea delivery, and dealing with the insurer’s visit on Friday. The schedule showed all of that in his crowded handwriting.

Still, he had not answered her question.

She had asked whether someone else was taking the welcome desk because of the access review or for another reason. He had answered neither part. Instead, he had moved the conversation beyond Saturday, beyond the event that had occupied four years of their shared work.

Deniz felt the conclusion form before she could stop it: He does not want to say it directly because the answer is yes.

She opened the message window and typed:

If you have already decided you don’t trust me, you could at least say so. I have helped run these events for four years, and being quietly replaced is not a reasonable way to treat me.

She paused with her thumb above the screen. The sentence was sharp, but not invented. She had been reliable. She had returned the key late, yes, but she had signed the log and put it in the marked envelope. One mistake—or one delay caused by helping her niece with a landlord problem—had not erased four years of work.

Her face grew hot. The feeling was larger than irritation. Shame, she thought. Shame mixed with anger.

Naming it did not make either feeling disappear. It did, however, make the draft look different. The message was no longer simply a request for an explanation. It was an attempt to make Arda feel the humiliation she was carrying.

Deniz placed the phone facedown and stood. Across the reception area, Arda was bent over a table covered with name tags, extension cords, printed transcripts, and two versions of the Saturday running order. A volunteer asked him where the spare microphone batteries were. He answered, found them, and returned to the running order without looking up.

He was overloaded. That was observable. So was the fact that he had not answered her question. Both things could be true.

Deniz went to the shared task list. Beside her name was: Assemble welcome-desk packets; confirm visitor list; bring the large-print programs.

She had planned to assemble the packets that afternoon. Each contained a map, a feedback card, and the names of the oral-history speakers. She selected the task and clicked “unassigned.”

For a moment, the change felt like relief. Then it felt like a door closing.

Mira looked over from the copier. “Were you still doing the desk packets?”

“I was,” Deniz said. “I’m taking that off my list for now.”

Mira glanced toward Arda, then back at Deniz. “Should I ask him?”

“No.” Deniz kept her voice even. “The list will show him.”

She did not want an intermediary carrying a sentence that she had not yet managed to say herself. At the same time, she knew the change would have consequences. Someone would have to assemble the packets, and Arda would have to notice that she had withdrawn from a task connected to the role he might no longer want her to hold.

Back at the table, Deniz turned to a clean page in her notebook. At the top she wrote the exact reply and the time: 11:47 a.m. Underneath, she recorded the surrounding facts: access review Friday, final preparations underway, Arda had answered other questions during the morning, and he had not clarified the welcome desk.

Then she added one sentence of her own: I felt ashamed and angry when the answer postponed the question.

She looked at the unsent message on her phone. Her hurt still had a reasonable basis. It also had an interpretation attached to it, and she could not yet prove that interpretation.

Deniz deleted the draft, not because the issue was settled, but because it was not. The empty message window left her with no relief and no explanation—only a visible gap in the work they were supposed to be doing together.

The next morning, Deniz returned to the Lale Community Hub with her notebook in the side pocket of her bag. She had slept, though not continuously. At three in the morning, she had found herself arranging the facts again: the key returned at 9:12, Arda’s sentence near the storeroom, the brief greetings, the unanswered question, and the task she had removed from the list.

By ten, the reception area was crowded with the ordinary signs of preparation. Printed transcripts waited in uneven stacks. A roll of blue tape leaned against the kettle. Someone had left a box of large-print programs beneath the table. Arda moved between these things with his phone in one hand and a clipboard in the other.

Deniz opened the shared schedule. The insurer’s access review was listed for Friday from ten until noon, directly across the final preparation period. Arda had marked several tasks in red: check the storeroom, confirm the exits, meet the inspector, test the microphone, and receive the tea delivery.

It explained why keys and doors occupied so much of his attention. It did not explain the sentence she had overheard, or why he had stopped speaking to her as he usually did.

Deniz left the schedule open and turned to her notebook. She drew three headings this time.

What I observed.

What I felt.

What I need to ask.

Under the first heading, she wrote the exact words: We need someone else at the welcome desk. She added that Arda had looked toward the key drawer afterward, that he had checked the storeroom latch twice, and that he had answered other volunteers warmly while speaking to her in short, practical replies.

Under the second heading she wrote: embarrassed, then ashamed and angry. She considered changing ashamed to hurt, but both seemed accurate. Shame had come from imagining that four years of dependable work could be reduced to one late return. Anger had come from being left to guess.

The last heading took more time.

What did you mean by “someone else at the welcome desk”?

Was the late key return part of your concern, and if so, what would you need from me now?

Neither question accused him of deciding against her. Neither pretended that the effect on her had been insignificant. They asked for the missing part of the situation.

Deniz read them twice. She was not sure the questions would produce the conversation she wanted. Arda might still be too busy. He might answer vaguely again. He might say that she had misunderstood, without explaining why his manner had changed. Preparing carefully did not give her control over his response.

It gave her a way to speak without handing her conclusion to him as if it were evidence.

Arda carried a stack of name tags past her. “The large-print programs can go by the entrance,” he said. His tone was courteous and efficient.

“Arda,” Deniz said.

He stopped, though his attention remained on the stack. “Yes?”

“I need to ask for fifteen minutes after planning today. Not about the running order. About the welcome desk and the key.”

His expression tightened—not angrily, Deniz thought, but with the concentration of someone checking how much time remained in a crowded day.

“Today?”

“If possible. Before either of us leaves.” She kept her hands on the notebook. “I’m not asking you to solve it while we’re setting up. I want to describe what I observed, tell you how it affected me, and ask two specific questions.”

Arda looked toward the table where the running order waited beneath a paperweight. For a moment, Deniz wondered whether he would postpone her again.

“After planning,” he said. “Fifteen minutes.”

“Thank you.”

“I may need to leave right after.”

“That’s fine. I’ll keep to the fifteen minutes.”

He nodded and carried the name tags away.

The answer was only an appointment, not an explanation. Deniz still did not know what he had meant, whether the late key had changed his view of her, or whether she would be able to return to the welcome desk. But the uncertainty now had a time and a place in which it could be addressed.

She closed her notebook and reopened the task list. The packet assignment was still unassigned. Deniz did not click her name back onto it. Not yet. She wrote the meeting time at the top of the page, then went to place the large-print programs by the entrance, leaving the difficult conversation for after planning.

After planning, the hub emptied by degrees. Mira carried the speaker list to the office, Cem went to collect the tea delivery, and the noise around the shared table thinned to the scrape of chairs and the hum of the old refrigerator.

Arda checked the time on his phone. “Fifteen minutes,” he said. “I’m here.”

Deniz kept her notebook open, though she did not read from it. “I’ll start with what I observed, then what it felt like, and then I want to ask what you intended.”

He nodded. “All right.”

“Last week, I returned the storeroom key at 9:12 after helping my niece with an unexpected landlord problem. I signed the log and put the key in the marked envelope. When I came back, I heard you say, ‘We need someone else at the welcome desk.’ Then you checked the latch and the key drawer. After that, your greetings to me became brief and practical. You were still warm with Mira and Cem.”

Arda looked down at his hands. He did not interrupt.

“Those are the things I know,” Deniz continued. “The meaning I gave them was that you no longer trusted me to represent the hub. I felt embarrassed first. Then ashamed and angry. I imagined that four years of organizing these events had been outweighed by one late key.”

She paused. The words sounded less overwhelming spoken aloud, but not less real.

“I’m not saying that was your intention. I’m asking what you meant.”

Arda let out a slow breath. “The sentence was about the insurer’s access review on Friday. They asked for one person to control the storeroom key while they checked the exits and equipment. I meant that we needed someone else at the welcome desk so you could handle visitor arrivals without also being responsible for the key.”

Deniz frowned slightly. “You meant someone else temporarily?”

“Yes. During the review. I should have said that.”

“The timing made it sound permanent.”

“I understand.” He glanced toward the reception area. “And when you returned the key late, I was already worried. Not because I thought you were careless. I knew you had signed it back in. But I needed to know whether the late return would affect the review schedule.”

“Why didn’t you ask me?”

Arda rubbed the edge of his clipboard. “Because I thought you were upset about the key. When you came in the next day, you looked as if you wanted to avoid me. I decided not to press you while I was overloaded.”

Deniz felt the old anger stir, this time with a different shape. “I was avoiding you because I thought you were excluding me. You took my silence as proof that I felt criticized, and then your distance made me more certain I was being excluded.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is what happened.”

The simplicity of the answer did not make the hurt disappear. It gave the hurt somewhere specific to rest.

“I can see why the late key concerned you,” Deniz said. “The insurer’s review and all the preparation were real. But your avoidance affected me. You knew me well enough to ask, and instead you let me guess.”

“You’re right.” Arda met her eyes. “I’m sorry I avoided the conversation. I was trying to prevent another difficult task from landing on the busiest week, but I made the situation worse.”

Deniz nodded once. “And I’m sorry I treated my interpretation as if it were evidence. I withdrew the welcome-desk packets instead of asking you directly. That left you with more work and gave you another reason to think I was pulling away.”

“It did,” he said. “Though I should have checked before drawing that conclusion.”

For a moment, neither of them spoke. The explanation fit the schedule, the key log, and Arda’s workload. It also left room for the part that had genuinely hurt: he had chosen efficiency over clarity, and she had filled the silence with the most painful explanation available.

Arda opened the drawer beneath the planning table and took out a blank sign-out sheet. “Let’s make the procedure clearer before Friday. Anyone taking a key writes the time out, purpose, and expected return. When it comes back, the person receiving it initials the sheet.”

“Where will the spare be?”

“In the coded lockbox inside the office, not in the reception drawer. I’ll show you the code after we finish.”

“And if the arrangement changes?” Deniz asked.

“We check in directly. No hints overheard across a room.”

A reluctant smile touched Deniz’s mouth. “That last part may be the most useful.”

Arda smiled too, but his expression remained serious. “You’re still on the welcome desk Saturday, if you want it.”

“I do. Under the new protocol.”

“Then I’ll put your name back on the packet task.”

“Please do. I’ll assemble them tonight and bring them tomorrow.”

He wrote her name on the shared task list. The small movement did not restore four years by itself. It marked a decision they had both made and could now follow.

Arda stood and showed her the lockbox, then they filled in the first line of the new sheet together: storeroom key, planning room, returned at 4:18. Deniz initialed the receiving column. Before they returned to the reception area, Arda said, “If I seem distant again, ask me what is happening. I’ll answer plainly.”

“And if I’m embarrassed or angry, I’ll name that instead of withdrawing a task.”

They went back to the table. The welcome-desk packets were still waiting, but now the assignment beside them had Deniz’s name.

By Saturday morning, the Lale Community Hub had the particular disorder of a place preparing to look welcoming. Chairs stood in uneven rows. Photographs from the oral-history project waited in labeled folders. Someone had left a coil of microphone cable beside the tea urn.

Deniz arrived early with the welcome-desk packets under one arm. Arda was at the reception counter, checking the new sign-out sheet.

“Morning,” he said. “The insurer’s representative may come through the side entrance at ten. If anyone needs the storeroom, tell me first, and we’ll record it.”

Deniz put her bag down. “Understood.”

She signed out the storeroom key, writing the time, purpose, and expected return. Arda glanced at the entry, then nodded. Nothing in his face suggested suspicion; nothing in hers required her to pretend that the earlier weeks had not happened.

At ten past ten, Mira arrived with three boxes of printed photographs. “The display boards are in the storeroom,” she said. “Can I take the key?”

Deniz looked toward Arda. “Mira needs access for the photographs. I’ve signed out the key. Do you want to handle the opening, or shall I bring the boxes while you greet the insurer?”

“I’ll greet them,” Arda said. “Take the key, and let me know when you return it.”

His answer was plain and immediate. Deniz felt a small loosening in her chest—not triumph, exactly, but the relief of not having to interpret a pause.

She and Mira carried the boxes through the side corridor. The insurer’s representative was still speaking with Arda when they returned. Deniz placed the key on the counter.

“Storeroom key returned at ten twenty-four,” she said. “The photographs are in the main room.”

Arda initialed the sheet. “Thank you.”

The open house began at eleven. Familiar faces filled the room: Cem with his camera, Nevin from the language group, two retired teachers who had contributed memories of the old market street. Deniz welcomed each person, pointed out the listening stations, and explained that the recordings would play every fifteen minutes.

Near the entrance, she noticed that the schedule said the first guided display would begin at eleven thirty. Arda was still helping the insurer examine an exit sign, while Mira was waiting beside the photographs.

“Arda,” Deniz called, keeping her voice low, “I see eleven thirty on the schedule. Are we starting then, or waiting until your review is finished?”

He checked the page. “The review should take another ten minutes. Let’s begin at eleven forty-five. I’ll make the announcement, and you can stay here.”

“Good. I’ll tell the visitors who are waiting.”

There was no defensiveness in his reply, and no careful distance in hers. The timing had been unclear; now it was not. Deniz turned to the small group by the display boards and explained the change. One visitor used the extra time to listen to an interview about the neighborhood bakery. Another asked Cem about the photographs. The delay became part of the morning rather than a problem anyone had to hide.

At eleven forty-five, Arda joined Deniz at the front. He introduced the project, and she described how the recordings had been collected. When the first story began—a woman remembering the tram conductor who knew every passenger’s name—the room settled into attentive quiet.

Later, while visitors moved between the photographs and the listening stations, Arda brought Deniz a cup of tea.

“You handled the key and the timing well,” he said.

“So did you,” Deniz replied. “You answered before I had time to invent an explanation.”

He smiled, then grew thoughtful. “I’m glad you asked.”

“So am I.” She looked toward the sign-out sheet on the counter. “The protocol helps. But it’s not doing all the work.”

“No,” Arda said. “We are.”

The words did not erase the embarrassment Deniz had carried, or the fact that she and Arda had once let silence speak for them. But throughout the afternoon, they checked in when plans shifted, named what they needed, and returned the key exactly as agreed.

At closing time, the last visitors thanked them together. Arda stacked the folders while Deniz locked the display cabinet. Their work still required attention. Trust, she understood, was not the absence of another awkward moment. It was what they did when one appeared.
