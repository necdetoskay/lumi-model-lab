The Space Between Words

By four o’clock, Deniz was at the community center’s long blue table, where they and Mina had spread out their welcome map for Saturday’s open house. The map showed the reading room, art corner, kitchen, and small garden behind the building. Deniz had drawn the paths in bright colors and made title cards for each place.

“People should be able to find everything without asking three times,” Deniz said, lining up the cards. “I’ll explain the map, too. You can talk about the garden.”

Mina smiled, but she kept glancing toward the folding screen beside the table. “Sure. The garden part is good.”

Deniz reached for the card marked READING ROOM. Mina moved first. She gathered the whole stack against her chest.

“Hey,” Deniz said. “I still need those.”

“Not yet,” Mina replied.

She carried the cards behind the folding screen. One slipped sideways, and Deniz saw a dark, wavy mark along its edge. The paper looked damp, as if it had been left near a dripping cup. Mina quickly turned the stack so the cards faced her.

“What are you doing?” Deniz asked.

“Just fixing something.” Mina’s voice was quiet. “I’ll bring them back.”

Then she disappeared behind the screen.

Deniz stood with a blue marker in one hand. The map was still on the table, but it suddenly looked unfinished in a new way. The title cards were the part Deniz had been trusted to make. Without them, the paths and pictures seemed to belong to somebody else.

Deniz waited a moment. Mina did not come out.

Maybe Mina had noticed a mistake. Maybe she wanted to change the lettering. Maybe she had decided Deniz should not explain the map after all. Deniz picked up the ruler and put it beside the blank space where the cards had been.

The last thought made Deniz’s chest feel tight. Hurt, Deniz thought. Or left out.

They did not want to accuse Mina without knowing. Still, Mina had taken the cards and said almost nothing. Deniz slid the map carefully toward the center of the table, then packed the marker into their bag.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” Deniz called.

From behind the screen came a small, “Okay.”

Deniz left the center carrying the unanswered question with them: why had Mina hidden the cards?

The next afternoon, Deniz returned to the community center with the unfinished map tucked between two pieces of cardboard. The blue table was busy with paper, markers, and the soft scrape of chairs across the floor.

Mina was behind the folding screen. Deniz could see her shoes moving back and forth beneath it.

“Hi,” Deniz said.

“Hi,” Mina answered, but she did not look around the screen.

Deniz set the map on the end of the table. The empty spaces where the title cards belonged seemed to stare back. They picked up the ruler and began measuring a path from the front door to the art corner.

A few minutes later, Mina stepped out. She held a small cloth in one hand and a card in the other. When she saw Deniz, her fingers tightened around the card.

“Oh,” she said. “You’re here.”

Deniz waited for her to explain. Mina did not. Instead, she reached toward the ruler.

“Can I have that?”

Deniz handed it over. Mina took it without asking whether Deniz still needed it, then went behind the screen again.

The ruler had been Deniz’s idea for making straight borders. Mina knew that. She also knew Deniz was there to work. Why not ask for help?

Deniz bent over the map, pretending to check the paths. From behind the screen came the rustle of paper.

“I need to put this one—” Mina began. Then she stopped.

A second voice did not answer. Mina spoke again, quieter this time.

“Deniz can’t see this yet.”

Deniz’s hand froze above the green path.

They had not meant to listen. The words had simply slipped through the gap in the screen. Deniz looked toward the room’s front desk, where Mr. Alvarez was sorting mail. No one else seemed to notice.

A moment later, Mina came out carrying the title cards. She laid them on the table, then quickly placed a sheet of brown paper over them.

“Are those mine?” Deniz asked.

“Not yet,” Mina said.

There it was again. Not yet. No explanation. No glance at Deniz’s face.

Deniz’s stomach felt twisty. The first thought arrived before they could stop it: Mina had picked somebody else to explain the map. Maybe somebody else would stand beside the display on Saturday while Deniz was left with nothing to say.

That was a guess, Deniz reminded themself. What Deniz had actually seen was Mina hiding the cards, avoiding eye contact, and saying Deniz could not see them yet. The guess still felt heavy, especially beside yesterday’s hurt.

Deniz wanted to ask Mina directly. Instead, they folded the map and carried it to a smaller table near the window.

“I’ll work over here,” Deniz said.

Mina looked up at last. Her mouth opened, but no words came out.

Deniz did not tell Mr. Alvarez or anyone else what they suspected. The thought felt too sharp to pass around when Deniz did not even know if it was true.

Still, they kept their eyes on the smaller table, away from the folding screen and the covered cards.

Deniz stayed at the small table until the green path was measured twice. The map looked neat, but it felt wrong without its title cards. The folding screen stood between Deniz and Mina like a wall made of cloth.

At last, Deniz carried the map back toward the display area. They told themself they were only checking where the map would fit. They were not snooping. The Saturday open house was close, and the display still needed space for the welcome signs.

The folding screen stood beneath a ceiling tile near the corner. A pale brown mark spread across the tile, with a darker spot in the middle. Below it, the top edge of a cardboard box was damp. Several scraps of paper rested on a chair, their corners curled and wrinkled.

Deniz touched one scrap with a fingertip. It was cool and soft. The dark edge looked like the edges of the title cards.

So Mina had a reason to move the cards.

That did not explain why she had covered them. It did not explain the words, “Deniz can’t see this yet,” or why Mina had looked away. A leak could explain hiding wet paper. It could not explain everything.

Deniz set the map beside the display board and crouched to see whether the center space was still free. A sheet of paper had slipped partly out from behind the screen. Only the corner showed, but there was enough to see a quick pencil drawing.

It was the welcome map.

In Mina’s sketch, the map sat in the middle of the display, with arrows pointing toward the front desk, the art corner, and the reading room. Small boxes surrounded it. One box was labeled with a title card shape. Another had a line that looked like a place for Deniz’s name.

Deniz leaned closer, then stopped. The rest of the drawing was hidden. They did not pull the paper out.

Mina had not drawn the map off to the side. She had put it at the center.

A warm, uncertain feeling stirred in Deniz’s chest. Maybe Mina was trying to protect the cards and make the display better. Or maybe the sketch was old, and Mina had changed her mind afterward.

Then Deniz remembered last week at the blue table. They had been lettering a card when Mina suggested a different border.

“I want to handle the labels myself,” Deniz had said. “I’m being careful with them.”

Deniz had meant the letters. The lines had to be straight, and the words had to fit. But Mina might have heard something else: Don’t touch my cards. Don’t change them.

Deniz picked up the map. The two stories bumped against each other in their mind. Mina could have been protecting the damp cards. Mina could also have thought Deniz did not want help. Neither story answered why she had kept quiet.

Deniz looked at the covered cards behind the screen. Guessing had carried them as far as it could. To know what Mina meant, Deniz would have to ask her—not with an accusation, but with the part they knew was true.

They carried the map back to the table and waited for Mina to come out.

Deniz waited until the room grew quiet. The map lay on the table, its green paths and blue rooms bright beneath the afternoon light. Behind the folding screen, paper rustled. Mina was still working.

Deniz pulled a small square of scrap paper from the supply box and found a pencil. At the top, they wrote:

What happened:
The cards disappeared. Mina covered them. She said, “Deniz can’t see this yet.”

Deniz stared at the words. They were facts, even if they felt sharp.

Under that, Deniz wrote:
Feeling:
Left out. Hurt.

The pencil pressed hard enough to leave a dent in the paper. Deniz rubbed the dent with a thumb, then added one more line.

Question:
What did you mean, and why didn’t you tell me?

It was a better question than Are you replacing me? That question already contained an answer Deniz could not prove.

Deniz turned the scrap over and tried the words quietly.

“I felt left out when the cards disappeared.”

The sentence sounded strange at first. It did not say Mina was mean. It did not say the sketch, the leak, or the damp paper solved everything. It only said what had happened inside Deniz when the cards vanished.

Deniz tried again, a little louder. “I saw you take the cards behind the screen, and then you covered them. I felt left out. Can you tell me what was going on?”

A chair scraped behind the screen.

Deniz folded the scrap once, then unfolded it. Their stomach wobbled. Waiting had been easier when they were guessing. A guess could be pushed away or held tightly. A real answer might do something different.

Mina stepped around the screen. She carried a stack of cards clipped together with a wooden peg. The paper looked dry now, though the corners were slightly curled. A few new marks showed along the top edges, but Deniz could not see what they made.

Mina noticed the map on the table. Her hand tightened around the cards.

“I thought you were angry with me,” she said.

Then she stopped, as if the rest of the sentence had gone missing.

Deniz looked at the cards, then at Mina. The hurt feeling was still there, but it had changed shape. It was no longer a solid wall. It was a question waiting between them.

“I wasn’t trying to be angry,” Deniz said. “But I was hurt.”

Mina glanced toward the folding screen. “I know. I just thought…”

She stopped again.

Deniz touched the folded note in their pocket. “Can we talk about it?”

Mina nodded, but neither of them moved toward the table yet. The cards stayed in Mina’s hands, and the unfinished question stayed between them.

Mina set the cards on the project table. “The leak was right above the screen,” she said. “When I saw the edges getting wet, I moved them before they were ruined. I put them under the fan in the supply room.”

Deniz touched one curled corner. “That explains why you moved them. But why cover them? And why say I couldn’t see them?”

Mina looked down at the cards. “Because I changed the titles.” She turned the top card over.

Instead of the old block letters, a bright blue border curved around the words. Tiny arrows pointed toward the map. “I thought the new design would look better,” Mina said. “But you told me, ‘I want to handle the labels myself.’ I thought that meant you didn’t want me changing anything.”

Deniz remembered the afternoon clearly now: the careful lettering, the ruler lined up with each card, and the worry that one crooked word would spoil the whole display. “I meant I wanted to write the words carefully,” Deniz said. “Not that you couldn’t help. I should have said that.”

Mina picked at the wooden peg. “Then I worried you were angry when you saw me hide the cards. I kept working because I wanted to fix everything before showing you.”

“I thought you were hiding them because you had chosen someone else to present with you,” Deniz said. The thought sounded smaller when spoken aloud. “I saw the cards disappear, and you wouldn’t look at me. Those things made my guess feel true.”

Mina nodded slowly. “And I heard your words and guessed what you meant. Then I saw you working at the window table and guessed again.”

For a moment, the room held only the soft hum of the fan.

“I’m sorry I treated my guess like it was a fact,” Deniz said.

“I’m sorry I kept you out of the work instead of asking,” Mina replied. “And I’m sorry I changed the cards without telling you.”

Deniz unfolded the scrap note and placed it beside the map. “Can we choose the labels together now?”

Mina’s shoulders loosened. “Yes.”

They spread the dried cards across the table. Deniz kept the clearest letters and trimmed the curled edges. Mina added the blue borders and small arrows, checking each one before moving to the next. When they disagreed about a title, they held it above the map and tried both places. One version made the path confusing, so they changed it.

Soon, the map had labels for the reading corner, art table, snack station, and quiet room. Deniz wrote the final words while Mina steadied the ruler. Then Mina placed the cards, and Deniz checked that each arrow pointed to the right spot.

“What should we each explain on Saturday?” Mina asked.

Deniz pointed to the left side. “I’ll show the entrance, reading corner, and art table.”

“And I’ll explain the snack station and quiet room,” Mina said. “Then we can switch for questions.”

Deniz looked at the map. Their names were written together at the bottom, beneath the title: Welcome to Our Community Center.

“It’s ready,” Deniz said.

Mina smiled. “Together?”

“Together.”

They carried the finished map to the display board and fastened it beneath the clean, dry spot. The blue arrows made the paths easy to follow. Behind them, the folding screen stood open, no longer hiding anything.

On Saturday morning, the community center hummed with voices and footsteps. Sunlight fell across the welcome map, making the blue arrows shine. Deniz stood beside it while Mina greeted the first family through the door.

“Welcome!” Deniz said. “The reading corner is this way, and the art table is beside it.” Deniz traced the paths without blocking the labels.

Mina pointed to the other side. “The snack station is near the back wall. The quiet room is past the tall plants.”

A boy about Deniz’s age leaned closer to the map. “Where do we get name tags?”

Deniz glanced at Mina. Mina pointed toward a small table near the entrance. “Right there,” she said. “Deniz can show you.”

Deniz led the boy to the table, then hurried back when the next visitors arrived. Sometimes Deniz spoke first, and sometimes Mina did. When someone asked about the craft supplies, Mina explained. When a grandmother wondered whether the reading corner had books for younger children, Deniz knew exactly which shelf to point toward. They passed questions between them like a ball, without dropping any.

Near the middle of the morning, Mina reached toward the reading-corner card. Its lower edge had lifted slightly from the display board. She touched the corner, then stopped with her fingers still there.

Deniz felt a quick pinch of worry. For half a second, the old guess returned: Mina was changing something without saying so.

Then Deniz looked at the loose card and at Mina’s hand. The worry was a feeling, not an answer.

“What do you need?” Deniz asked.

Mina turned to them. “The corner is peeling up. I need to press it flat, but I don’t want to cover the arrow. Can you hold the map steady?”

“Sure.” Deniz placed one hand beside the title while Mina smoothed the card. Together, they pressed a fresh strip of tape along its edge. The arrow still pointed clearly toward the reading corner.

“Thanks for asking,” Mina said.

“Thanks for telling me,” Deniz replied.

A visitor wearing a yellow scarf came over and studied the map. “Did you two make this?”

“We did,” said Mina.

“Together,” Deniz added.

The visitor smiled. “It makes the whole building easy to understand.”

By lunchtime, nearly every blue arrow had guided someone to the right place. Deniz and Mina stood beside the map, tired but pleased. Their names rested beneath the title, side by side.

Mina bumped Deniz’s shoulder gently. “Ready for one more group?”

Deniz looked at the open room, the clear labels, and Mina waiting beside them. “Together?”

“Together.”

They turned to welcome the next visitors. When the open house ended, they carried the leftover tape back to the supply table and left the center through the familiar glass doors, sharing a relieved smile. Nothing felt perfectly certain forever. But beside Mina, Deniz felt trusted again—and knew where to begin when a worry appeared.
