The Space Between Words

At fifty, Deniz knew the rhythm of the community hub almost by sound.

On Thursday evenings, the front door sighed open every few minutes, the kettle clicked off in the shared kitchen, and the repair room filled with the small, satisfying noises of useful work: a screwdriver turning, a zipper being tested, a chair leg tapped gently into place. Deniz had coordinated the weekly repair evening for three years. They knew who preferred the bright table near the window, who needed tea before discussing a broken appliance, and which volunteers could be trusted with a delicate hinge.

The hub was familiar without being stagnant. Its notice screens showed the week's workshops, and a new digital sign-in tablet stood beside the old corkboard. People Deniz knew well crossed the room with greetings and questions. Leyla, Deniz's working partner, was checking the printed notice for Thursday's session.

Deniz paused beside her. The notice listed the time, the room, and the names of the volunteers. Leyla's name was there. So were Omar's and Sanna's.

Deniz's name was not.

For a moment, Deniz wondered whether they had missed a second page. They read the notice again, slowly. The omission remained, plain as a blank space at the bottom.

“Leyla, the new notice is up,” called Sanna from the tool cabinet. “Should I put the old one in recycling?”

“Not yet,” Leyla answered. She lifted a folder from the planning shelf. Its blue label read DENIZ—REPAIR EVENING in Deniz's careful block letters.

Deniz watched her carry it toward the storage cupboards at the back of the hub.

That folder held the rota, supplier contacts, notes about accessibility, and the list of people who had asked for help. It was not a personal keepsake. It was working material, and moving it without a word felt significant.

Leyla returned, but instead of coming to the noticeboard, she stopped near the half-open office door. A man from the neighborhood joined her. Their voices dropped. Deniz caught only fragments: “next month,” “coverage,” and “we need to be realistic.” When Leyla glanced across the room, she saw Deniz looking and gave a small, distracted lift of her hand before turning back.

The separate details pressed together in Deniz's mind: the missing name, the folder in storage, the private conversation. Perhaps Leyla had decided the repair evening needed someone else. Perhaps the decision had already been discussed with everyone except Deniz.

A hot, tight feeling rose beneath Deniz's collar. They did not want to make a scene, and they did not want to ask a question while their voice might reveal more than they intended. So they picked up the box of replacement cables and carried it to the side table, away from the office door and the noticeboard.

For the rest of the evening, Deniz answered practical questions and checked the tools, but left the larger decisions to Leyla. That withdrawal felt controlled. It also felt like being pushed out before anyone had said the words.

When the last volunteer left, Deniz remained by the side table, looking toward the storage cupboards. The repair evening still depended on their planning. The partnership with Leyla mattered more than one notice, even if the notice seemed to say something larger.

Deniz took the box to the supply shelf and made a quiet decision: protect the work, and protect the partnership if possible. But first, they would need to understand what had happened. For now, Leyla's intention was still hidden behind an omitted name, a moved folder, and a conversation Deniz had not been invited to hear.

At home after the repair evening, Deniz made tea, opened the notebook they used for schedules, and wrote a heading across a blank page: WHAT HAPPENED.

Under it, they listed the details.

The new notice named Leyla, Omar, and Sanna. It did not name Deniz.

Leyla moved the blue repair-evening folder from the planning shelf to storage.

Leyla spoke privately with a neighborhood man. Deniz heard “next month,” “coverage,” and “we need to be realistic.”

Leyla gave a distracted wave but did not explain anything.

Deniz read the list twice. It was an accurate account, but it felt strangely thin. The facts stood on the page like furniture in an empty room. They did not say why any of it had happened.

On the next line, Deniz wrote: WHAT I TOLD MYSELF.

They hesitated, then added: Leyla has decided I am no longer wanted. She has arranged for someone else to take over. The private conversation was about replacing me.

Seeing the sentences written down changed their weight. They were not observations. They were conclusions built around observations.

Deniz leaned back. The feeling had been hot and tight at the hub, but now they could name it more accurately. Hurt, certainly. Embarrassment too—the embarrassment of possibly having been discussed as a problem while standing only a few metres away. Beneath both was fear: not fear of being unable to manage the work, but of losing a partnership that had become dependable over three years.

They wrote those words as well. HURT. EMBARRASSED. AFRAID OF LOSING TRUST.

Then Deniz opened their phone and searched the recent messages with Leyla. Near the end of the previous month, they found a message they had nearly forgotten.

I need some space after this month. I can’t keep absorbing every extra task.

Deniz remembered writing it late at night, after a week of supplier calls, two cancellations, and a long discussion about whether the hub could afford new soldering irons. They had meant: I need fewer additional jobs. I still want to coordinate the repair evening, but not at the cost of every free evening.

The message itself did not say that.

Deniz read Leyla’s reply beneath it: Understood. Let’s make the next month lighter.

That reply could have meant several things. Leyla might have agreed to protect Deniz’s time. She might have understood that Deniz wanted to step away from coordination. Or she might have planned to ask what “space” meant and then become distracted by the grant deadline and everything else at the hub.

The old suspicion had not disappeared. The omitted name still hurt. The moved folder still seemed too deliberate to ignore. Yet Deniz could now see evidence pointing in two directions: toward exclusion, and toward a clumsy attempt to respond to a request Deniz had made too broadly.

They closed the message thread and returned to the notebook. At the bottom of the page, Deniz wrote: Find out before deciding.

They would not apologize for wanting a sustainable workload. They would not accuse Leyla of an intention they had not established. Tomorrow, Deniz would return to the familiar hub and compare the dated notice with the ordinary repair-evening records before deciding what to ask. The records might clarify timing or practical changes, even if they could not tell Deniz what Leyla had intended.

The goal remained the same: preserve the partnership and keep a workable role in the repair evening. Withdrawal might protect Deniz from another uncomfortable moment, but it would not protect the trust they still hoped to keep.

The next afternoon, Deniz returned to the hub with the notebook in their bag and no plan to corner Leyla between tasks. The familiar place was quieter than it had been during the repair evening. Sunlight fell through the high windows onto the shared worktables, and the charging lockers blinked patiently along one wall. Omar was sorting donated cables near the entrance. He lifted a hand in greeting, and Deniz returned it, grateful for the ordinary exchange.

Instead of going straight to the planning shelf, Deniz opened the hub’s shared calendar on the wall tablet. The repair-evening notice was dated for the first Thursday of the coming month. It listed Leyla as lead, with Omar and Sanna assisting. Deniz’s name was absent.

The date mattered. This was not a notice for the evening just completed, nor an old draft left behind. It was a new arrangement, or at least a notice that looked like one.

Deniz copied the date into the notebook and walked to the records cabinet. The shared rota was kept in a clear folder beside the printer, with the digital version open on the nearby monitor. Deniz checked the entries for the weeks around the notice.

Leyla had taken two additional daytime shifts at the hub. Beside one was a note: urgent grant deadline—covering intake and budget figures. Another entry showed her scheduled to meet the electrician about the repair room. The rota explained why Leyla had seemed distracted and why she might have been discussing coverage with someone after the last evening. It did not explain why Deniz’s name had disappeared from the notice.

Deniz carried the notebook toward the repair room. A strip of yellow tape crossed the doorway, marked CEILING WORK—ACCESS LIMITED. Inside, the tables had been pushed together beneath the uncovered section of ceiling. A small maintenance sheet was clipped to the door.

Ceiling inspection and minor repair: Tuesday and Wednesday. Move stored materials from upper shelves before contractors arrive. Return folders after work is complete.

Below that, in the hub manager’s handwriting, was a list of shelves to clear. Planning materials—repair evening. Supplier records. First-aid inventory.

Deniz looked through the narrow window in the door. The blue folder was visible in the storage alcove, placed on a shelf with the other cleared materials. Its label faced outward. The folder had not been hidden or discarded. The note gave a practical reason for moving it, and Deniz accepted that reason. Still, the folder had been moved without a word, and the omission from the notice remained.

Back at the worktable, Deniz opened the notebook to a clean page and drew three headings: OBSERVATIONS, FEELING, QUESTION.

Under observations, they wrote: The notice dated next month lists Leyla, Omar, and Sanna, but not me. My blue planning folder was moved before ceiling work. The rota shows Leyla covering an urgent grant deadline. The maintenance note explains the folder’s location.

Under feeling, Deniz wrote: I felt pushed aside and embarrassed. I am still worried that my role has been changed without a direct conversation.

They paused before the final heading. The question had to be precise enough to invite an answer, not shaped like a verdict.

Under question, Deniz wrote: Did you understand my message about needing space as a decision to step back from coordinating the repair evening? If so, what arrangement did you intend?

Deniz read the three sections together. The records had narrowed the uncertainty. They had not settled it. The folder move had a clear practical explanation, and Leyla’s workload was real. Neither fact proved what Leyla had meant by leaving Deniz’s name off the notice.

Deniz closed the notebook and placed a finger beneath the question. Before speaking with Leyla, they would keep to what they had seen, say what it had felt like, and ask what Leyla had understood. The repair evening still needed a workable coordinator. So did the partnership.

Deniz kept the notebook open while the hub settled into its afternoon rhythm. A delivery cart rattled past the worktables. Someone in the kitchen tested the new coffee machine, and it answered with a brief mechanical sigh. These were ordinary sounds, but Deniz felt less ordinary than they had a few minutes earlier.

The question on the page was clear. Sending it would still require care.

Deniz did not want to write, You are replacing me. That was an interpretation, not an observation. They also did not want to pretend the notice had not stung. A careful message could hold both facts: something had hurt, and its meaning was not yet known.

They opened the hub's messaging system and found Leyla's name. Before typing, Deniz read the three headings once more.

Observations. Feeling. Question.

Then they wrote:

"Hi, Leyla. I noticed that the notice for next month's repair evening lists you, Omar, and Sanna, but not me. I also saw that my blue planning folder had been moved to storage before the ceiling work. I understand now why the folder was moved, but seeing these changes together hurt, and I felt pushed aside. I want to understand what you intended. Did you read my message about needing space after this month as a decision to step back from coordinating the repair evening? Could we talk privately after closing?"

Deniz read it twice. The message named what they had seen and how it had felt. It did not announce Leyla's motive, demand an apology, or recruit an audience. It left room for an answer that might be difficult to hear.

They added one final sentence: "I would like us to work out a clear arrangement rather than leave it to assumptions."

Their thumb hovered over Send. At fifty, Deniz had learned that a well-considered message was not the same as a risk-free one. Clarity could make a conversation possible, but it could not control the other person's response. They sent it.

For several minutes, the screen showed only the delivered mark. Deniz put the phone face down and began sorting the supplier receipts on the worktable. They had finished one stack when the phone buzzed.

Leyla had replied.

"Hi, Deniz. Thank you for telling me directly. I am sorry the notice and the folder move felt like being pushed out. The folder was moved for the ceiling work, as you saw, but I understand that I should have told you. And I realize the notice may look like a decision about your role. I did not want to discuss this in the middle of the hub. Yes, I can talk after closing. Would the small meeting room suit you?"

Deniz let out a breath they had not noticed holding. Leyla's message did not settle the question. It did not say what she had understood from the request for space, or whether Deniz was still expected to coordinate. But it accepted the need for a conversation without turning the message into a contest.

Deniz answered, "The small meeting room works. I will stay until closing, and then we can talk for half an hour. I would like to discuss the notice, the coordination role, and what each of us understood."

Leyla's response came soon after. "Agreed. Half an hour, and we will make a practical plan."

The wording steadied Deniz. A practical plan did not promise that the conversation would be comfortable. It did promise that the repair evening, and the partnership behind it, could be discussed as shared work rather than as a verdict already delivered.

Across the room, Leyla appeared in the doorway carrying a stack of forms. She glanced toward Deniz, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Then Leyla gave a small acknowledging nod. Deniz returned it.

They went on with their separate tasks. The distance between them had not vanished, and the notice still waited on the wall with Deniz's name missing. But now there was a time, a place, and a question neither of them could mistake. After closing, they would sit down privately and find out what the other had meant.

After closing, the hub changed character. The worktables were cleared, the soldering irons unplugged, and the screens above the reception desk had gone dark. In the small meeting room, the ceiling light made a quiet circle over the table.

Leyla arrived with two mugs of tea. Deniz had brought the notebook, though they left it closed between them.

“Thank you for staying,” Leyla said.

“I wanted us to talk without an audience.”

Leyla nodded and sat down. For a moment, the only sound was the ventilation system moving air through the building.

Deniz opened the notebook. “I wrote down what I observed so I wouldn’t turn an interpretation into a fact. The notice for next month lists you, Omar, and Sanna, but not me. My blue planning folder was moved to storage. I saw you speaking privately with someone about coverage and being realistic.”

Leyla listened without interrupting.

“Seeing those things together,” Deniz continued, “I felt pushed aside. Hurt, and embarrassed too. I thought you had decided to replace me without telling me. I know now that the folder was moved for the ceiling inspection. That explains the folder, but not the notice. I want to understand what you understood from my message about needing space.”

Leyla wrapped both hands around her mug. “I understood it as more than needing fewer extra tasks. I heard you saying that you could not continue coordinating the repair evening after this month.”

Deniz took that in. “The wording did leave room for that reading.”

“It did,” Leyla said. “You wrote, ‘I need some space after this month. I can’t keep absorbing every extra task.’ I was looking at the grant deadline, the rota, and all the things that had been landing on you. I thought I was responding to what you needed.”

“By changing the notice.”

“Yes.” Leyla lowered her eyes. “I asked Omar and Sanna whether they could cover the first Thursday. Then I made the notice quickly because I had to submit the grant figures. I meant to speak with you before posting it. I did not.”

Deniz felt the old tightness in their chest, but it no longer had to carry the whole story. “So you were trying to make the workload lighter, but you made a decision about my role without checking.”

“That is what happened.” Leyla looked directly at them. “I was wrong to assume. Your message gave me evidence for reducing your responsibilities, but not for removing you from coordination. I should have asked what you meant.”

Deniz traced the edge of the notebook. “And I should have written that I wanted fewer extra tasks, not less connection to the repair evening. I can accept that my words were unclear. I can’t accept responsibility for an assumption you kept to yourself.”

“You shouldn’t have to.”

The answer was plain, without an attempt to make the discomfort disappear. Deniz let out a slow breath.

Leyla reached into her folder and took out the revised rota. “Would you still like to coordinate?”

“Yes,” Deniz said. “But not by absorbing every gap. I can handle the planning, the opening checklist, and the first hour of each repair evening. After that, someone else needs to be the point person for late arrivals and closing.”

Leyla studied the page. “I can take closing on the first Thursday. Omar has offered to handle late arrivals on the third Thursday. Sanna can update the supply list. That leaves you coordinating the schedule and the repair-room plan, without carrying the whole evening.”

“That sounds workable.”

“I’ll put your name back on the notice,” Leyla said. “Not just as a correction, but with the role stated clearly: Deniz, coordination and repair-room planning.”

The phrase settled something in Deniz that an apology alone could not have reached. A role restored in writing was different from being told privately that they were still wanted.

“There’s one more thing,” Deniz said. “If either of us thinks a responsibility needs to change, let’s ask directly before changing the rota or posting a notice. No shorthand. No relying on one sentence to carry more meaning than it can hold.”

Leyla gave a small, tired smile. “Agreed. A check-in before a change goes public.”

“And if one of us says we need space, we ask: space from what?”

“That would have helped.”

“It would have.”

Leyla stood and gathered the old notice from the table where she had placed it. “Would you come with me to replace it?”

They walked together to the noticeboard. The hub was nearly empty now, its polished concrete floor reflecting the lights from the windows. Leyla removed the paper with the three names. Deniz watched as she opened the posting system on her tablet, changed the listing, and printed a new copy at reception.

The replacement notice read: “Weekly Repair Evening: Deniz—coordination and repair-room planning; Leyla—closing and support; Omar—late-arrival support; Sanna—supplies.”

Leyla handed Deniz the pushpins. Deniz fixed the four corners to the board.

Neither of them pretended that the first notice had not hurt, or that the last few days had been simple. But the new notice said what the old one had failed to say. Their arrangement was visible, specific, and chosen together.

Back in the meeting room, Leyla picked up her tea. “I’m glad you asked me.”

“I’m glad you answered.”

They sat for a few more minutes, reviewing the dates and responsibilities aloud. When they left the room, the hub was quiet, but the silence between them no longer carried an unanswered verdict.

The next Thursday, Deniz arrived at the hub twenty minutes before the repair evening began. At fifty, they had learned to arrive early not from nervousness but from respect for the work. A room ran better when its tools, people, and expectations were ready before the first question was asked.

The revised notice was still on the board. Deniz checked the opening list, tested the card reader, and placed the sign-in tablet beside the box of spare screws. Leyla came in carrying a coil of extension cable.

“Before we put this out,” she said, lifting the cable, “can we decide whether it belongs near the sewing table or the charging station? The sewing table is likely to need it first.”

Deniz looked at the room. “Near the sewing table, but let’s leave a note on the supply cart so Omar knows where it went.”

Leyla took a marker and wrote the note before taping it to the cart. It was a small change, hardly worth a meeting, but they had agreed to confirm changes aloud. Neither of them treated that agreement as excessive.

Omar arrived next, followed by Sanna with two boxes of donated zippers. Familiar greetings moved through the room. Someone turned on the lamps above the worktables. Someone else filled the kettle. The hub settled into its ordinary rhythm: tools clicking, chairs shifting, people calling across the room to ask whether a loose hinge could be tightened without replacing the whole cabinet.

Deniz handled the opening questions and then moved to the repair-room schedule. Leyla took the closing checklist from the noticeboard and marked the tasks she would cover later. Their work fitted together without either person hovering over the other.

Near the middle of the evening, Deniz noticed that Leyla had gone quiet. She stood by the tablet, looking at the rota with her arms folded. The old feeling arrived quickly—a small tightening beneath Deniz's ribs, followed by the thought that something had changed without them being told.

For a moment, Deniz nearly returned to the worktable. Silence had once seemed to confirm the worst explanation. But what did they actually know? Leyla was looking at the rota. She had not said that the arrangement was changing. She had not moved Deniz's materials or called anyone aside. The feeling was real; its explanation was not yet evidence.

Deniz walked over. “You’ve been looking at the third-Thursday line. Is something different?”

Leyla glanced up. “Yes, but not about your role. Omar just told me he may be ten minutes late next week. I was checking whether I could shift the late-arrival handover.”

“Can we talk through it before changing the rota?”

“Of course.” Leyla pointed to the page. “I can cover the first ten minutes, and Omar can take over when he arrives. I’ll write that in the notes rather than changing your coordination line.”

Deniz felt the tightness ease. “That works. Thanks for saying what you were checking.”

Leyla gave them a direct look. “Thanks for asking instead of having to guess.”

The sentence held no accusation. It did, however, remember what they had discussed.

Later, when the first wave of visitors had finished, Deniz stood beside a man repairing the wheel on a shopping trolley. The man had attended the hub for years, and they talked about which parts could be cleaned and which needed replacing. Across the room, Leyla helped Sanna sort supplies. Omar arrived with an apology and took over the late-arrival table exactly as planned.

At nine o’clock, Deniz closed the sign-in list. They were tired, but the tiredness felt measured: the kind that belonged to a useful evening, not to carrying every responsibility in the building. They handed Leyla the closing checklist and kept the next week's planning task for themselves.

Before leaving, Leyla checked the noticeboard. “Anything to confirm?”

Deniz looked at the posted roles, then at the notes beside the rota. “Only that we keep doing this when a change is small. Small changes are where people make large assumptions.”

Leyla nodded. “Agreed.”

Deniz switched off the worktable lamps. The hurt from the previous week had not vanished as if it had never existed. It had become part of the history of their partnership, something they could remember without letting it speak for every quiet moment. They locked the supply cabinet, said goodnight to the others, and left the hub with Leyla beside them—still responsible, still independent, and no longer working alone inside an unanswered question.
