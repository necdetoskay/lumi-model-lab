The Bench in the Quiet Garden

The lake was making little silver wrinkles in the afternoon sun. Elif watched each one travel toward the shore, growing wider and softer until it disappeared.

Beside the community garden, bean leaves trembled on their vines. A yellow butterfly rested on one leaf, then opened and closed its wings.

Narin sat on the quiet bench beneath the willow tree. She was repairing the bird feeder with a short piece of wire. Elif liked watching Narin’s careful fingers. They twisted the wire once, tested it, and twisted it again.

“Can you hold this?” Narin asked.

Elif held the feeder steady. A sparrow hopped along the bench, tilted its head, and flew to the garden fence.

“There,” said Narin. “That should keep the seeds from spilling.”

Elif looked at the feeder. “It’s fixed.”

Narin smiled, but then her smile became thoughtful. She set the wire beside her.

“Elif, I have something to tell you. My family will be moving to another district soon. Not today, and not tomorrow, but soon.”

The lake kept shining. Somewhere behind them, a watering can tipped over with a soft clunk.

Elif heard every word. Another district. Moving soon.

She looked down at her shoes. A tiny green sprout had pushed through the soil beside her toe. She counted its two round leaves. Then she counted the cracks in the path.

Narin did not ask her to answer.

A sparrow landed on the feeder. It pecked once, twice, and lifted into the pale sky. Elif watched until its wings became a small dark flicker and then were gone.

She placed the feeder carefully on the bench.

“See you,” Narin said gently.

Elif nodded. She walked home past the beans and the lake, carrying a tight, unnamed feeling beneath her ribs. Behind her, the quiet bench waited under the willow tree.

The next afternoon, Elif went back to the community garden.

The willow leaves whispered above the quiet bench. The lake shone between the branches, and the repaired bird feeder hung where Narin had placed it.

Elif sat down and found a fallen reed beside her shoe. She picked it up and rolled it between her fingers.

When she thought about Narin moving, the reed bent. Elif straightened it. Then she twisted it again.

Across the garden, Narin was lining up small seed packets. She wrote on each one with a dark pencil: BEANS, LETTUCE, MARIGOLDS. Her handwriting leaned slightly to the right.

She looked up and smiled at Elif. “These are for next season,” she said. “I’m labeling them before I forget what is what.”

Elif watched a tiny fleck of soil fall from a packet. She kept turning the reed.

Narin placed the packets in a wooden box. “The garden will still be here after we move,” she said. “You can come by whenever you like. I may come back to help with the seedlings, too.”

Elif looked at the rows of green plants. Some were small enough to fit beneath her palm. Others had opened wide leaves that caught the sunlight.

Narin did not ask Elif what she thought. She only picked up another packet and began writing.

A breeze moved across the lake. The reed slipped from Elif’s fingers and landed near the bench leg.

Elif picked it up. Its twisted middle was marked with little pale lines. She studied those lines for a while. They were not leaves, or cracks in the path, or letters. They were simply what her hands had made while she was thinking.

She laid the reed beside the bench, where the willow’s shadow reached the ground.

Narin finished the packet and tucked it into the box. “Would you like to see the marigold seeds?”

Elif nodded.

Together, they leaned over the box. The seeds were small, brown, and quiet. Elif did not say goodbye. She did not say that she was worried.

But she stayed beside Narin, listening to the lake and noticing the steady feeling in her hands.

The next day, Elif carried a small square of folded paper to the birdwatching area beside the lake.

It was quieter there than in the garden. Reeds whispered at the water’s edge, and the repaired feeder hung from a low branch near the path. Elif sat on a flat stone and watched two sparrows hop toward it.

One sparrow landed first. It pecked at a seed, then flew to the willow. The other sparrow took its place. After a moment, the first bird returned, and the second moved aside.

Elif followed their small movements with her eyes. They did not stay beside each other every second. Still, they kept coming back to the same feeder.

She looked across the water toward the garden bench. Narin might move to another district, but the bench would still be beneath the willow. The feeder would still hang there. Perhaps people could go away and still find a way to return.

Elif opened the paper. On the front, she drew the bench with its crooked wooden seat. She added the feeder, the lake, and three long lines for the willow branches. The first branch came out too thick, so she turned the paper over and began again on the other side.

Inside the card, she wrote:

I will miss you.

Elif read the words. Her fingers pressed the paper flat.

Then she tried another sentence.

I am sad you are moving.

She crossed it out, not because it was untrue, but because the dark line made the words hard to see. She wrote them again underneath in smaller letters.

At the bottom, she added:

I hope we can meet in the garden.

Elif paused. The sentence felt like a small bridge—only a few words, but wide enough for her to step onto.

She read all three lines softly. Her voice sounded thin beside the lake, yet it did not disappear.

A sparrow lifted from the feeder. The other waited on the branch, then followed.

Elif folded the card carefully and held it against her chest. She was not ready to give it to Narin today. But she had made the card herself, and the words inside were hers.

Elif returned to the quiet bench beneath the willow with the card tucked inside her sweater pocket.

The lake was almost still. A small ripple traveled from the reeds to the shore, growing wider and wider until it touched the bench’s reflection. Elif watched it disappear. Then she took out the card.

“I will miss you,” she whispered.

Her chest felt tight at first. She placed one hand on her stomach and breathed in slowly through her nose. The willow leaves rustled. She breathed out. After a few more breaths, her shoulders dropped a little.

Narin came along the garden path carrying a pair of gloves and a small trowel. “I need to put these away before the afternoon gets too warm,” she said.

Elif stood. Her fingers found the card. She held it out.

Narin looked at the folded paper, then at Elif. She did not reach for it right away.

Elif’s hand trembled. The words inside the card were ready, but the rest of her seemed to be waiting behind a closed door.

“I…” Elif began. She looked at the lake, then back at Narin. “I need one more moment.”

“All right,” Narin said.

She sat on the other end of the bench, leaving a comfortable space between them. She did not ask what Elif wanted to say. She did not take the card.

For a while, they listened to the water.

Then Narin glanced toward the garden beds. “The seedlings look thirsty. Would you like to help me water them?”

Elif nodded.

They carried the watering can together. Tiny lettuce leaves lifted from the dark soil. Bean vines curled around their thin sticks, reaching higher than they had the day before. Elif poured slowly, making a shining half-moon around each plant.

The card stayed safe in her pocket.

She had not given it away. She had not run home, either. When Narin passed her the can, Elif took it with both hands. The water trembled inside, then grew still as she did.

The next afternoon, Elif returned to the bench beneath the willow. Narin was already there, filling the repaired feeder with seeds.

Two sparrows landed on the edge. One hopped aside while the other pecked at a seed. Then they traded places. Elif watched their small, careful movements. They came close, moved apart, and came close again.

Her hand rested over the card in her pocket. She breathed in slowly, as she had beside the seedlings. The air smelled of damp soil and lake water. When she breathed out, the tight feeling in her chest loosened just enough.

“Narin,” she said.

Narin turned toward her but did not hurry her.

Elif took out the card. The front showed the willow, the bench, the feeder, and a strip of blue lake. She held it with both hands and gave it to Narin.

Narin looked at the picture first. Then she opened the card.

“I will miss you,” Elif said. Her voice shook, but she kept going. “I feel sad that you are moving. I know the new district is not very far, but it will be different.”

Narin listened quietly.

Elif looked down at her shoes. “I hope we can meet here in the garden sometimes.”

The words rested between them, small and honest.

“I will miss you too,” Narin said. “And I would like to meet here.” She touched the edge of the card carefully. “Thank you for telling me.”

Elif’s eyes felt warm. She was still sad. Saying the words had not made the move disappear. But the feeling no longer seemed locked behind a closed door.

Narin sat beside her, leaving the familiar comfortable space. “My boxes will be gone by the end of the week,” she said. “Would next Saturday morning work for our first garden visit after I move?”

Elif pictured the seedling beds, the feeder, and the lake shining behind them. Saturday morning felt close enough to hold.

“Yes,” she said. Then, after a moment, she added, “We can check the bean vines.”

“And the sparrows,” said Narin.

They watched the two birds return to the feeder. Elif tucked her hands beneath her knees. The sadness was still there, quiet as a shadow beside her. So was something steadier: a picture of this bench, this garden, and a Saturday they had chosen together.

On the Saturday after Narin moved, Elif sat on the quiet bench beneath the willow. The space beside her was empty. She noticed it first as a pale patch of sunlight on the wooden seat, then as the place where Narin usually set her garden gloves.

Elif’s chest felt tight, but not as tight as before. She let the feeling be there. Across the lake, a ripple slipped through the shining water and disappeared.

A little white note waited in Elif’s pocket. Narin had left it with Elif’s family the evening before.

I’ll be there Saturday morning, it said. Bring your bean-vine expert eyes.

Elif smiled a tiny smile. She placed the note beside the handmade card’s place in her drawer, where she kept the picture she remembered: the willow, the bench, the feeder, and the blue strip of lake. Then she hurried to the garden.

Two sparrows were at the feeder. One pecked at a seed while the other waited on the bench. When the first bird hopped away, the second took its turn.

“Your turn,” Elif whispered.

The sparrows shared the feeder without making a fuss. Elif watched until she heard footsteps on the garden path.

Narin waved from the gate. Her coat was different, and a new street was now part of her mornings, but her wave was the same.

“Elif!”

“Narin!” Elif ran to meet her. Then she slowed, remembering the seedlings. “Come see. The bean vines grew taller.”

Narin followed her between the beds. Elif showed her the newest leaves, the curling tendrils, and one tiny flower hiding under a broad leaf.

“You were right,” Narin said. “They do need a place to hold on to.”

Elif touched the wooden stick beside the vine. “They found one.”

After watering the beds, they returned to the bench. For a while, they sat without talking. The empty space between them no longer felt quite so wide. It was still there, and Elif still missed having Narin nearby every afternoon. But Narin was beside her now.

A sparrow splashed in a shallow puddle. Elif laughed first. Narin laughed too, and soon they were comparing the bird’s feathers to wet paint, whispering so it would not fly away.

The lake kept shining. The seedlings lifted their small green leaves. On the bench, their shoulders gently touched.
