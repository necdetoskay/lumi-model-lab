The Quiet Signal

Deniz was seven, and she liked counting things. In the public garden beside the library, she counted red flowers, white stepping-stones, and the gulls above the roof.

Then she heard it.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

The sound was soft, like a spoon touching a cup. Deniz stood still. It came from the old observation point at the far side of the garden.

A minute later, it happened again.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

Deniz held up three fingers. “Three taps, twice,” she whispered. “And both times they came from the same place.”

She looked at the old point. Its rail shone in the sun, but no person stood behind it. The garden path was empty. Deniz could not see who—or what—had made the sound.

She took a pencil from her pocket and drew the rail, the little roof, and three marks for the taps. Beside the picture, she wrote: SAME SOUND. SAME DIRECTION.

“Maybe it is a message,” she said. “But maybe it is an ordinary sound.”

Deniz hurried to Aunt Mira, who was shelving books near the library door. She showed her the sketch.

“I want to find out what makes it,” Deniz said. “Will you come with me? I will stay beside you and behind the rail.”

Aunt Mira nodded. “We can look for clues safely.”

Deniz tucked the sketch away. She still did not know what the taps meant, but now she had a careful question to follow.

Aunt Mira led Deniz into the library’s small town-history corner. Deniz placed her sketch on a table. It showed the old observation point and three marks for the taps.

“Let’s compare it with what we can see here,” Aunt Mira said.

Deniz looked at an old town map. The garden was drawn beside the library, and a tiny square marked the observation point. She followed the path with her finger. “It is in the same place,” she said.

Next, Aunt Mira set out a faded photograph. In it, the observation point stood above the sea. A metal screen covered one side of the rail. Near the screen was a narrow flap, thin and straight, like a little door that could move.

Deniz leaned close. “I did not see that from the garden.”

“Now you know to look for it,” said Aunt Mira.

Deniz copied the screen and the flap into her notebook. Then she drew two thought bubbles. In one she wrote MESSAGE. In the other she wrote WIND-MADE SOUND.

“Both are still possible,” Deniz said. “The three taps might mean something. Or wind might move that flap against the screen.”

Aunt Mira nodded. “What could help you compare those ideas?”

“Someone at the workshop might help me make a way to watch the wind,” Deniz replied.

She tucked the sketch and notebook safely into her bag. Before leaving, she looked once more at the photograph. The flap was small, but it gave her a new question: Could moving air reach it when the three taps sounded?

Together, Deniz and Aunt Mira headed to the workshop.

At the workshop, Deniz found a long table covered with paper, string, and wooden blocks. Aunt Mira helped her cut a small arrow from stiff paper. They pushed the arrow onto a straw so it could turn.

“What will it show?” asked Mr. Ivo, the workshop helper.

“Which way the air moves,” Deniz said. “But it cannot tell me what makes the taps.”

She drew three boxes in her notebook. The first said TAP. The second said NO TAP. The third said WIND DIRECTION. Under them, she wrote: WATCH, THEN WRITE.

Mr. Ivo opened a side door that faced the garden. He placed the paper arrow on a stand near the doorway. Aunt Mira stood with a small recorder. It held the sound Deniz had heard: tap, tap, tap.

“Ready to observe?” Aunt Mira asked.

Deniz watched the arrow. It turned and pointed toward the old observation point. Then Aunt Mira played the recording outside. Tap, tap, tap.

Deniz quickly marked the TAP box and drew an arrow in the WIND DIRECTION box. “The air and the sound happened together here,” she said. “That is a connection.”

“Is it proof?” asked Mr. Ivo.

Deniz shook her head. “No. The paper arrow did not move the sound. We need to watch the real flap.”

Mr. Ivo drew a simple plan: stand behind the rail at the old point, keep hands away from the metal, and watch the arrow, the flap, and the taps together. Aunt Mira would stay beside Deniz.

Deniz copied the plan. Her next question was ready: Would the real flap move when the real three taps sounded?

Aunt Mira led Deniz to the old observation point. Deniz carried her paper wind arrow in one hand and her first sketch in the other. They stopped behind the rail, just as Mr. Ivo’s plan said.

The arrow turned toward the metal wind screen. Deniz looked at the narrow flap shown in the library photograph. It hung beside the screen, its lower edge loose.

“There!” Deniz whispered.

A small breeze moved across the garden. The flap trembled. Tap, tap, tap.

Deniz held her pencil ready. She marked TAP in her chart. Then the breeze shifted. The arrow swung to one side, away from the screen. The flap became still, and the taps stopped.

Deniz looked at Aunt Mira. “The wind, the flap, and the taps happened together,” she said. “That makes wind seem likely.”

“But does it prove the cause?” Aunt Mira asked.

Deniz shook her head. “Not yet. The sound could still be something else that happens at the same time.”

They waited. A new breeze came from the garden. The arrow pointed toward the screen again. The loose edge gave one tiny shake, but no tap followed.

Deniz frowned, then checked her notes. One tap was not three taps. She did not want to choose an answer from one good-looking clue.

“Can we do one careful comparison?” she asked. “You can hold something over the opening while I watch the arrow and listen. I will stay behind the rail.”

Aunt Mira nodded. “I will do the testing. You will observe.”

Deniz placed her pencil beside the chart. The next question was ready: Would covering the opening change the sound?

Aunt Mira picked up a flat board from beside the rail. She stayed on the safe side and held it over the opening near the loose flap.

Deniz watched the paper arrow. It still pointed toward the metal screen. The breeze was still coming from the garden.

She listened.

No tap came.

Aunt Mira kept the board in place. Deniz waited and watched the flap. Its loose edge no longer trembled. The arrow pointed the same way, but the three-tap sound was gone.

Deniz drew a line in her chart and wrote, “Opening covered: no taps.”

“Now we will change only one thing,” she said. “Take the board away, but keep watching.”

Aunt Mira slowly moved the board aside. The breeze reached the opening again. The narrow flap shook against the metal screen.

Tap, tap, tap.

Deniz heard the pattern clearly. She saw the flap move at the same time. She wrote, “Opening uncovered: three taps.”

She looked from her chart to the screen. “The board stopped the sound even though the breeze and arrow stayed the same,” she said. “When the board came away, the flap touched the screen again, and the taps returned.”

Aunt Mira smiled. “What do you think that tells us?”

Deniz thought about the message idea. A message would not need the opening, the breeze, and the loose flap in the same way.

“The taps are not a warning or a message,” she said. “The wind moves the loose metal flap. It taps the metal screen three times.”

She looked at the flap but did not reach for it. “It needs to be secured. We should report it so a town worker can repair it.”

Aunt Mira lowered the board. Deniz closed her notebook carefully. Her question had become an answer, not because she had guessed, but because the test changed one thing and showed what happened.

Aunt Mira used her phone to report the loose flap to the town office. Soon, a town worker came to the observation point with a small tool bag.

Deniz stayed behind the rail. She watched as the worker secured the flap to the metal screen. Deniz did not touch the tools or climb near the opening. She held her notebook and paper arrow close.

A breeze came from the garden. The paper arrow pointed toward the screen. The repaired flap stayed still.

Deniz waited.

No tap came. Not one. The screen made no three-tap pattern in the same breeze that had made it before.

“The repair worked,” Deniz said. “Before, the breeze reached the loose flap. The flap hit the screen three times. When Aunt Mira covered the opening, the taps stopped. When she moved the board away, they came back. Now the flap is secure, so there are no taps.”

The worker nodded. “That is a very clear report.”

At the library, Aunt Mira helped Deniz place her sketch and observation chart in the town folder. Deniz set the pages flat. Her drawing showed the old point and the direction of the sound. Her chart showed three taps, no taps, the wind direction, and the board test.

The librarian read the notes. “You kept looking at what happened instead of choosing the first answer.”

Deniz smiled at her pages. “I wondered if the taps were a message. Then I found clues. The clues helped me ask a better question.”

Outside, the garden leaves rustled in the breeze. The repaired screen stayed quiet. Deniz listened, watched, and knew why.
