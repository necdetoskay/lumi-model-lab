The Shared Map

The market square buzzed with Garden Day plans. Baskets of ribbons, seed packets, and bright paint stood beneath the striped awnings. At a little wooden table, Ege spread out a large sheet of paper.

“I have it!” he said, dipping his brush into blue ink. “The explorers will begin at the green gate, follow this winding path past the round fountain, turn left at the stone bench, and—”

He drew a neat line from one place to the next and filled the edges with tiny words.

Lina leaned closer. “They could also look for the yellow sunflowers. Then, near the pond, we could draw a purple—”

“And here they must turn right,” Ege said, adding another arrow. “If every path is marked exactly, no one will get mixed up.”

“What if they choose different paths?” Lina asked. “A red tulip could be a clue, and a picture of a—”

Ege’s brush hurried across the paper. “There should be one proper route first. I can label every flower bed.”

Lina gently placed her hand beside the map. Her ideas were bright as the gardens themselves: colored flowers, little pictures, and several ways to explore. “Pictures can help make the map feel like an adventure,” she said.

Ege looked at his crowded sheet. Lina looked at the long blue route. Neither design was finished, and there was hardly room for the other one.

At last, Ege rolled the paper halfway. “Let’s take this draft to the gardens tomorrow.”

Lina nodded. “We can watch children try both ideas before we choose.”

Together they tucked the unfinished map into a folder. The blue route and Lina’s colorful clues traveled side by side, waiting for their test.

The next morning, Ege and Lina carried the folder into the colorful gardens. Sunlight shone on the marigolds, roses, and tall blue delphiniums. They spread the unfinished map on a low table near the green gate.

“Let’s see what explorers notice first,” Lina said.

Three children gathered around. Ege pointed to the blue line. “Begin here, pass the fountain, and follow the path to the stone bench.”

The children started along the garden walk. Soon they stopped beside a sign covered with Ege’s tiny labels.

“Which one says stone bench?” asked Mira, squinting at the words.

Tomas found the bench, but he was unsure which arrow came next. He looked back at the map, turning it sideways. Ege moved closer and read the directions aloud, yet the children still needed him to tell them where to go.

A second group tried Lina’s clues. They spotted the yellow sunflower picture and hurried toward the sunflowers. Then they found the purple pond mark and the red tulip mark—but not in the route’s order. The children wandered between the flower beds, carrying the right colors but unsure which place came next.

No one complained. They simply returned to the table and tried again, pointing at the map and asking questions.

Ege and Lina watched quietly. At first, Ege traced his blue line with one finger. Lina placed her finger beside the colored pictures. Then they looked at each other.

“The words show the path,” Ege said slowly, “but there are too many for the children to use by themselves.”

“And the colors help them recognize places,” Lina replied, “but the pictures do not show the order.”

Ege followed a child’s puzzled glance from the blue route to the flower clues. “They need both,” he said. “A clear path and pictures that help them find each step.”

Their original ideas still lay separately on the crowded sheet. They had discovered the need, but not the design that could answer it.

Ege carefully folded the draft. “Let’s take this new problem back to the market.”

Lina tucked the folder under her arm. Together they left the garden, thinking about how two map systems might fit on one page.

Back in the market square, Ege and Lina spread the folded draft across their worktable. Around them, baskets of apples, bright cloth, and flower garlands made the town feel busy and cheerful.

Ege opened his notebook. “I know what we should do,” he said. “Keep one exact route from the green gate to the fountain, then the bench, pond, and flower beds. I’ll add clearer directions at every turn.”

He began writing: TURN LEFT. WALK TWELVE STEPS. PASS THE FOUNTAIN.

Lina leaned toward the page. “But remember what we saw. When the words confused them, the children looked first for colors and pictures. Mira found the sunflower because of the yellow picture, not because she could read the label.”

Ege’s pencil stopped.

He looked at the tiny directions covering his blue route. Then he remembered Tomas turning the map sideways and the children searching for the next colored mark.

“Tell me more,” Ege said.

Lina pointed to the tested draft. “They recognized places quickly when they saw a picture. The sunflower helped them find the flower bed. The pond picture helped too. They only lost their way because the pictures were not connected in order.”

Ege listened until she finished. He did not add a word. Then he drew a careful line through several labels.

“These directions are getting in the way,” he said. “Your observation is useful, Lina. I was trying to make the map exact, but I made it too full. Let’s keep the route clear and use your pictures to mark each step.”

He removed the long instructions, leaving only simple arrows and short path lines. Beside the first turn, Lina drew a yellow sunflower. Beside the next, she added a blue pond. A little bench and a green gate followed. She numbered the pictures so explorers could see the order without reading many words.

Ege connected each symbol to the blue route. Lina checked every marker against the garden landmarks. When a line passed too far from the pond picture, Ege shifted it. When a symbol sat between two paths, Lina moved it beside the correct one.

At last, they looked at the page. It was still a draft, but now the route and the pictures belonged together.

“One shared map,” Ege said.

“One shared draft,” Lina agreed, sliding a fresh sheet between them. Together, they began planning its larger version.

The next morning, Ege and Lina carried a wide sheet of paper to the worktable. It was large enough for many small hands to see. Sunlight warmed the market square while Ege measured spaces with a ruler.

“I’ll draw the connected paths,” he said. “The blue line will join the gate, flower bed, bench, pond, and fountain. These circles can be meeting points.”

Lina set out her paints. She made a green gate, a yellow flower, a brown bench, and a blue pond. Beside each picture, she placed its number. “I’ll keep the pictures bright and simple,” she said. “Then children can match them to the real garden.”

Ege drew a path from the first picture to the second. Lina leaned over the map. “The flower symbol is beside the path, but the line bends before it. A child might think the turn comes earlier.”

Ege slid the line closer. “Good catch.”

Then he passed the map to Lina. She checked the pond and bench. Ege checked her numbered pictures against his route. Back and forth they worked, one adding and the other checking, until every symbol sat beside the correct path.

They carried the draft into the gardens for a second test. Two children followed the blue line from the gate and matched the flower picture to the yellow bed. They found the bench, then the pond, without needing long directions.

At the beginning, however, the children paused. The first path began near the edge of the paper, and the green gate picture was small among the other marks.

“Where do we start?” asked Mira.

Ege and Lina looked at each other. They had made the route clear, but they had not made its beginning clear enough.

Ege drew a large green circle at the first meeting point. Lina painted START inside it and added a tiny gate. Then they made a shared legend along the bottom: each picture, its color, and the place it showed.

The children tried again. This time, they found the starting mark, followed the blue line, and matched each picture in order.

Ege held one side of the map while Lina held the other. “Now it is ready for Garden Day,” Lina said.

“Ready because we checked it together,” Ege replied, as they carried the tested map back toward the town.

Garden Day filled the market square with music, ribbons, and the smell of warm bread. Beside the garden path, Ege and Lina fastened their large map to a wooden stand. The green START circle shone at the top, and the shared legend stretched along the bottom.

A group of children gathered around it.

“Where should we go first?” asked Mira.

“Start at the green circle,” said Ege, pointing to the blue line. “Then you may follow the path toward any numbered place.”

Lina tapped the pictures in the legend. “Number one is the yellow flower bed. The little yellow flower on the map will help you recognize it.”

The children set off. Some followed the blue route toward the flower bed, while others chose the path leading to the pond. They did not have to move in one fixed order. At each turn, they looked down at the lines and then around at the real garden.

“There’s a blue picture here!” called Sami.

“Find the place with the same color in the legend,” Lina replied.

“Pond!” Sami shouted, and his group hurried along the connected path.

Near a fork, two children stopped. One pointed toward the bench symbol, while the other pointed toward a path that curved toward the gate.

Ege crouched beside them. “See how this line joins the brown bench picture? That path will take you there. The gate path is the other blue line.”

The children compared the map with the garden. Then they chose the bench path together.

At the flower bed, Lina showed a small group how the numbered pictures matched the order on the route. “You found number one. Now look for the next picture connected by the blue line.”

“Bench!” said Mira. “And after that, the pond!”

All around the gardens, the map became a busy meeting place. Children returned to check a symbol, traced paths with their fingers, and called to one another when they recognized a landmark. Ege explained where paths met. Lina helped children read the colors and pictures. When a child found a place, the two friends invited the others to compare it with their own routes.

By afternoon, every group had visited the flower bed, bench, pond, gate, and fountain. The children came back smiling, carrying new discoveries to add to the map. Ege held one side of the paper while Lina held the other as the last explorers gathered around it.

The blue paths were covered with little pointing fingers, and every bright symbol had been found.

The children gathered around the map one more time before the Garden Day ribbons came down.

“May we add what we found?” asked Mira.

Ege and Lina spread the map across the wooden stand. The children drew tiny pictures beside Lina’s bright symbols: three yellow petals beside the flower bed, a round blue ripple beside the pond, and a little brown seat beside the bench. Sami added a green gate with a careful square handle.

“Look here,” said Ege. He pointed to a blue path near the fountain. “When we explored with the last group, we found that this path joins the pond path behind the tall sunflowers.”

Lina leaned close. “Then we should show that connection.”

She drew a neat blue line between the two paths and checked it against the garden. Ege held the map steady while she added it. Then he held the ruler for her, making sure the new line met the pond symbol without covering the children’s drawings.

“Now it shows what we discovered together,” Lina said.

“And what everyone added,” Ege replied.

They carried the map back to the market square. The wooden stand was moved beside the town noticeboard, where people could see it on the way to the gardens. Ege fastened the top corners, and Lina tied the lower ones with bright green string. The map belonged there—not tucked into Ege’s folder or rolled away in Lina’s basket, but open for every future Garden Day explorer.

A breeze lifted one corner. Ege caught it, and Lina pressed the paper flat. Their hands met over the green START circle.

Ege looked at Lina. For a moment, he studied the map instead of reaching for his pencil.

“What do you see next?” he asked.

Lina smiled and looked from the paths to the colorful gardens beyond the square. “I see a map that still has room for both of us.”

Ege nodded. “Then let’s walk and look together.”

They left the market side by side, carrying the empty folder between them. Behind them, the shared map fluttered gently beside the noticeboard, its bright symbols and blue paths pointing toward the gardens.
