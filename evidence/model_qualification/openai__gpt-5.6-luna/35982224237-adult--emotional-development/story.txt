The Space Between Words

By six o’clock, Mosaic House had begun its usual transformation. The front room smelled of coffee, toasted cumin, and the lemon cleaner someone had used on the folding tables. Near the noticeboard, two people Deniz knew well were arguing cheerfully about whether the lentils needed more salt. In the kitchen, Mara was counting bowls without being asked. Mosaic House was like that: part community centre, part shared living room, a place where Deniz regularly crossed paths with neighbours, volunteers, and friends.

Deniz came in carrying a canvas bag of printed menus and a folder of supplier invoices. At thirty-two, they had learned not to trust a plan that existed only in someone’s head. The monthly community supper was three days away, and they had spent their lunch break confirming the bread order and checking whether the donated tables would fit in the courtyard.

They stopped beside the noticeboard.

The supper rota had changed.

Their name was no longer beside welcome desk, table layout, and volunteer briefing. Instead, those tasks were divided among Mara, Jonas, and Priya. Leila’s name appeared at the top of the sheet, followed by a neat list of revised assignments. Deniz read it twice. The handwriting was unmistakably Leila’s: small, slanted, and decisive.

“Ah, you’re here,” Jonas called from the kitchen. “Leila said I should take the first shift on Saturday.”

“Right,” Deniz said. “I see that.”

Jonas returned to arranging bowls. Nothing in his tone suggested a secret. Still, Deniz felt the folder grow heavier in their hand.

They took out their phone and opened the message thread with Leila. Two days earlier, Deniz had written: *I can’t lead the supper this week—I’m buried under the grant deadline. I can still do the practical pieces, though. Let me know what you need from me.*

Leila had replied the next morning: *Okay, thanks for letting me know. I’ll sort the rota.*

That was all.

At the time, Deniz had been relieved not to have to explain the grant deadline again. Now the short reply seemed to contain a different meaning. *I’ll sort the rota* sounded less like coordination and more like a decision made without them. Perhaps Leila had taken “I can’t lead” to mean “I’m stepping away.” Or perhaps she had welcomed the excuse to move Deniz aside and was simply being efficient about it.

The second possibility arrived with such force that Deniz almost mistook it for evidence.

Across the room, Leila stood with two volunteers, pointing at a stack of name cards. She looked busy, focused, entirely at home in the work they had usually handled together. Deniz could walk over and ask what had happened. They could also wait until the first sharpness in their chest had settled.

Instead, they placed the menus on the reception desk and checked the supplier invoices. The bread was confirmed. The lentils still needed collecting. The rota still had gaps around Sunday’s washing-up shift.

Practical facts, Deniz thought. Facts first.

But the hurtful story had already begun arranging itself: Leila had decided Deniz was no longer needed, and everyone else had been told before Deniz. With the supper so close, there was no easy way to ignore the change—and no honest way yet to know what it meant.

Deniz carried the folder to the small table beneath the window, where the afternoon light made the supplier invoices look almost cheerful. Around them, Mosaic House continued as usual. Someone tested the microphone in the hall. A child laughed near the lift. Priya came in carrying a box of paper napkins and greeted Deniz with a quick wave before joining the kitchen team.

Nothing about the room suggested that a decision had been made against them. That fact was inconveniently weak against the rota, but it was still a fact.

Deniz opened the message thread again.

*I can’t lead the supper this week—I’m buried under the grant deadline. I can still do the practical pieces, though. Let me know what you need from me.*

They read the first sentence, then the second.

The words *can’t lead* were clear enough. If Leila had understood that as Deniz stepping back from the public-facing work, reassigning the welcome desk and volunteer briefing was reasonable. The phrase *this week* might have sounded less temporary in the middle of everything else. And “I can still do the practical pieces” was broad. It did not name bread, tables, menus, or the Sunday washing-up shift. It asked Leila to decide what counted.

Deniz scrolled to Leila’s reply.

*Okay, thanks for letting me know. I’ll sort the rota.*

Brief, yes. Perhaps too brief for a conversation they had usually handled in several messages and a voice note. But there was no sentence saying, *We’ll manage without you*, or *Don’t come in on Saturday*, or even *I’ve taken you off the supper.*

Deniz looked at the printed rota. Their name had disappeared from three tasks, but the page did not say why. The changes had happened after their message. That timing supported the uncomfortable interpretation, yet it also supported another one: Leila had needed a reliable plan with only three days left and had treated Deniz’s message as a change in availability.

Neither explanation erased the fact that Leila had not checked first.

That was the part Deniz kept returning to. Even if Leila had misunderstood, why had she discussed the changes with Mara, Jonas, and Priya before speaking to the other person organizing the supper? The omission had landed as dismissal. It made Deniz hesitate before opening the folder, as if the practical work they had done no longer belonged to them.

They checked the bread order. Confirmed. They checked the table measurements. Suitable for the courtyard. The lentils still needed collecting, and Sunday’s washing-up shift remained unfilled. The deadline was real; people needed to know where to be, and supplies could not be arranged by waiting for feelings to settle.

Deniz typed a message to Leila: *What happened with the rota?* Then deleted it. On its own, the question sounded like a demand for a defence.

They tried again: *Can we talk about the supper plan before the rota gets finalised? I want to make sure I understand what you meant.*

Their thumb hovered over send. Understanding Leila’s meaning did not require pretending the change had been fine. It only meant refusing to turn an assumption into a verdict.

Deniz sent the message, then placed the phone face down beside the invoices. The answer might explain everything, or it might confirm that the distance they sensed was real. For now, the evidence allowed both possibilities. What Deniz knew for certain was simpler: they felt pushed aside, and the supper still needed coordinating.

Deniz left the phone face down for nearly ten minutes. Around them, Mosaic House kept offering ordinary reasons to look up: the scrape of chairs across the hall floor, the hiss of the coffee machine, Mara asking someone whether the courtyard tables had covers. The familiar sounds made the situation feel both smaller and more difficult. This was not a private dispute with no consequences. People were already relying on the rota, and Leila was someone Deniz had trusted with the untidy work of making things happen.

They opened their notebook to a clean page. The first line came easily:

*What happened.*

Leila replied briefly after Deniz said they could not lead the supper that week. She revised the rota. Deniz’s name disappeared from the welcome desk, the volunteer briefing, and the task of coordinating the serving tables. She spoke about at least some of those changes with Mara, Jonas, and Priya before speaking to Deniz.

Deniz paused before writing the next heading.

*What I inferred.*

Leila has decided I’m not needed. She has been arranging things around me, not with me. Maybe she wants the supper to be hers now.

The last thought looked harsher on paper than it had sounded in Deniz’s head. They drew a line through it, not because it was impossible, but because they had no evidence for it beyond the feeling of being displaced.

The feeling itself deserved more than a line through it.

Deniz wrote: *hurt*. Then, after a moment, *embarrassed*. It was embarrassing to see other people discussing tasks Deniz had helped build over several months, as though their role could be quietly redistributed without explanation. Beneath those words came the one they had been avoiding: *afraid of losing trust with Leila*.

That fear made the rota seem more deliberate than it might be. If Leila had simply misunderstood the message, then the change was careless and painful. If she had meant to exclude Deniz, it was evidence of a larger break. Neither possibility felt easy, but neither had been established.

Deniz turned back to the message thread and read it once more.

*I can’t lead the supper this week—I’m buried under the grant deadline. I can still do the practical pieces, though. Let me know what you need from me.*

They had meant: I cannot take the visible coordinating role, but I remain part of the organizing team. Yet “the practical pieces” was vague, and “I can’t lead” occupied the first sentence. With three days until the supper, Leila might reasonably have heard a broader withdrawal. She might have thought that assigning the tasks elsewhere was the responsible thing to do.

That did not make the lack of consultation imaginary. Deniz had checked the bread order, measured the donated tables, and kept track of the unfilled washing-up shift. They had offered continued help. Leila had changed the plan without asking what that help would look like.

Both things could be true. Deniz could have written an unclear message, and Leila could have handled the uncertainty badly. Feeling hurt did not prove intention. It did, however, explain why the change mattered.

A voice rose from the kitchen. “Deniz, do you know whether the lentils are coming Friday or Saturday?”

“Friday,” Deniz called back. “I’ll confirm the collection time.”

The answer came automatically, part of the work they were still doing. No one in the kitchen needed to know that Deniz was wondering whether to tell them about the rota. Priya had waved earlier; Mara had been assigned a new task. Either person might offer an interpretation, but asking them would turn an uncertain conversation into a group alignment. Deniz did not want witnesses, sympathisers, or a story that reached Leila before Deniz did.

They closed the notebook, then opened it again and added one final sentence: *Ask Leila what she understood, and tell her what the change felt like without deciding why she made it.*

The sentence was not a solution. It did not restore the three tasks or explain the brief reply. It was simply a way forward that left room for Leila’s account and for Deniz’s own experience to remain real.

Deniz slipped the notebook beneath the folder of invoices. Before the supper, they would speak to Leila directly. Not to prove that exclusion had happened, and not to prove that it had not, but to find out what each of them had believed the other was saying.

By late afternoon, Mosaic House had settled into its in-between hours. The lunch crowd was gone, but the building had not yet filled for the evening workshops. A cordless drill whined somewhere upstairs. In the kitchen, someone rinsed a stack of mixing bowls. Sunlight lay across the noticeboard, catching the corners of the revised rota.

Deniz saw Leila near the storeroom, comparing a list with the boxes of serving dishes. For a moment, the sentence in the notebook seemed much easier to write than to say.

They waited until Leila finished counting plates. Then Deniz approached, keeping the folder of invoices tucked against their side as if it might lend them steadiness.

“Do you have ten minutes sometime today?” Deniz asked. “I want to clarify the supper rota. Not in front of everyone, and not to blame you. I think we’ve understood my message differently, and the plan still needs sorting.”

Leila looked up. Her expression was attentive but guarded enough that Deniz noticed it. “Now?”

“If now works.” Deniz glanced toward the kitchen, where Mara was taping labels onto storage containers. “Somewhere quiet.”

Leila put down the list. “The small meeting room is free.”

They crossed the hall without stopping to explain themselves. Deniz felt the familiar pull to make the conversation neater than it was—to arrive with a perfectly measured voice and no trace of accusation. Instead, their hands were cold, and the first version of the opening sentence had already vanished.

The meeting room held a round table, four mismatched chairs, and a window overlooking the courtyard. Someone had left a half-erased diagram of a shelving unit on the whiteboard. Leila closed the door but did not sit until Deniz did.

Deniz opened the folder. “I wrote down the specific things I want to talk about, because I don’t want to say ‘you pushed me out’ when I can’t actually establish that.”

Leila’s eyebrows lifted slightly. “All right.”

“On Monday, I said I couldn’t lead the supper this week because of the grant deadline, but that I could still do the practical pieces. You replied, ‘Okay, thanks for letting me know. I’ll sort the rota.’ Then I saw that the welcome desk, the volunteer briefing, and the serving-table coordination had all been reassigned. You’d already discussed some of it with Mara, Jonas, and Priya.” Deniz heard their voice tighten. “What affected me was that the changes happened without you checking what I meant by ‘practical pieces.’ I felt left out of a plan I’m still responsible for.”

They stopped. The last sentence sounded less controlled than they had hoped, but it was accurate.

Leila rested both hands on the table. “I’m glad you asked me directly. I’ve been worried you’d heard the rota as a message that we didn’t want you involved.”

“That is more or less how it felt,” Deniz said. “But I don’t know that it was the message you meant to send.”

The admission left a small, uncomfortable space between them. Neither of them filled it immediately. From the corridor came the rumble of a trolley and a burst of laughter from the workshop room.

Leila nodded toward the folder. “Can we look at the rota together? The supper is in three days, and we need a plan people can rely on. I agree we shouldn’t try to settle this by making everyone else guess what’s happening.”

Deniz exhaled, not quite relieved. The problem was still there, and Leila had not explained the change yet. But she had agreed to stay with the question.

“Yes,” Deniz said. “And I want to ask for one thing before we start. If my role needs changing, please check with me first. Even if the answer seems obvious.”

Leila met their eyes. “I can do that. Let’s go through what I understood, and what you intended.”

Deniz turned the rota so it faced them both. The three reassigned tasks sat in the middle of the page, waiting.

Leila tapped the message thread on her phone. “When I read this, I focused on the first part: ‘I can’t lead the supper this week because of the grant deadline.’ Then you said you could do ‘the practical pieces.’ I wasn’t sure whether that meant collecting supplies and checking the tables, or whether you wanted to stay involved in the whole plan but not hold the lead.”

“I meant the second one,” Deniz said. “I couldn’t run the volunteer briefing or be the person everyone came to for decisions. But I could still coordinate the serving tables and cover the welcome desk.”

“I understand that now. I didn’t then.” Leila looked down at the rota. “The deadline was close. Mara had asked what she could take on, Jonas said he could brief the volunteers, and Priya offered to coordinate the tables. I thought I was making the plan dependable instead of waiting for you to confirm what you had capacity for.”

Deniz felt the old interpretation rise again: she had made a plan without them because she no longer trusted them. But the details were solid. The supper was three days away. People had offered help. Leila had not written, “We don’t need you.” She had written, “I’ll sort the rota,” and then sorted it too broadly.

“That makes sense as a practical decision,” Deniz said. “It still landed badly. I saw my tasks moved to other people, and I didn’t know whether you’d decided I was unreliable or just unavailable.”

Leila’s face tightened. “I’m sorry I changed your role without checking. I can see why that felt like being pushed out. I should have asked, especially because we normally make these decisions together.”

The apology did not erase the week’s uneasiness, but it met the part of the problem that a better reading of the message could not. Deniz let their shoulders drop.

“And I should have written more clearly,” they said. “Leading and participating are different things, but I made you infer the difference. ‘I can’t lead’ came first, and I treated ‘practical pieces’ as if it were precise.”

Leila gave a small, rueful laugh. “It was precise to you.”

“Very precise to me.”

They both looked at the rota again. This time, the page seemed less like evidence and more like something they could alter.

“What would you actually have capacity for?” Leila asked.

Deniz pulled the folder closer. “I can keep the bread order and lentil collection. I can coordinate the serving tables if Priya is still willing to help with setup. I can also take the welcome desk, but not the volunteer briefing. I need to leave by eight-thirty to finish the grant application.”

“I’ll take the briefing and the overall on-the-night decisions,” Leila said. “Mara can stay on the welcome desk with you for the first hour. Jonas can handle the washing-up shift on Sunday if he’s still free; I’ll confirm that today.”

Deniz crossed out the old assignments and wrote the new ones in the margin. Leila added her initials beside the changes. The revised plan was not a return to the original rota. It was more accurate: Deniz’s availability had limits, but it had not disappeared.

“There’s one more thing,” Deniz said. “If either of us changes a role, can we check directly before updating the rota? Even a quick message: ‘I’m thinking of moving this task—does that match what you meant?’”

“Yes,” Leila said. “And if your message is ambiguous, I’ll ask instead of filling in the gaps myself.”

“I’ll do the same.” Deniz looked at the phone between them. “If I’m stepping back from something, I’ll name what I’m still taking on and when I’ll be unavailable.”

Leila turned the revised rota toward herself and photographed it. “I’ll send this to the volunteers, with the actual task boundaries. Not just names.”

A familiar sound came through the wall: someone testing the microphone in the main hall, followed by a burst of feedback. Deniz and Leila winced at the same time.

“Still Mosaic House,” Leila said.

“Still three days from the supper.”

“Still enough time.”

Deniz believed that—not because the discomfort had vanished, but because the plan now contained what each of them had meant. Leila opened a new message to the volunteers. Deniz checked the lentil collection time one more time, then sent Leila the details rather than assuming she would find them in the folder.

Before they left the room, Leila paused at the door. “For what it’s worth, I didn’t want you less involved.”

Deniz met her eyes. “I know that now. I didn’t know it when I saw the rota.”

“That difference matters.”

“It does.”

They carried the corrected page back into the hall together, not entirely untouched by the misunderstanding, but no longer arranging the evening around what either of them feared the other had meant.

By Saturday afternoon, Mosaic House had filled with the smells of cumin, baked bread, and something sweet warming in the kitchen. Deniz arrived with the bread order, checked the lentils from the collection point, and found the revised rota clipped to the noticeboard. Their name was beside the welcome desk and serving tables, with “leave by 8:30” written clearly underneath.

The words still made Deniz pause, but not in the old way. The page no longer looked like a verdict. It looked like a plan with edges.

Mara waved from the entrance. “I’m taking the first hour with you, right?”

“That’s what I have,” Deniz said, turning the rota so they could both see it. “And then I’m moving to the serving tables with Priya.”

“Perfect.” Mara began arranging name badges in a shallow wooden tray.

In the hall, Leila was briefing the volunteers. She spoke over the scrape of folding chairs and the occasional clatter from the kitchen, giving each person a task and a place to start. When she caught Deniz’s eye, she lifted a hand in greeting. Deniz lifted theirs back.

The first hour passed in a steady stream of arrivals. Neighbours carried containers through the doors. Jonas appeared with a bundle of clean tea towels and confirmed that he would handle the Sunday washing-up shift. Priya set out serving spoons while Deniz helped an older couple find a quiet table near the courtyard doors.

Nothing was perfectly smooth. One of the donated tables wobbled until Jonas folded a piece of cardboard beneath its leg. The card reader lost its connection, then recovered. A volunteer arrived late and stood uncertainly beside the drinks station.

Leila crossed the hall, looking toward Deniz. “Can you move to drinks for a few minutes? We need someone there while I sort out the kitchen queue.”

Deniz felt a quick tug of uncertainty. They glanced at the rota, then at the crowded serving tables. The request made sense, but the agreement between them was still new enough to require use rather than trust by habit.

“Before I move,” Deniz said, “do you mean for a few minutes, or should Priya and I switch the serving-table plan?”

Leila stopped. “Just fifteen minutes, while I get the queue moving. Priya can stay with the tables. I’ll come back and take drinks when the kitchen settles.”

“Good. I wanted to check the boundary.”

“I’m glad you did,” Leila said. “I should have said it the first time.”

Deniz went to the drinks station. The question had taken less than half a minute. It had also kept a small uncertainty from growing into a private story.

Later, when the main meal was served, Deniz found themselves laughing with Priya over an avalanche of napkins. Leila moved between tables, answering questions and making decisions without trying to hold every task herself. When she passed Deniz a fresh stack of bowls, their hands brushed, and neither of them hesitated.

At eight twenty-five, Deniz checked the time. The grant application still waited at home, but the serving tables were cleared and the last tea was being poured.

“I’m heading off in five,” Deniz told Leila. “The washing-up team has the kitchen, and Priya knows where the leftover bread is going.”

“Thank you for telling me,” Leila said. “I’ll close the hall with Jonas.”

Outside, the evening air was cool and smelled faintly of rain. Through the glass doors, Deniz could see Mosaic House settling back into itself: chairs being stacked, familiar people saying goodnight, the noticeboard glowing under the corridor lights.

Leila joined them on the steps. “It went well.”

“It did.” Deniz looked through the doors. “Not because nothing got confusing.”

Leila smiled. “Definitely not because of that.”

“But we checked.”

“We checked.”

They stood together for a moment, tired in the ordinary way, with the supper finished and the next decisions still belonging to both of them.
