The Space Between Words

Every Saturday morning, Deniz and Ece met in the library’s familiar reading room before Story Corner began. Deniz liked being in charge of the small jobs: putting picture books in neat piles, setting out the word-game cards, and writing the activity schedule on the whiteboard.

This Saturday, Deniz had an idea. Instead of playing only one word game, they could play three. There was a letter hunt, a silly sentence race, and a game where everyone made up a new ending for a story.

“Look!” Deniz said, turning the schedule toward Ece. “We can fit all of these in.”

Ece leaned over the table. She had brought a stack of quiet-story cards, each with a short tale on one side and a question on the other.

“What about the quiet stories?” she asked. “We could have twenty minutes for them.”

Deniz checked the whiteboard. The schedule was already crowded, but there was one space between the sentence race and the final book. “We can put them here,” Deniz said, writing **Quiet Stories — 5 minutes** in the space. “Then we still have time for everything else.”

Ece stared at the five-minute slot. “Five minutes isn’t really enough.”

“We can read a very short one,” Deniz replied. Deniz was already imagining everyone laughing at the sentence race. “The games will be fun.”

Ece’s fingers rested on the edge of the schedule. Then she gently folded the paper closed.

“I need to check something,” she said.

Deniz’s smile slipped. “What thing?”

“Just something.” Ece tucked the story cards into her bag. Before leaving, she noticed Deniz looking at the closed plan.

“I’ll see you later,” Ece said, but her voice sounded quieter than usual.

After the door swung shut, Deniz stood beside the table. The facts were easy to name: Deniz had added two games, Ece had asked for more quiet-story time, Ece had closed the plan, and now Ece had left.

But another thought pushed in. Maybe Ece was tired of doing Story Corner with Deniz. Maybe she wanted to work alone.

Deniz looked back at the schedule. Through the window, Ece paused in the hall and wrote something on a small note beside the plan. Deniz could not read it. The note might have been about the activities. It might have been about something else.

Deniz only knew that the plan had changed—and that their stomach felt tight.

The next Saturday, Deniz arrived early at the library and carried the word-game cards to the reading room. The room looked the same as always: tall windows, round tables, and the soft squeak of the cart Ms. Rana used for returned books.

Deniz expected Ece to come straight to their usual table. Instead, Ece put her bag at a smaller table near the shelves. She gave Deniz a quick wave.

“Hi,” Ece said.

“Hi,” Deniz answered.

That was all. Ece took out the quiet-story cards and lined them up without saying anything else.

A minute later, Ms. Rana walked past. Ece spoke to her in a low voice. Deniz heard only a few words.

“Could we change the schedule?” Ece asked. “I can use the small table for my section.”

Ms. Rana nodded. “You can mark the request on the activity board.”

Ece thanked her, then returned to the small table. She did not look angry. Still, she was quieter than usual, and she had chosen a different place. She seemed to be making plans without Deniz.

Deniz’s face felt hot. The feeling had two parts: hurt, because Ece was acting as if their usual partnership had ended, and embarrassment, because Deniz remembered saying that five minutes was enough for quiet stories.

Deniz pulled a notebook from their backpack. Writing felt safer than guessing.

Under the heading **What I saw**, Deniz wrote:

- Ece is sitting at another table.
- Ece spoke to Ms. Rana about changing the schedule.
- Ece is not talking much.
- Ece brought the story cards.
- Ece said hi to me.

Deniz stared at the last two lines. They did not fit neatly with the others.

Under **What I’m guessing**, Deniz wrote:

- Ece does not want to work with me.
- Ece is angry about the schedule.
- The note might have said she wants to do Story Corner alone.

Deniz tapped the pencil against the page. A guess could feel huge, especially when a stomach was tight, but it was still a guess.

Across the room, Ece checked the story cards. She had promised to bring them, and she had. That did not prove everything was fine. It did mean Deniz could not honestly say Ece had disappeared or refused to help.

Deniz looked at the five-minute slot on the schedule. “We can put them here,” Deniz had said. Ece had warned that five minutes was not enough, but Deniz had been busy imagining the games.

Maybe the crowded plan had caused this. Maybe Ece needed something different. Deniz still wanted to know what the note meant, but asking while feeling this hurt might make the question come out sharp.

So Deniz closed the notebook and watched Ece arrange the cards. Before confronting her, Deniz decided, there had to be more to learn.

Deniz kept the notebook open but did not write anything else. The two lists had helped, yet the question about the note still sat in Deniz’s mind like a pebble in a shoe.

The activity board stood beside the library’s help desk. It was a large whiteboard where people could see the Saturday plans and requests. Nothing on it was private. Ms. Rana was nearby, sorting returned books into a cart.

“Can I check the Story Corner schedule?” Deniz asked.

“Of course,” Ms. Rana said. “The board is right there.”

Deniz walked over and read the lines carefully. The original plan was still written in blue marker:

10:00 — word scramble
10:10 — card clues
10:20 — mystery words
10:30 — quiet stories

Beside the last line, Ece had written: **Please keep this section. Ece will lead.**

Deniz blinked. Ece had not erased the quiet-story section after all. She had asked for it.

Then Deniz looked at the times again. The activities ran one after another with no space between them. If the word scramble took ten minutes, the cards would have to be put away while the clue cards were being handed out. If one game ran long, the quiet-story section would be squeezed even more.

Deniz pictured the crowded table from the first planning day. Games, cards, books, pencils—everything had been pushed into every possible minute. Ece had said five minutes was too short. Deniz had answered, “We can make it work.”

Maybe Ece wanted the small table because she needed room to lead the stories properly. That made sense. It did not automatically mean she wanted to stop working with Deniz.

But the request could mean two things at once. Ece might want to lead that part herself, and she might still feel ignored because Deniz had filled the schedule without listening. The board could show what Ece asked for. It could not show how she felt.

“Did Ece change the request?” Deniz asked, pointing to the board.

Ms. Rana glanced at the writing. “She added her name this morning. That’s all I know.”

She returned to the books, leaving Deniz with the board and the quiet squeak of the cart.

Deniz went back to the notebook. Under the guesses, Deniz added one more line: **Ece might need space for the stories, not space away from me.**

The sentence felt less painful than the others, but it was still a guess.

Deniz closed the notebook. There was a real problem with the schedule, and Deniz had helped create it. There was also a friendship problem, or maybe only a fear of one. To find out, Deniz would have to ask Ece carefully instead of letting the board answer for her.

Deniz found Ece at the small table near the windows. The quiet-story cards were spread in neat rows, and three picture books waited beside them. Ece was checking the cards, but she looked up when Deniz approached.

For a moment, Deniz almost turned back. The note beside the schedule seemed to grow heavier each time Deniz thought about it.

Instead, Deniz opened the notebook. On a clean page, Deniz wrote three questions:

**What happened?**
**How did you feel?**
**What would be fair now?**

The questions looked simple. Saying them out loud felt much harder.

Deniz tapped the pencil against the page. “I want to ask Ece what happened,” Deniz whispered. “Not ask why she’s doing this.”

That sounded less like an accusation. Deniz added another line underneath: **Say how I felt, not what I think Ece meant.**

Deniz still felt hurt. The separate table, the short answers, and the changed role were real. They had made Deniz feel pushed away and embarrassed. Offering the table did not make those feelings disappear. It only gave Ece a choice about how to run her part.

Deniz picked up a stack of books. “Ece, you can use this table for the quiet stories,” Deniz said. “I’ll move the word-game cards to the other one. Then, after we finish setting up, can we talk?”

Ece glanced at the notebook, then at Deniz. “About the schedule?”

“About the schedule and the note.” Deniz tried to keep their voice steady. “I want to understand. I also want to tell you how it seemed to me.”

Ece held one story card between her fingers. For a second, she looked as nervous as Deniz felt.

“Okay,” she said. “After we arrange everything.”

Deniz carried the books to the quiet-story table. Ece made room for them without saying anything.

As they worked, Deniz kept noticing the silence. Usually, they would argue cheerfully about which game belonged first. Now there was only the soft slide of books across the table and the click of cards being stacked.

Deniz wanted to ask about the note right away. The three questions in the notebook seemed to tug in different directions: listen first, explain the hurt, then find a fair plan.

At last, Ece placed the final card on top of the pile.

“I’ll explain the note myself,” she said quietly. “I don’t want you to have to guess.”

Deniz’s stomach tightened. That could mean the note held a kind explanation, or it could mean something worse than Deniz had imagined. Still, Ece had agreed to talk.

Deniz closed the notebook but kept a finger between its pages. “All right,” Deniz said. “I’ll listen.”

They finished arranging the books, cards, and pencils. The questions were ready. The answer was not.

Deniz and Ece sat at the small table between the two piles of supplies. The library sounded different now that they had stopped moving: pages turning, a cart squeaking near the shelves, someone whispering by the windows.

Deniz opened the notebook. “I’ll try first,” they said. “When you changed to the other table and asked Ms. Rana to change the schedule, I felt pushed away. You were quieter too. I started thinking you didn’t want to work with me anymore.”

Ece looked down at the story cards. “I can understand why it looked that way.”

Deniz took a breath. “But those are the things I noticed. The part about not wanting to work together was my guess.”

Ece rubbed the corner of a card between her fingers. “The note was about the schedule. I wrote, ‘Ask to lead quiet stories.’”

Deniz’s shoulders loosened a little. “You wanted to lead it yourself?”

“Yes.” Ece glanced at the crowded activity list. “When you added the word races, the card match, and the clue game, the quiet-story part got squeezed into five minutes. I said it was too short, but you said we could read faster.”

Deniz remembered saying that. At the time, the extra games had seemed exciting. “I thought we could fit everything.”

“I know you didn’t mean to leave me out,” Ece said. “But I felt unheard. It seemed like there wasn’t any space for my idea unless I made my own table.”

Deniz looked at the neat rows of cards. Ece had brought every story card she promised. That fact had been there all along, beside the hurtful-looking ones.

“I’m sorry I crowded the plan and brushed off what you said,” Deniz replied. “I should have asked how much time you needed.”

Ece lifted her eyes. “I’m sorry I changed things without telling you. I should have said, ‘I need to lead this part,’ instead of getting quiet and letting you guess.”

The apology did not erase the awkward days, but it made them feel less heavy.

Deniz pulled the schedule closer. “What if we take turns? You lead the quiet-story section first for ten minutes. Then I lead one word game for ten minutes. We can switch once more if there’s time.”

Ece studied the page. “And we leave five minutes at the end for choosing books and putting things away.”

“Right. No squeezing.” Deniz drew three lines through the extra games. “We can save the card match for another Saturday.”

Ece smiled a little. “That sounds fair.”

Deniz added a box at the bottom of the schedule: **Check before changing.**

“If either of us wants to change a part,” Deniz said, “we ask the other person first. Even if it seems like a small change.”

“Ask first,” Ece agreed. She tapped the note. “I should have done that with this.”

Deniz closed the notebook. “And I should have asked what the note meant instead of filling in the answer.”

For the first time that morning, Ece gave a small laugh. “We both filled in too much.”

Together, they rewrote the schedule. The quiet stories had room. The word game had a clear turn. Between them, the new check-in box sat like a little doorway neither friend had to walk through alone.

By the time Story Corner began, the reading room had filled with children and the soft rustle of library books. Deniz stood beside Ece’s small table while Ece held up the first story card.

“Today we’re taking turns,” Ece announced. “I’ll start with a quiet story, and Deniz will lead a word game after.”

A few children settled onto the rug. Ece read about a fox who collected blue buttons, pausing to let everyone guess where the next button would turn up. Deniz watched the listeners lean closer. The story had room to breathe, just as Ece had wanted.

After ten minutes, Deniz took the word-game cards. The game was called Last Letter, and each player had to say a word beginning with the last letter of the word before it. The children became very quick—and very enthusiastic.

“Table!” called Sami.

“Elephant!” said Lina.

“Tiger!”

The game rolled on. Deniz glanced at the schedule. Their ten minutes had already passed, and Ece’s second story was supposed to begin next. A child was still thinking hard, tapping a finger against their knee. Everyone looked eager to continue.

Before, Deniz might have crossed out the next part without saying anything. Now they put one hand over the cards.

“We’re running longer than we planned,” Deniz said. “I noticed the game has taken about fifteen minutes. Ece, can we check what to change?”

Ece looked at her story cards, then at the children. “I have a long story left, but I could read the shorter one. It’s only four pages.”

“Then we can finish the game with one last round,” Deniz suggested. “After your short story, we’ll still have time to put everything away.”

Ece nodded. “That works for me. Does it work for you?”

Deniz looked around. The children agreed with a chorus of yeses, though Sami asked if they could play Last Letter again another Saturday.

“One game at a time,” Deniz said, smiling. “We’ll save it for then.”

The final round ended with the word “moon.” Ece gathered the players close and read the shorter story, changing her voice for a sleepy owl and a very proud mouse. Even with fewer pages, nobody seemed rushed.

When Story Corner ended, Deniz and Ece returned the cards to their boxes and stacked the books. The schedule had not gone perfectly. One game had stretched, and a story had been shortened. Still, nothing had been changed silently.

Deniz noticed the difference between the fifteen minutes that had actually happened and the worry that might once have filled in the rest. This time, they had asked. Ece had answered. Together, they had chosen what came next.

At the bottom of the schedule, the little box still said: **Check before changing.** Ece tapped it with her pencil.

“Keep that for next Saturday?”

“Definitely,” Deniz said. “And maybe the blue-button story.”

“Only if I get to read it again.”

“Deal.”
