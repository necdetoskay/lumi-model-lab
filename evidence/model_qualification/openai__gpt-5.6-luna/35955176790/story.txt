The Bench in the Quiet Garden

In the lakeside neighborhood, mornings began with little sounds. Elif heard bicycle bells near the path, water lapping against the shore, and a sparrow hopping beneath the community garden gate.

She noticed these things before she noticed herself.

Nuri was kneeling beside the tomato seedlings when Elif arrived. Nuri was her neighbor, her garden helper, and the person who knew exactly which strawberries might be ready first.

“Elif,” Nuri said, brushing soil from their hands, “I have something to tell you. My family is moving to another district soon.”

The words seemed to float above the rows of green leaves.

Elif looked at a tiny drop of water trembling on a stem. Then her chest tightened. She wanted to ask when. She wanted to say, “Please don’t go.” But her words folded themselves away.

“Oh,” she said.

Nuri waited for a moment, but did not ask Elif to explain. “We’ll still be nearby,” they said gently. “And we have a little time before we move.”

Elif nodded. She watched Nuri fill the watering can and tilt it over the seedlings. Water darkened the soil in small, shining patches. Nuri watered each plant carefully, even the smallest one near the garden wall.

Elif stayed close, listening to the soft patter. She cared about Nuri. The thought felt warm and heavy at the same time.

When the watering was done, Elif walked to the quiet bench at the garden’s edge. From there, she could see the lake between two trees. A breeze stirred the tall grass, and the bench gave a tiny creak as she sat down.

Elif pressed her hands together and watched a yellow leaf turn in the air.

She still could not say what was inside her. But perhaps she could find a gentle way to begin.

The next morning, Elif returned to the community garden before the sun had climbed very high. Nuri was already beside the seedlings, holding the watering can with both hands.

“Would you like to help?” Nuri asked.

Elif nodded.

They worked along the first row together. Nuri tipped the can, and Elif guided the thin stream toward the roots. The soil changed from pale brown to deep, shining brown. Tiny drops clung to the leaves. Above them, a leaf shadow trembled on the path.

Elif noticed each thing. She also noticed her breathing. In. Out. In. Out. When her chest felt tight, she watched the water sink into the earth and waited for the next easy breath.

Nuri did not ask what Elif was thinking. They only moved the can closer when Elif reached for it and smiled when she watered the smallest seedling near the wall.

Afterward, Elif carried a square of blue paper and a pencil to the quiet bench. The lake flashed between the trees. A small brown bird hopped near the garden gate, then flew to the fence.

Elif drew it carefully. First came the round body, then two bright eyes, then thin legs gripping the fence. She added a little green leaf beside it, like the leaves Nuri had watered.

The drawing looked ready before Elif did.

She turned the card over. The blank side waited.

Elif pressed the pencil tip to the paper, then lifted it again. Words felt much bigger than the bird. She could not yet write what was inside her. Not all of it. Maybe not even the first part.

So she drew one more tiny seedling beneath the bird.

Nuri passed by with the empty watering can. “That bird has a good view,” they said.

Elif looked at the card and nodded. She held it gently in both hands. The space beneath the drawing was still empty, but it no longer felt like nothing. It was a place where words might begin.

That afternoon, Elif carried the blue card past the garden gate and along the lakeside path. The birdwatching area stood where the reeds bent toward the water. A wooden rail marked the place, and several small windows in the birdhouse faced the lake.

Elif rested the card on the rail. The bird she had drawn looked very small beside the wide water.

Then a bird called from the reeds.

“Peep-peep-peep!”

Elif lifted her head. The sound came once more, and then stopped. For a moment, the whole lakeside seemed to pause. No wings fluttered. No reeds rustled. Elif could hear the soft lap of water against the shore.

The quiet did not feel empty. It felt like a space waiting to be noticed.

Elif looked down at the blank part of her card. She thought about Nuri’s watering can, the careful tilt of it, and the way Nuri always sent the water close to each seedling’s roots. She thought about the dark soil shining afterward. She thought about how the garden felt welcoming when Nuri was there, even on mornings when Elif had no words.

Her pencil touched the card.

She waited.

The pencil moved a little, stopped, and moved again. Elif’s chest tightened. She watched a small ripple travel across the lake until it became wide and smooth.

Then she wrote:

“I will miss our garden.”

Elif read the sentence once. The words looked plain, but they made her fingers warm around the pencil.

Below it, she added, “Your careful watering makes it feel welcoming.”

She almost rubbed the words away. They were true, and true words felt wobbly when they belonged to her. But the letters stayed on the card.

A bird lifted from the reeds and crossed the bright strip of sky. Elif followed it until it disappeared beyond the trees.

She folded the card carefully, though she did not put it away. The move was still coming. Nuri would still leave the neighborhood. Writing the words had not changed that.

But now the card held something Elif could carry: not everything in her heart, perhaps, but a beginning.

The next afternoon, Elif carried the blue card back to the community garden. She had folded it once, then unfolded it, then folded it again. The corners were beginning to soften.

Nuri was near the quiet bench, gathering the small garden tools. One by one, they brushed soil from a trowel and placed it beside the watering can. The seedlings stood in their neat rows, their leaves lifting toward the light.

Elif walked closer. The card felt warm in her hand.

“Nuri,” she began.

Nuri looked up and smiled. “Hi, Elif.”

Elif held out the card.

For one tiny moment, it seemed she might let go. Then Nuri bent to fold the garden cloth and lift the tools into a basket. The movement made Elif notice the packed shape of the basket, the quiet scrape of metal, and the thought that Nuri would soon be carrying things away from this garden.

Her fingers closed around the card.

“I made…” Elif stopped.

Nuri waited only a moment, then carried the basket toward the shed. Nuri did not ask what the card said. Elif watched the blue sleeve disappear behind the shed door.

She was disappointed, but she had not gone backward. She had come closer than before. She had held out the card.

Elif sat on the bench. A stripe of sunlight lay across one end. Slowly, as the afternoon moved, the stripe slid over the wood, crossed her knee, and rested on the toes of her shoe.

Elif breathed in while the light moved. She breathed out when a sparrow hopped along the garden path. The bench felt steady beneath her.

She opened the card and read the words silently. Then she tried one sentence aloud, so softly that it seemed meant for the leaves.

“I will miss our garden.”

The sentence trembled. Elif said it again. It still trembled, but it stayed together.

She looked toward the place where Nuri had gone. Tomorrow, she decided, she would ask Nuri to meet her here at the bench. She would bring the card. She would begin with that sentence.

Elif tucked the card safely into her pocket. The move was still coming, and her chest still felt tight. But now she had a place, a time, and a beginning.

The next morning, Elif reached the quiet bench before Nuri did. The blue card rested in her pocket, its soft corners pressing against her fingers.

She watched a drop of water tremble on a bean leaf. It slid down slowly, caught the sunlight, and disappeared into the dark soil. Elif breathed in. She breathed out. Her chest still felt tight, but she stayed.

Soon Nuri came along the garden path. A small basket of tools swung from their hand.

“Hi, Elif,” Nuri said. “You came.”

Elif nodded. She took out the card. For a moment, the bird she had drawn seemed to flutter beneath her thumb.

“I made this for you,” she said.

Nuri took the card carefully. “Thank you.”

Elif looked at the bench, then at the card. The words were waiting there, but saying them aloud felt different from reading them silently.

She touched the edge of the blue paper. “It says…”

Her voice grew small. She paused and listened to a bee humming near the marigolds.

“I will miss our garden,” she read. Then she added, “And I feel sad because our garden time matters to me.”

The last words came out unevenly, but they came out.

Nuri did not hurry to fill the quiet. They looked at the card, then at Elif.

“I’ll miss you too,” Nuri said. “I’ll miss watering the seedlings with you and seeing what you notice.”

Elif’s eyes grew warm. The sadness did not vanish. It spread through her like a gray-blue cloud, gentle but real. Beside it, something steadier appeared: Nuri had heard her. Her words had reached the place she meant them to go.

Nuri sat beside her on the bench. “Would you like to visit the birdwatching area one more time before we move?”

Elif pictured the wooden rail, the reeds, and the birdhouses facing the lake. She pictured sitting quietly beside Nuri, listening for the familiar call.

“Yes,” she said. This word was easier. “One final visit.”

Nuri smiled, not as if everything were fixed, but as if the plan mattered.

Elif slipped the card back into her pocket. The garden looked the same—the bright leaves, the damp soil, the little paths between the beds. Yet something between her and Nuri felt more open.

They sat together until the bee moved on, and neither of them needed to hurry the next moment.

The final visit came on a bright morning when the lake shone between the reeds.

Elif and Nuri stood at the birdwatching area beside the wooden rail. Nuri carried the small basket of garden tools. Elif carried the blue card, though it was still tucked safely in her pocket.

For a while, neither of them spoke. Water tapped softly against the shore. A breeze moved through the reeds, making them whisper and bend.

Then a familiar bird called from the birdhouses across the water.

Elif looked up. Nuri looked up too.

“There,” Nuri whispered.

The bird lifted from the roof of a birdhouse, circled once, and settled in a willow tree. Elif noticed its brown wings, the quick turn of its head, and the small pause before it called again. She felt the sadness in her chest, but she also felt the calm space around it.

“I’m glad we came here,” she said.

“Me too,” Nuri replied. “I’ll remember this bird. And the garden.”

Elif held the rail with both hands. “I’ll remember how you watered the seedlings. You always noticed when one needed more.”

Nuri smiled. “You noticed that.”

“I notice lots of things.” Elif smiled a little, too.

A car waited at the end of the neighborhood street. Nuri’s family had packed their things, and the move to the other district was still happening. It was not a pretend goodbye or a goodbye that could be postponed.

Nuri hugged Elif gently. Elif hugged back, pressing her cheek against Nuri’s shoulder for one quiet moment.

“Goodbye, Nuri,” Elif said.

“Goodbye, Elif. Keep looking for birds.”

“I will.”

Nuri walked toward the waiting car and turned to wave. Elif waved until the car disappeared behind the trees.

Then she returned to the community garden. The bench was empty, with a stripe of sunlight moving across its seat. Elif sat down and listened.

The garden sounded different without Nuri’s footsteps, but the leaves still rustled. Somewhere near the lake, the familiar bird called once.

Elif placed one hand over the blue card in her pocket. She was sad. She was steady, too. She let both feelings sit beside her as the sunlight slowly crossed the bench.
