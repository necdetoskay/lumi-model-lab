The Quiet Signal

After school, seven-year-old Deniz went to the public garden by the sea. The wind smelled salty, and gulls called over the path.

Deniz was looking toward the old observation point when—blink, blink, blink! Three pale flashes winked from its railing. They were quiet and quick. Then the railing went dull again.

Deniz counted to ten. Nothing happened. He counted to ten once more.

Blink, blink, blink!

The flashes came again. They had the same small spaces between them. Deniz felt curious, but a little worried too. Was someone sending a message? Did someone need help?

He looked around carefully. A man pushed a stroller. Two children fed crumbs to sparrows. A blue bicycle leaned against a tree. The fountain splashed. None of these seemed to send the flashes. No person stood at the old observation point, and no one waved from the path.

Deniz stayed on the garden path. He did not go up to the old point alone. He watched for one more minute, but the railing stayed quiet.

“Three flashes,” he whispered. “I need to find out what they mean before I guess.”

He decided to remember the time and visit the library the next day. Maybe a map or an old picture could help.

The next afternoon, Deniz went to the library with a small notebook. He sat near a window and drew the old observation point. He drew the railing too, with three tiny marks for the flashes.

“I must write what I saw,” he said. “Then I will not have to trust only my memory.”

He checked the library clock. In his notebook he wrote:

Yesterday: 3:40. Three flashes.
Today: 3:42. Three flashes.

He added, “The spaces between them were the same.”

The librarian, Ms. Aylin, brought him a town map. Deniz found the public garden and the old observation point. He traced a straight line between them with his finger.

“I can see the railing from the garden,” he said.

Ms. Aylin showed him an old photograph of the observation point. In the picture, a narrow metal strip shone along the railing. One end stuck out a little, like a loose silver ribbon.

Deniz leaned close. “That could be the bright thing I saw.”

He looked through the library window toward the sea. The afternoon sun was shining in the same direction as the observation point. Both times, the flashes had happened when sunlight reached that side of the railing.

Deniz drew a sun beside his notes. Then he drew a question mark.

“Sunlight might be part of it,” he said. “But a person could still be sending the flashes. I need to check more.”

He closed the notebook carefully. Now he had a map, two timed observations, and a photograph of the shiny strip. The flashes were still a mystery, but the mystery had begun to take shape.

The next day, Deniz took the notebook to the town workshop. Mr. Emre, the workshop guide, gave Deniz a small mirror, a piece of foil, and a lamp.

Deniz set the lamp on a table. He held the mirror so the light bounced onto a white wall. A bright spot appeared.

“What happens if the shiny thing moves?” Mr. Emre asked.

Deniz tilted the mirror a little. The bright spot slid across the wall. He tilted it again. Flash. Flash. The light jumped to the same small place each time he moved the mirror back and forth.

Deniz tried the foil next. He held it still. It made one soft, steady shine. Then he gently wiggled it. The wall showed separate flashes instead of one long shine.

Deniz looked at the old photograph in his notebook. The narrow metal strip stuck out from the railing like a loose silver ribbon.

“If wind moved that strip,” he said slowly, “sunlight might bounce off it more than once. It might make three flashes.”

Mr. Emre nodded. “That is a good idea to test. Is it safe to climb up and check it alone?”

“No,” Deniz said. “I should watch from the path with an adult.”

He drew the lamp, mirror, and foil beside his notes. Then he wrote:

Prediction: Wind may move the shiny strip and make three flashes.

Deniz closed the notebook. The little test showed how moving foil could make separate flashes, but it did not prove what was happening at the observation point. Tomorrow, he would look from a safe place with Mr. Emre. He still needed to see whether the real strip could move in the sea wind.

The next morning, Deniz carried the notebook to the old observation point with Mr. Emre. They stayed on the path below the railing.

“Can I climb up?” Deniz asked.

Mr. Emre shook his head. “No. We can see enough from here. What do you notice?”

Deniz looked through the railings. The narrow metal strip from the old photograph was bent away from the rail. One end stuck out like a tiny silver flag. Its edge lifted and dipped in the sea breeze.

“It is loose,” Deniz said.

Mr. Emre pointed, but did not touch it. “What else?”

Deniz leaned forward, keeping both feet on the path. White salt marks covered the strip and the rail beside it.

“Salt is on it,” Deniz said. “And the edge can turn.”

A cloud moved away from the sun. For one moment, the strip caught the light.

Flash!

Deniz’s heart gave a little jump. He checked the notebook. A moving shiny surface could make a flash. The strip really could move. But before the next flash, a gray cloud slid across the sun.

The railing went dull. The sea breeze still fluttered the loose edge, but no light bounced from it.

Deniz waited. The cloud stayed in place.

“We saw one,” he said. “But not three.”

Mr. Emre nodded. “So what do you know, and what do you still need to find out?”

Deniz wrote: Strip bends. Edge moves in wind. Salt marks. One flash. Clouds stopped the test.

“I know the strip might make the flashes,” he said. “I do not know yet if it can make all three.”

As they walked back, Deniz looked at the dark cloud. He wondered about the loose metal edge, too. Was it only a strange signal, or could it become a problem near the path? He needed one clear test before choosing what to report.

The next afternoon, Deniz and Mr. Emre stood in the public garden with the notebook open between them. The library clock said 3:40.

Deniz looked toward the old observation point. The sky was clear. Sunlight reached the railing, and the sea breeze lifted the loose metal edge.

“Watch carefully,” Deniz said.

The strip turned.

Flash.

Deniz held her pencil above the page. The strip dipped and turned again.

Flash.

A little gust moved across the garden. The edge lifted once more.

Flash.

Deniz made three small marks in the notebook. The spaces were the same as before. Three pale flashes, just as she had seen at 3:40 and 3:42. They came from the shiny strip, not from a person. No one stood near the railing. No hand waved. The strip moved only when the wind moved it, and sunlight bounced from it each time.

Mr. Emre smiled. “What do your notes tell you?”

Deniz read them aloud. “A still shiny thing makes one shine. A moving one can make separate flashes. The wind moved this strip, and the sun made the flashes.”

She looked at the bent edge. It was still loose above the path.

“I know what the signal is now,” Deniz said. “But the strip could come loose more. Someone should fix it.”

They walked to the garden caretaker’s small office. Deniz showed the caretaker her notebook. She pointed to the map, the photograph, the workshop drawing, and the notes from the cloudy day.

“I thought it might be an emergency signal,” Deniz explained. “But we watched at the right time. The wind moved the strip, and it made three flashes. It is not an emergency message. It is a loose piece of metal, and it could be unsafe.”

The caretaker studied the notes and then looked toward the observation point. “Thank you for showing me. I will secure it before anyone goes near that railing.”

Deniz felt her worry grow smaller. She had not guessed. She had watched, tested, and checked. The flashes had an answer, and the loose strip had a safe next step.

The next morning, the caretaker had finished the work. The metal strip was fastened flat against the railing. Its loose edge was gone, and no part of it hung over the path.

Deniz and Mr. Emre stood in the public garden and looked up from a safe distance. Deniz did not climb or touch the railing. She opened her notebook to the page marked 3:40.

The sun reached the observation point. The sea breeze moved the grass and rustled the trees.

But the railing stayed quiet.

No flash.
No flash.
No flash.

Deniz checked the clock. It was 3:40.

Mr. Emre asked, “What do you think happened?”

Deniz turned the pages of her notebook. “First, I saw three flashes. Then the map showed I could see the railing from the garden. The old photograph showed the shiny strip. At the workshop, the lamp and moving foil made separate flashes. At the observation point, I saw salt marks, a bent strip, and a loose edge. Wind moved it, and sunlight bounced off it.”

She pointed to the last page. “Yesterday, the real strip made three flashes at this time. Today it is fastened, so there are no flashes.”

“And no loose edge over the path,” said Mr. Emre.

Deniz nodded. The quiet felt good now. It was not a secret message or a danger warning. It was sunlight, wind, and a loose piece of metal making a pattern.

Deniz closed her notebook. She felt proud—not because she had guessed, but because she had watched carefully, tested her idea, and made a safe choice. The observation point was safe and quiet again.
