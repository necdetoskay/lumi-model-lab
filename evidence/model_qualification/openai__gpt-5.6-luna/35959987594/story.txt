The Signal in the Mist

Ada stood on the mist-covered coastal cliffs with her notebook tucked beneath one arm. Below, the sea folded gently against the rocks, and dark openings led to the tidal caves. She had come to watch the strange evening light.

For three evenings, it had appeared at nearly the same time. Tonight, just as the sun slipped behind the cloud bank, a pale blink shone near the shoreline.

Ada held her breath and counted. One, two, three... The light glimmered again, then faded. A few moments later, it flashed once more. After only a few minutes, the mist thickened, and the light vanished completely.

“Not a guess yet,” Ada said, writing carefully. “Appears near sunset. Lasts about four minutes. Seen from the west cliff.”

She looked around for changes. Beside the path stood an old waystone, rounded by wind and salt. Its carved marks were softened, but a wave-shaped line could still be seen on one side. Ada drew it in her notebook.

Near the cliff edge, the tide had left a dark line across the rocks. Freshly exposed seaweed clung below it, bright green and shining with drops. Yesterday, that seaweed had been covered by water.

Ada glanced toward the misty cave mouths. She was curious about the light, but curiosity did not mean rushing. The waystone, the tide line, and the newly uncovered seaweed might each tell part of the story.

She marked their positions, checked the steady path beneath her boots, and began walking toward the tidal caves—without entering them. First, she would study the markings and the tide. Then she would decide what the evidence allowed her to believe.

The next evening, Ada returned to the old waystone before the light was due. She had spent the day thinking about its softened wave-shaped mark.

“Perhaps the stone is sending the signal,” she said. “It stands in just the right place.”

Still, she did not write that down as a fact. She crouched beside the waystone and examined its seaward face. The stone was cool, rough, and gray. No glow shone from its cracks. It gave off no warmth. Even when Ada tilted her small looking glass toward it, the surface showed only dull stone and a smear of salt.

She drew three careful marks in her notebook: no light, no warmth, no bright surface.

Then the evening sun slipped through a break in the clouds.

A pale flash appeared—not on the waystone, but across the cliff, farther toward the misty shoreline. Ada turned quickly. A thin streak of brightness was sliding along a dark, wet band of rock. It moved as the sun lowered, shining for a moment and then fading.

Ada followed the streak with her eyes. It was not coming from the waystone. The wet stone was catching the light and carrying it along its uneven surface, almost like a tiny traveling wave.

She checked the waystone again. It remained quiet and dark.

“So my first idea was wrong,” Ada said, and crossed out her sentence. “The waystone does not make the signal. It may point toward something that reflects it.”

Below her, the tide line still marked the rocks, and the freshly exposed seaweed trembled in the breeze. The nearest cave mouths waited beyond the mist, but Ada did not hurry toward them. If the waystone was a guide, its wave mark and the changing tide might explain where to go.

She copied the moving reflection, the dark wet band, and the waystone’s position into her notebook. Then she watched the last glimmer vanish, ready to study the remaining marks before stepping into the caves.

Ada returned to the waystone the next afternoon, while the mist still rested lightly over the cliffs. She knelt beside its softened wave-shaped mark and copied it into her notebook. Then she looked toward the rocks below.

The tide had risen since yesterday. A dark, wet line crossed the stones, and the stepping stones that led toward the nearest cave were mostly covered. Ada did not try to cross. Instead, she searched the waystone more carefully.

Near the bottom of the carved wave, she found a tiny notch. It was easy to miss, almost hidden beneath a strip of pale seaweed. Ada brushed the seaweed aside and compared its position with the shore. The notch sat at the same height as the lowest band of fresh seaweed on the rocks—the band left when the water had recently been there.

“Maybe this is not just a wave,” Ada said. “Maybe it tells me when the path is ready.”

She drew a line between the notch, the seaweed band, and the covered stepping stones. The idea was useful, but Ada wanted another mark to test it.

Following the cliff path inland, she found a second old waystone half veiled by mist. Its face held two worn lines: one curved like a wave, and one pointed toward the shoreline. Ada turned until the pointed line aimed at a narrow cave opening across the rocks. From there, she could see the tops of several stepping stones leading toward it.

The route was becoming clearer. The first waystone showed a tide height. The second pointed toward a particular cave. The seaweed showed that the tide was still too high, because the lowest band had not yet been reached by the falling water.

Ada marked the spot where the safe stones would appear. Then she sat on a dry ledge and waited, watching the water slip quietly away. One stone emerged, then another, their rounded tops shining with wetness.

She did not hurry. When the water lowered to the seaweed band, the stepping stones formed an unbroken path.

Ada packed her notebook under her coat and took one careful step toward the cave. The markings had shown her when and where to go, but they had not yet told her what waited beyond the mist.

Ada stepped from stone to stone, following the path the tide had uncovered. Each rounded top was damp beneath her boots, but the water had fallen to the seaweed band, just as the first waystone had shown. Ahead, the cave opening waited beneath a curtain of mist.

Inside, the air felt cool and quiet. Water made a soft, ticking sound as it slipped from the roof. Ada set one hand against the wall and moved slowly, watching where she placed her feet. The cave was not dark; a pale evening glow rested near the entrance.

Then a narrow streak of light slid across the wet wall.

Ada stopped. The streak moved just as the reflection had moved across the cliff outside, traveling along the shining stone while the sun lowered. It reached a bend in the wall and faded. A moment later, another thin line appeared a little farther along.

“So the cave changes the path of the light,” Ada murmured. “But I still need to see where it begins.”

She waited beside a flat rock and listened to the water. Foam gathered at the edge of a shallow pool, then slowly pulled back. As it retreated, it uncovered a narrow passage between two smooth walls. Beyond the passage, a pale patch showed through a thin water curtain.

Ada did not step into the passage. The water was still moving, and the safe route ended at the dry rock beneath her. Instead, she took out her notebook and sketched the cave entrance, the stepping stones, the wet wall, and the newly visible patch. She added arrows for the direction of the sliding light and a small mark for the seaweed level outside.

When the foam crept forward again, the pale patch blurred behind the water. When it drew back, the patch returned.

Ada studied her drawing. The waystone had shown the tide level. The tide had uncovered the stepping stones and the narrow passage. The lowering sun had sent light along the wet wall. All the clues seemed to meet here, but she needed to watch the exact alignment once more before deciding what the pale patch meant.

Ada stood on the dry cliff path outside the tidal cave, safely beyond the stepping stones. She had returned before the tide covered the lowest step and would not cross back over it. From the cave mouth, she could still see the wet wall and the pale patch beyond the narrow passage. Her notebook lay open beside her.

The seaweed mark had shown the safe tide level, and the waystone had guided her to the cave. Now she remained outside and watched the final alignment from this secure vantage point.

The sun lowered. A beam slipped through the mist and touched the wet wall inside the cave. It traveled along the shining surface, just as the streak had moved across the cliff outside. Then it reached the pale patch beyond the water curtain.

For one bright moment, the patch flashed silver-white. Light sprang across the cave, skimmed the wall, and shone toward the entrance.

Ada studied the pattern without leaving the dry path. The flash was not coming from the waystone. That stone had helped her find the right tide and passage. The lowest seaweed band had shown when the stepping stones would be safe. The lowering sun had supplied the beam, and the smooth pale patch had caught it.

“Quartz,” Ada said softly. She had seen pieces of it before, clear and pale, shining when wet. “Wet quartz is reflecting the evening sun.”

The water curtain thickened as the tide turned. A little wave slid across the passage and covered the pale patch. The silver flash weakened, broke into dull glimmers, and vanished.

Ada compared the moment with her notebook. The signal had lasted only a few minutes, just as it had on the cliff. The waystone mark had led her to the cave, the seaweed tide line had marked the safe timing, the cave’s position had aligned the beam, and the wet quartz had reflected it. Each clue supported the others.

She recorded the quartz reflection and the tide-covered disappearance, then closed her notebook. Before starting home, she noticed one faint second mark continuing along the inland side of the waystone. It was not needed to explain the light, so she recorded it for another careful day.

Ada headed along the cliff path, carrying a complete answer to the evening mystery and leaving the rising tide safely behind her.

Ada reached the cliff path just before the tide covered the lowest stepping stone. She stopped on the dry grass and listened to the gentle water below. The cave was quiet again, its bright secret safely understood.

She opened her notebook beneath the nearest waystone and turned to a fresh page. First she drew the lowest seaweed band and the notch in the wave-shaped mark. Beside it, she sketched the stepping stones that appeared when the tide fell. Then she drew the cave wall, the narrow passage, and the pale quartz patch behind the water curtain.

“Tide, waystone, sunlight, and wet quartz,” she wrote. “All four are needed.”

She added the time the flash began and the time it vanished. The evening sun had touched the quartz for only a few minutes. Then the rising water had covered it, just as her observations had predicted. The waystone had not made the light at all. It had helped her know when and where to look.

Ada closed the notebook, but before she started home, the mist lifted from the inland side of the path. More of the old waystone came into view. There, beneath a patch of lichen, was the faint second symbol she had noticed earlier. It continued away from the coast, a small line of marks disappearing into the grass.

Ada tilted her head. The symbol was not part of the route to the cave, and it could not change the answer she had found. She did not guess what it meant. She simply drew it carefully in her notebook and placed a tiny question mark beside it.

The mist drifted back, soft and silver. Ada smiled and tucked away her pencil.

One mystery had become an answer because she had watched, waited, and compared each clue. The new marking could wait too. She followed the cliff path home, carrying her notes and one quiet question for another patient day.
