The Missing Pattern

Deniz was seven, and she liked getting things ready by herself. Before visitors came, the room guide gave her one simple job: “Please check the Pattern Trail table.”

The public learning space was full of useful things. Maps hung on the wall. A basket held four smooth stones. Small labels, sharpened pencils, and a wooden ruler waited on the table. A stack of picture cards sat beside the ruler.

Deniz looked at the trail schedule. It said:

MAP — ____ — LABELS — DRAW

She read it twice. The empty place made her pause.

Maybe a card had been lost. Maybe the blank was meant to be there. Or maybe someone had changed the order and forgotten to fix the schedule.

Deniz picked up a card, then put it down again. Choosing one just because it looked right would be guessing. She touched the blank line with her finger and studied the things around it instead.

The map, basket, labels, pencils, and ruler were all ready. But the trail was not. Something belonged in the empty place, and Deniz did not yet know what it was.

She took a pencil and opened a clear space on the table. First, she would look for clues.

Deniz began with the map. It showed three stops: a blue circle, a yellow square, and a red triangle. She copied the colors on her paper with a pencil: blue, yellow, red.

Then she looked at the labels. Three labels had the same colored marks. One had a blue circle. One had a yellow square. One had a red triangle.

Deniz peeked into the basket. Three stones had matching colors, too. A blue mark showed on one stone, a yellow mark on another, and a red mark on a third. The fourth stone had no colored mark that she could see.

She lined up the map, the labels, and the basket. Blue, yellow, red. The same three colors traveled through all three places.

But what did that mean for the blank in the schedule?

Deniz read the labels without picking up any stones. Each label had words and a little picture. Visitors could read them while the stones stayed in the basket.

“Maybe the trail skips the objects,” Deniz said softly. “Maybe it goes MAP, LABELS, then DRAW because the room is busy.”

That idea still seemed possible. The labels were ready to read, and the schedule did not say anything about touching a stone.

Deniz did not write the idea into the blank. Instead, she placed her pencil beside her color notes. The three colors were a pattern, but she needed to test what the pattern asked visitors to do.

Deniz began with the labels again. She kept the stones in the basket and read the words and pictures.

One label said, “Smooth stone.” Another said, “Round stone.” Deniz looked at the basket. Two stones looked smooth and round. Without touching them, she could not tell which label belonged to which stone.

“Reading first may not be enough,” she said.

She took out the stone with the blue mark. Then she found the blue circle on the map and placed the blue label beside them. The three clues agreed: blue map stop, blue-marked stone, blue label.

Next, Deniz checked the yellow square. She matched the yellow-marked stone to the yellow stop and the yellow label. It fit, too.

Last came the red triangle. Deniz matched the red-marked stone to the red stop and the red label. Now each stone had one clear place and one clear label.

Deniz looked at the two labels again. By themselves, they had seemed confusing. With the colored marks, they made sense.

“So the trail cannot skip the stones,” she said. “Visitors need to match first. Then they can read the label.”

She wrote a careful line beneath her color notes: MAP, MATCH, LABELS, DRAW.

The new order looked right, but Deniz was not ready to fill the blank yet. She still needed to find the picture card that showed matching and check that the whole trail worked for every object.

Deniz looked beside the ruler, where the picture cards had been stacked. Two cards lay near the edge. One showed a clock. The other showed two shapes joined by a line.

“A clock could mean the order,” Deniz said. She placed the clock card below her pencil note. It fit the schedule because a schedule tells when things happen. But it did not show what visitors should do with the map, stones, and labels. Deniz slid it back beside the ruler.

Then she picked up the card with the joined shapes. The shapes looked like two pieces coming together.

“Match,” Deniz said. “Put two things together.”

She tested it instead of trusting the picture. First, she found the blue stop on the map. She placed the blue-marked stone beside it. Next came the yellow stop and yellow-marked stone. Last came the red stop and red-marked stone.

Deniz used the ruler to check the little rows. Each colored map stop had one nearby marked stone. There was no need to add a guessed stone. The fourth stone, with no colored mark, stayed at the side of the basket.

The joined-shape card worked for all three pairs. It showed the action between the map and the objects. The clock card had worked only as an idea about the schedule.

Deniz looked at the schedule, then at her pencil notes.

“MAP, MATCH, LABELS, DRAW,” she said. “That order works each time.”

She held the matching card over the blank space, but did not put it down yet. The trail needed one more careful check: which objects truly belonged on the finished table?

Deniz placed the joined-shape card in the blank space.

“MAP, MATCH, LABELS, DRAW,” she read.

She began with the blue stop. She put the blue-marked stone beside it, then found the label with the blue mark. The label named that stone. Deniz set the label beside the stone.

Next came yellow. The yellow stop, yellow-marked stone, and yellow label belonged together too. Then red matched red in the same way.

Deniz checked each little row with the ruler. One map stop had one marked stone. Each stone had one matching label. The pattern worked three times, not just once.

At the side of the basket sat the fourth stone. It was smooth and gray. It looked interesting, but it had no blue, yellow, or red mark. Deniz held it near the map. There was no stop for it. She held it near the labels. None matched it.

“I could put it with the gray-looking label,” she said. Then she shook her head. “But that would be a guess.”

She left the fourth stone in the basket and chose the three checked stones for the display. With a pencil, she drew a small picture of the unmarked stone and wrote:

NO MATCH YET.

The room guide came over to see the table.

“Why is this one set aside?” the guide asked.

“Because the map has only three colored stops,” Deniz said. “I matched the stops, stones, and labels. The fourth stone has no mark and no label that fits, so I did not guess.”

The guide nodded. “That is a careful choice.”

Deniz looked once more at the schedule, the map, the three stones, the labels, and her note. Everything that visitors needed was ready. The unmatched stone stayed safely to the side, with its clear question waiting beside it.

Soon, the first visitors came to the table. They looked at the schedule.

“MAP, MATCH, LABELS, DRAW,” one visitor read.

They followed the blue line on the map. They matched the blue-marked stone to the blue stop, then read its label. Next they matched yellow, and then red. Each time, the color helped them choose the right stone. The labels told them what they had found.

At the end, the visitors used the pencils to draw the stones and their colored marks. No one needed to guess. The map, the stones, and the labels worked together.

One visitor pointed to the fourth stone in the basket. “What about that one?”

Deniz showed the small note. “It has no matching map stop or label yet. We can save it for an activity with more labels.”

The room guide smiled. “That is right. You checked before you chose.”

Deniz touched the matching card in the schedule. “I checked one clue, then checked another,” she said. “The map showed where to look. The mark helped me match. Then the label told me what it was.”

The guide nodded. “And when the clues did not match the fourth stone, you told the truth about it.”

More visitors came in. They followed the trail in the same order and made careful drawings. The Pattern Trail was open, and every part on the table had a reason to be there.

Deniz watched the blue, yellow, and red rows. Testing the pattern had helped her make the right choice. The gray stone stayed safely beside its note, ready for a properly labeled activity another day.
