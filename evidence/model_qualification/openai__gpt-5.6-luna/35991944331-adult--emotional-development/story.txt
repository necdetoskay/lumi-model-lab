The Space Between Words

At thirty-two, Deniz had learned that being dependable did not mean waiting for someone else to say what needed doing. At the neighborhood community space, they tracked the folding chairs, borrowed projector, key box, and small promises that made events work. They knew which regulars arrived early, which kettle leaked, and where the large-print signs were kept.

The accessibility open house mattered to Deniz's reputation and to the partnership they had built with Selin. Over the past month, they had shaped a day when neighbors could test the ramp, ask about captioning and quiet rooms, and suggest changes without navigating polite excuses. Their names were both on the volunteer announcements.

Deniz arrived with a canvas bag of extension cords and found three regular volunteers near the reception desk. Selin was speaking to them in a low voice.

"The funder wants the access notes organized by Friday," Selin said. "If we move the draft—"

She looked up as Deniz entered. Her sentence stopped. Then she touched the folder in front of her.

"Let's take this into the side room," Selin said.

The volunteers followed. Selin did not wave Deniz in, though the door remained partly open. Deniz stood beside the welcome table, still holding the cords.

Their stomach tightened first. Heat climbed into their face, followed by pressure in their chest and an urge to laugh as if nothing mattered. Another urge told them to walk into the room and demand an explanation.

Hurt, Deniz thought. Wary.

The words did not settle the feeling, but they gave it edges.

They opened the shared planning document on their phone. The version they remembered had listed Selin and Deniz together under “program and access.” Now Deniz's name was gone from the program notes, several headings had moved, and no comment explained why.

A month earlier, after staying late to clean up, Deniz had messaged Selin: I can't keep being the person who always cleans up afterward. The message had been tired and sharper than intended. Deniz had meant that the work needed to be shared, not that they wanted to step away from the open house.

A volunteer named Murat came out carrying clipboards. "Do these go by the entrance?"

"Yes," Deniz said. "Put the symbol cards beside them."

The familiar routine steadied them briefly. Then the side-room door opened. Selin emerged with the folder tucked against her ribs.

"I need to finish something before we circulate it," she said. Her voice was polite, but she did not explain what had changed. "Can you check the quiet-room signs?"

Deniz looked at the folder, then at Selin. "Sure," they said, because the signs needed checking and the question behind their teeth felt too large for the reception desk.

In the storage closet, Deniz counted the signs twice. The facts were simple: Selin had moved the discussion, stopped speaking when Deniz arrived, and changed the planning document without telling them. The story arriving behind those facts was sharper. Selin was shutting them out, keeping Deniz for setup but not for real decisions.

Deniz did not know whether that story was true. Still, by closing time, they were acting as if it were. They packed the cords, returned the key to its hook, and left believing Selin was deliberately sidelining them.

Deniz almost went back the next morning and asked Selin, in the middle of the reception desk, what exactly she thought she was doing. The question felt satisfying because it already contained an answer.

Instead, Deniz opened the planning document on a laptop in the community space's small workroom. The room smelled faintly of coffee and printer paper. Through the glass wall, Murat was sorting symbol cards into labeled trays, and someone in the kitchen was testing the kettle. The ordinary sounds made it harder to turn the situation into a private drama and easier to look at what was actually there.

Deniz opened the document's version history.

Selin had changed the headings the previous afternoon at 4:18. At 4:22, she had added a note beside the program section: Funder requires access details separated by activity. Draft not ready to circulate. Please hold comments until Friday review.

A calendar entry appeared next. Grant draft review—Friday, 10 a.m. The description included a deadline from the funder and a list of unfinished items: captioning costs, quiet-room staffing, and the wording about personal information collected from visitors. Selin had added the entry after their side-room conversation began.

Deniz read the note twice. It explained something. It did not explain everything.

The document still omitted Deniz from the program notes. Selin had made that decision without asking or even leaving a comment directed to them. The restriction was real. So was the silence around it.

Deniz clicked into the message thread.

Their own message sat near the top, sent at 11:47 the night before: I can't keep being the person who always cleans up afterward. I'm too tired to keep absorbing the last-minute work. We need a different arrangement.

Below it, Selin had replied the next morning: I hear you. Let's reduce what you're carrying for the open house.

Deniz remembered reading that reply and leaving it unanswered because they had been on a bus, late for another commitment. Now the words looked different. Reduce what you're carrying could mean share the cleanup. It could also mean stop giving Deniz a central role.

The next messages were practical: a question about chairs, a reminder about the access signs, then Selin's note about needing to keep the grant materials limited until the requirements were clear. There was no sentence saying, I am taking you off this. There was also no sentence saying, I have changed the plan and here is why.

Deniz leaned back. Their body had begun supplying its familiar conclusion: Selin had decided Deniz was difficult, useful for lifting and tidying but not worth consulting. It was a quick story, built from real details and filled in with guesses.

They opened a blank note and made two columns.

Observed: Selin moved the discussion. She stopped when Deniz entered. She changed access to the planning document. She removed Deniz's name from the program notes without explaining it. The draft had a funder deadline and unfinished privacy language. Deniz's message had come after a difficult cleanup and said they could not keep absorbing last-minute work.

Assumed: Selin wanted to sideline Deniz. Selin had decided they were unreliable. Selin meant to punish them for complaining.

The second column made Deniz wince, but it also loosened something in their chest. None of those assumptions was impossible. None was evidence yet.

They returned to the calendar. Friday's deadline was close enough that an angry confrontation could damage the open house, but close enough that avoiding the issue would be irresponsible. Deniz needed to know whether Selin had been temporarily controlling an unfinished draft or changing their partnership. The records could narrow the question. They could not answer it.

Deniz saved a copy of the current planning document, then closed the laptop. Before speaking to Selin, they would watch what happened with the materials and ask directly instead of making the most painful explanation do all the work.

For now, the evidence held two truths together: Deniz had been excluded from a decision, and Selin had reasons to believe Deniz was exhausted and wanted less responsibility. Neither truth canceled the other.

The next afternoon, Deniz arrived before the drop-in hours began. At thirty-two, they were used to noticing what a community space needed before anyone asked: the chairs arranged with clear paths between them, the kettle filled, the sensory supplies returned to their labeled drawers. They could work while unsettled. They just could not pretend that being unsettled made every conclusion reliable.

Selin was at the long table with the grant folder open beside her laptop. When Murat came in to ask about the quiet room, Selin closed the folder halfway.

"The draft isn't ready for circulation yet," she said. "I can show you the public sections after Friday's review."

Later, Aylin asked whether she could check the staffing figures. Selin gave her the same answer. She kept the folder angled toward herself and did not open the restricted document for anyone.

Deniz watched from the supply shelves, carrying a box of large-print labels. Selin was not treating Deniz differently in that moment. The unfinished material was being kept from everyone. That mattered. It did not erase the other fact: Selin had moved the earlier discussion and removed Deniz from the program notes without explaining the decision.

Both things could be true. Deniz repeated that silently while placing the labels in their drawer.

At the reception desk, Murat began telling Aylin that the planning group seemed tense. Deniz felt the familiar tug to say something—perhaps to mention the side-room meeting, the missing name, the message thread. A few sympathetic looks might have made the situation feel less lonely. They might also have turned an uncertain problem into a group opinion before Deniz had asked Selin a direct question.

"The grant draft is still being checked," Deniz said instead. "We should wait for the update before guessing about the open house plan."

Murat nodded and returned to sorting the symbol cards. The moment passed without becoming gossip.

During a quiet stretch, Deniz opened the blank note from yesterday. This time they wrote only what could help with a conversation.

Three observations:

Selin moved the planning discussion into the side room and stopped when I entered.

My access to the shared program notes changed without an explanation.

Selin limited the same unfinished grant materials when other people asked to see them.

Then one feeling: I feel hurt and wary.

Deniz paused over the words. Hurt described what had happened inside them. Wary described the caution that followed. Neither word proved that Selin had betrayed them. Neither word required Deniz to dismiss the unilateral decision.

Underneath, Deniz wrote two questions.

What did you intend when you moved the discussion and changed my access?

When you read my message about always cleaning up afterward, what did you think I was asking for?

The second question made Deniz uncomfortable. Their message had been tired and sharp. It had asked for a different arrangement, but it had not said clearly that they still wanted to co-lead the accessibility open house. Deniz could own that lack of clarity without taking responsibility for a silence Selin had chosen.

They looked toward the table. The grant deadline was still Friday at ten. Waiting indefinitely would not protect the open house; it would leave tasks drifting while both of them worked from different assumptions. Bringing the regulars into the disagreement would be unfair. They had come to use the space, prepare materials, and trust the coordinators to communicate.

So Deniz sent Selin a short message: Could we talk privately tomorrow before the open-space routine? I want to compare what we each understood about the planning changes. I have two questions, and I want us to keep the Friday deadline in view.

Selin read it several minutes later. Her reply was brief: Yes. Before opening works.

Deniz put the phone face down. The reply did not settle the matter, and their stomach did not suddenly loosen. But the next step was clear. They would bring the observations, name the hurt, and ask instead of announcing a verdict. The question of whether they could still share responsibility remained open, waiting for the conversation.

Before the open-space routine began, Deniz found Selin in the small meeting room with two mugs of tea between them. The room had a glass wall facing the corridor, so familiar faces passed without hearing. Deniz left the door partly open.

“I want to start with what I wrote down,” Deniz said. Their voice sounded steadier than their hands felt. “I felt shut out when the meeting moved and my access disappeared. Did you mean to exclude me?”

Selin looked at the table rather than answering immediately. “I meant to keep the unfinished grant material contained. The funder wants the access details separated by activity, and there are privacy-sensitive staffing notes in the draft. I was worried that a partial version would travel before we had checked it.”

“That explains why the folder was restricted,” Deniz said. “It doesn’t explain why my name came off the program notes, or why you didn’t tell me.”

“No,” Selin said. “I should have explained that. I thought I was making a temporary change until the review.”

“A temporary change made without me.”

Selin’s mouth tightened. “Yes.”

The acknowledgment landed, but it did not make Deniz’s chest relax. They opened their notebook and touched the two questions they had written there.

“I’m trying not to turn an interpretation into a fact,” Deniz said. “So I need to ask what you intended. When you moved the discussion and changed my access, were you trying to keep me out of the open-house planning?”

“No. I was trying to keep the draft from circulating, and I was also responding to your message.” Selin lifted her eyes. “You wrote that you couldn’t keep absorbing last-minute work and that you were done cleaning up after everyone. I read it as you wanting less responsibility for the open house. I thought removing you from the notes was part of reducing what you were carrying.”

Deniz let out a slow breath. The sentence had sounded different in their own head, where it had been a tired demand for a fairer arrangement. “I was exhausted,” they said. “And the wording was sharp. I can see how it sounded like withdrawal. But I wasn’t withdrawing from the open house. I was saying I couldn’t keep doing the invisible cleanup alone.”

“I didn’t hear that second part,” Selin said. “I heard that the workload was too much.”

“And then you decided what I wanted without checking.”

“Yes,” Selin said, more quietly. “I did.”

Deniz looked through the glass wall. Aylin was arranging symbol cards at the reception desk, following the familiar routine. The open house was still waiting for them: the quiet-room signs, the volunteer schedule, the access notes. None of those tasks had become simpler because the two explanations now fit together.

“I understand the privacy concern,” Deniz said. “I also need you to understand that the silence damaged my trust. When I saw my name gone, then watched the discussion stop when I entered, it felt deliberate. Even if that wasn’t your intention, that is what happened for me.”

Selin nodded, though her expression remained guarded. “I understand that it felt deliberate. I’m not sure yet what I should have done differently while the draft was unfinished.”

“Tell me what is restricted and why. Keep me in the planning conversation even if I can’t see every note.”

“That sounds reasonable,” Selin said. “But we still need to decide who handles the actual work. I thought you wanted out. You thought I was pushing you out.”

“Exactly.” Deniz’s voice caught on the word. “And I don’t want us to solve that by guessing again.”

Selin’s phone vibrated on the table. She glanced at it, then stood so quickly that her chair scraped the floor. “The funder’s review link just opened. They’ve sent the access form we need to complete before the ten o’clock check-in.”

Deniz checked their own phone. The calendar reminder was already glowing: **Grant review—nine minutes**.

For a moment neither moved. The practical deadline had arrived before they had agreed on the emotional one.

“We have nine minutes,” Selin said. “I can submit the access form, but I still don’t know how we divide the open-house work.”

“And I still don’t know what repair looks like beyond hearing an explanation,” Deniz said.

Selin picked up the grant folder. “Then we pause this conversation, not end it.”

Deniz closed the notebook. They understood more than they had when they entered: Selin had been protecting unfinished, privacy-sensitive material and had also made an assumption; Deniz’s message had been unclear, and Selin’s silence had still caused harm. Understanding both sides did not yet restore trust or assign a single task.

“After the review,” Deniz said, “we come back to this and decide in writing.”

Selin met their eyes. “After the review.”

They left the room together, carrying the unresolved conversation with them.

The access form took six minutes. Selin read each question aloud while Deniz checked the activity notes against the calendar. They caught one mistake in the quiet-room description and corrected it before Selin submitted the form.

When the confirmation appeared, Selin closed the laptop but did not pick up the grant folder.

“Now,” she said.

Deniz returned to the small meeting room and opened their notebook. The effort of staying calm had left them tired, but the tiredness felt cleaner than the uncertainty from earlier.

“Before we decide tasks,” Deniz said, “I want to make sure we heard each other accurately. I heard that you restricted the draft because it had unfinished, privacy-sensitive information. I also heard that you read my message as a request to step back from the open house.”

Selin nodded. “And I heard that you were asking for shared cleanup and shared responsibility, not leaving the project. I also heard that removing your name and not explaining it made you feel deliberately pushed out.”

“Not just feel,” Deniz said. “The exclusion was real. The meaning I attached to it—that you wanted me out—was an assumption. But the decision was still made without me, and that hurt.”

Selin folded her hands on the table. “Yes. Both things are true. I had a reason to control the draft, but I handled the planning change badly.”

Deniz let the words settle. They did not erase the tight place beneath their ribs. Still, they made room for the next sentence.

“I was unclear,” Deniz said. “My message came after a terrible cleanup, and I used sharper words than I should have. I accept responsibility for that. I don’t accept responsibility for being silently removed from the work instead of being asked what I meant.”

“You shouldn’t,” Selin said. “I should have asked.”

A quiet knock sounded on the glass. Aylin pointed toward the reception desk, where two volunteers were waiting with a question about the open-house schedule. Deniz raised a finger to signal they would be there soon. The work was still moving around them; it could not be left suspended while they examined every feeling.

Deniz turned back to Selin. “Here is what would help me trust the process again. Give me access to the completed sections, even if the unfinished privacy-sensitive parts stay restricted. Add a decision log to the shared folder. If something changes, record what changed, why, and who needs to know.”

Selin opened the laptop. “I can do that now.”

She shared the folder with Deniz’s account and adjusted the permissions. The completed access overview and volunteer schedule appeared on Deniz’s screen. The staffing notes remained marked **restricted—review pending**.

“I’ll state the unfinished items plainly,” Selin said, typing as she spoke. “Staffing details need privacy review. Captioning costs need one receipt. The quiet-room plan needs confirmation from the sensory-access adviser. Nothing here is being hidden from you; these sections simply aren’t ready to circulate.”

Deniz read the new entry. “That distinction would have prevented a lot of guessing.”

“I know.” Selin added a column titled **Decision, reason, next check-in**. “You’ll be able to comment on completed sections. I’ll keep the restricted material identified without opening it beyond the people who need it.”

The change was practical, visible, and smaller than an apology could pretend to be. Deniz still felt wary. But the document no longer required them to guess where they stood.

They moved to the task list. Deniz took responsibility for the accessibility signs, the quiet-room layout, and the volunteer briefing. Selin owned the final grant attachments, the privacy review, and contact with the funder. They shared the program schedule and agreed that either could flag a problem in the decision log instead of changing the plan alone.

“At four,” Deniz said, “we check the open-house list together. Before submission, we do one last review. If either of us is overloaded, we say so directly rather than writing something that can be read three different ways.”

Selin gave a brief, tired smile. “Directly sounds safer.”

“Clearer, at least.”

They drafted an update for the regular collaborators:

**The open-house plan is being revised for the funder’s review. Deniz and Selin are sharing program coordination. The completed sections are available in the planning folder; items marked restricted are still under privacy review. Please add questions to the decision log so changes stay visible.**

Deniz read it twice. It explained the work without turning their private misunderstanding into community news.

“Send it?” Selin asked.

“Send it.”

The message went to Aylin, the volunteers, and the two regular facilitators who had been waiting for the schedule. Almost immediately, Aylin replied with a check mark and a question about the quiet-room chairs. Deniz added the question to the log. Selin answered with the current plan and marked it for the four o’clock check-in.

They were working together again, though ease had not returned with the shared folder. Deniz could still remember the moment their name disappeared. Selin could still remember reading the exhausted message and deciding too quickly what it meant.

At the door, Deniz paused. “I’m glad we finished this conversation. I’m not back to feeling completely easy yet.”

Selin nodded. “I don’t expect you to be. I’ll make the changes anyway.”

Deniz stepped into the corridor, where the volunteers were ready for the next task. The hurt had not vanished, but it now had a name, a record, and a plan for what would happen next.

By Saturday morning, the community space had changed its usual shape. The reception table stood beneath a large accessibility sign, the quiet room held two rows of chairs with a clear path between them, and a printed schedule sat beside the tea urn. Deniz arrived early, as they often did, but this time the work was not theirs to discover and absorb alone. Their name appeared beside the volunteer briefing and quiet-room setup on the shared task list. Selin’s name was beside the funder materials and the afternoon program.

At thirty-two, Deniz was used to carrying practical responsibility: unlocking doors, checking supplies, answering messages, and noticing what had been forgotten. Today, that independence felt steadier because the information was visible. They could see what they owned and what Selin owned without having to infer the boundary from silence.

Aylin came in with a stack of large-print handouts. “The first group is nearly here,” she said. “Do we put these by the entrance or in the quiet room?”

Deniz glanced toward the decision log, open on the reception tablet. The question was not difficult, but the answer depended on the flow of the room. Earlier, Deniz might have chosen quickly and hoped it matched Selin’s plan. Instead, they looked across the space.

“Selin, can I check something?”

Selin set down the name tags she was sorting. “Of course.”

“Are the large-print handouts meant to be available in both places, or only at reception?”

“Both,” Selin said. “The reception copies are for anyone arriving. The quiet-room copies are for people who want to settle there without coming back through the crowd.” She walked over and added the arrangement to the decision log, including the reason and the next check-in after lunch.

“Good,” Deniz said. “Aylin, could you put half in each place?”

Aylin nodded and carried the handouts away. No one needed to know that the question mattered beyond the placement of paper. The regulars simply saw two coordinators making the plan easier to use.

The open house filled gradually. A familiar older couple tested the clear route from the entrance to the seating area. Two parents asked about the quieter room. A volunteer guided visitors toward the captioned presentation and returned to the desk when the schedule changed. Deniz gave the briefing, explained the room’s purpose, and adjusted the chairs when a wheelchair user said the turning space felt narrow. Selin answered questions about the program and marked completed items on the log.

Once, Selin approached with her phone in hand. “The funder’s representative is asking whether the sensory-access adviser confirmed the quiet-room plan.”

“Did they?” Deniz asked.

“Yes. The confirmation came in last night. I added it to the folder.”

“Then let’s show them the completed note, not just tell them.”

Selin opened the folder and turned the screen toward Deniz before sending the reply. The answer went out with the confirmation attached. It was a small exchange, almost invisible among the movement of the open house, but Deniz noticed the difference: information was offered instead of guarded, and a question could be answered without becoming a test of loyalty.

By late afternoon, the last visitors had left. The regular facilitators folded tables while Aylin returned the sign-in sheets to the supply cabinet. No one lingered to judge Deniz and Selin or ask what had happened between them. They had been given a clear update, a workable plan, and a successful event; that was enough.

Deniz and Selin sat together at the reception table with the decision log between them. They recorded what had worked: the printed schedule, the two locations for handouts, the quiet-room route, and the practice of checking changes before announcing them. They also noted that the volunteer briefing needed ten extra minutes next time.

Selin closed the laptop. “You asked instead of filling in the answer yourself.”

“I was tempted to fill it in,” Deniz said. “Asking was faster than repairing a wrong assumption later.”

Selin smiled, though neither of them treated the moment as proof that every future misunderstanding would be easy. “The log helped.”

“So did the explanation.”

They packed the remaining handouts together. The old ease had not returned all at once. Deniz could still remember the hurt of seeing their name removed, and Selin could still remember how quickly she had interpreted the exhausted message. But now, when either of them needed to know what the other meant, there was a practiced way to ask, answer, and leave the answer where both could find it.

At the door, Deniz checked the lock and Selin checked the schedule for the next meeting. Then they left the community space side by side, with the decision log saved for the work ahead.
