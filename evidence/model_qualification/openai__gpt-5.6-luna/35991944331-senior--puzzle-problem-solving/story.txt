The Missing Pattern

At seventy, Deniz still preferred to begin with the evidence in front of her. She arrived early at the public learning space, set her canvas bag beneath the worktable, and switched on the long row of lights. The room smelled faintly of paper, wood, and the coffee that had been served at the morning meeting.

Her task was posted beside the entrance: CANAL WALK MAP ACTIVITY — 4:00. Beneath it, in smaller print, was the note: ROOM B AVAILABLE AS BACKUP.

Deniz carried the notice board’s information in her mind, but she copied the time and room note into her notebook. Schedules changed; pencil marks could be checked.

On the central table lay the route map. It showed a neat grid of square spaces running from the old bridge to the canal. Small pictures marked the places along the way: a bridge, a lock, a garden, and other landmarks. Yet one square near the middle was empty. The paper beneath it was clean and uncreased, as though the missing piece had been lifted rather than torn away.

Three shallow trays stood beside the map. The first held several stiff map pieces. The second contained small landmark models made of painted wood. The third held narrow labels, each printed in clear black letters. A ruler, a sharpened pencil, and an ordinary pair of scissors rested at the edge of the table.

Deniz did not pick up the empty map or begin arranging the activity materials. She examined the trays where they stood. A bridge model sat beside a label reading BRIDGE. A little lock stood near LOCK, and a garden bed was paired with GARDEN. The map pieces showed the same familiar shapes, though she did not yet know whether they belonged in the empty space or somewhere else.

She moved the ruler closer and opened her notebook to a fresh page. At the top she wrote:

Map preparation, 10:15.
One square empty.
Three trays: map pieces, models, labels.
Ruler and pencil available.
Canal Walk, 4:00. Room B backup.

For a moment, the empty square seemed to invite a quick guess. Perhaps a route piece had been set aside. Perhaps someone had arranged the trays in a hurry. But guesses were poor foundations for a group activity, especially one meant to help people read a map carefully.

Deniz straightened the labels, leaving each beside its model. Then she placed the pencil across the notebook and looked again at the grid. Before setting out chairs, handouts, or the route pieces, she would test the arrangement and record what held true. Only after that would she decide what the session required.

Deniz began with the measurement, because the empty square might have been made for something quite different from a route piece. She placed the ruler along the top edge of a filled square, marked the distance with her eyes, and then laid it across the clean space. The measurements matched. She checked the height as well. The empty space was the same size as every other square on the grid.

She wrote in her notebook:

Empty square: same width and height as grid spaces.
No torn edge. No crease.

That result kept both possibilities open. A proper map piece could fit there, but a marked space could have been printed to the same dimensions. The measurement alone could not decide.

Next, Deniz followed the route from the bridge. The pictures did not form a random collection. The larger landmarks and smaller landmarks alternated as the squares continued toward the canal: bridge, lock, garden, lock, bridge, and so on. She did not assume the pattern continued perfectly around the empty place. Instead, she copied the visible order into her notebook and left a gap where the evidence stopped.

She turned to the trays. The first held map pieces in several shapes. The second held painted wooden models. The third held narrow labels. At first glance, one piece seemed suspicious. A flat shape near the back of the first tray looked unlike the square spaces on the map. Perhaps a visitor had lifted the correct piece from that tray and put the wrong one in its place. If so, the other pieces might also have been shifted.

Deniz moved slowly rather than taking the first impression as proof. She set the questionable piece beside the model tray. Its outline matched the small gate model. She found the label GATE in the third tray and placed it beside both. The three items agreed: shape, model, and word.

She tested the rest. A bridge-shaped piece matched the bridge model and the BRIDGE label. A lock-shaped piece matched the lock model and LOCK. The garden piece matched the garden bed and GARDEN. Even the pieces that did not seem to belong near one another on the map were paired correctly within the trays.

Deniz shifted each label just far enough to compare its printed word with the model beside it. Then she returned every item to its original tray and made a second list.

Bridge piece, bridge model, BRIDGE label.
Lock piece, lock model, LOCK label.
Garden piece, garden model, GARDEN label.
Gate piece, gate model, GATE label.
No mismatched set found.

The misplaced-piece explanation had been reasonable, but the evidence did not support it. Nothing in the trays was carelessly mixed. Each shape had its matching object and printed name. The physical arrangement was consistent, even though the empty square still interrupted the map’s repeating sequence.

Deniz drew a small line beneath her notes. The ruler had confirmed that the space belonged to the grid. The tray comparisons had confirmed that the materials had not been scrambled. Neither observation told her what the empty square meant.

She placed the map pieces in a neat row, kept the outdoor and indoor materials within reach, and looked once more at the schedule by the entrance. The Canal Walk was still listed for four o’clock, with Room B available as backup. Before she chose either arrangement, she needed to understand the pattern itself—not merely fit an object into the space.

Deniz left the pieces in their neat row and walked to the noticeboard near the entrance. The schedule was posted there beneath a sheet of clear plastic. She had already read the important line: Canal Walk, four o’clock, with Room B listed as a possible backup.

Now she looked at the small marks beside the activities. They were not pictures of bridges, locks, or gardens. Each was a plain square, printed about the same size as a map space. The Canal Walk entry had one square beside it. Room B had a different mark, a simple rectangle. The marks were part of the printed schedule, not pencil notes added by someone later.

Deniz copied the schedule marks into her notebook. Then she compared the square beside Canal Walk with the empty space on the route map. The shapes were alike, but that was not enough. A square could be used for many purposes, and the empty space still fitted perfectly where a route piece might have gone. She measured the marks with her eyes, then checked the map grid again before making any conclusion.

She took the posted room map from its frame only far enough to read it more closely. The learning space was drawn as a small plan: entrance, central table, noticeboard, Room B, and a corridor leading toward the outdoor route. Along the corridor, little symbols marked places where a person might need to pause and check something before continuing. Most were paired with familiar landmarks. One was a square. The corridor line and the route line were not identical drawings, but the marked positions could be compared in order.

Deniz returned to the route map and followed its line with her pencil, careful not to press hard enough to mark the paper. Bridge, lock, garden, lock, bridge. The landmarks alternated in a regular order. At the empty space, the sequence stopped. The square there did not behave like the pictures around it. It interrupted the pattern without damaging it.

She compared the position of that space with the square on the room map. They appeared in corresponding places along the route: not at the beginning, and not after the final landmark, but at a point where a traveller would need information before going farther. Deniz checked the order once more from the first landmark to the last visible one. The position matched, but position alone could not tell her what information waited there.

“That is a possibility,” Deniz said quietly. “Not an answer.”

At seventy, she had learned not to let a pleasing resemblance do the work of proof. The matching shape suggested that the square might mark a condition to check rather than another landmark to visit. The schedule strengthened that idea, since the Canal Walk entry carried the same kind of mark. But she still did not know what condition was involved, and she would not fill the gap with an assumption simply because the pieces looked orderly.

She looked again at the two preparations waiting on the table. The outdoor materials were arranged beside the Canal Walk map. The indoor materials for Room B remained stacked in their tray, ready if needed. Moving either set would be premature. If the square proved to be part of the route, the outdoor arrangement would remain useful. If it signaled a condition that affected the route, the indoor plan might be needed instead.

Deniz wrote three lines beneath her earlier notes:

Square on route map matches square on schedule.
Square appears at a checking point on room map.
Meaning still requires verification.

She added a short reminder not to treat the matching shapes as proof. Then she replaced the posted room map in its frame and stood for a moment beneath the noticeboard. The pattern had become more useful, but it had not yet supplied a decision. Before choosing the Canal Walk or Room B, she needed to inspect the route information connected with that marked place.

Deniz returned to the preparation table and opened her notebook to the page with the three cautious lines. The route map lay beside it, its empty square waiting in the middle of the alternating sequence. She did not touch the pieces yet. First, she needed to follow the marked position through the information available in the room.

She carried the posted room map from its frame to the table again and placed it beside the route map. With her pencil, she traced the corridor from the central table toward the outdoor path. The line passed the noticeboard, continued beyond the last indoor doorway, and ended at the route entrance shown on the plan. Near that entrance, a square marked the same checking point she had noticed before.

Deniz looked toward the noticeboard. Beneath the schedule, several public notices had been arranged in clear plastic sleeves. One was headed ROUTE INFORMATION FOR TODAY. She read it slowly, then read it a second time to make sure she had not skipped a line.

NORTH GATE CLOSED TODAY.

The words were printed in large, plain letters. Below them, a small diagram showed the gate between two short sections of path. There was no second route condition listed, and no suggestion that the notice was temporary in some uncertain way. The date at the top matched the date on the schedule.

Deniz copied the wording into her notebook. She underlined today, then set the pencil down.

The notice supplied a condition, but she still needed to see whether it belonged to the puzzle she was solving. A convenient fact was not automatically the relevant fact. She returned to the trays and lifted the printed labels one at a time. Bridge. Lock. Garden. Gate. Each label still rested beside its matching model, just as it had after her earlier comparison.

The gate model was a small wooden shape with two upright posts and a crossbar. On the route map, the empty square occupied the position where the gate appeared in the model sequence. The square beside Canal Walk on the schedule matched the empty space in shape. The marked position on the room map led toward the same part of the outdoor route. These were separate observations, Deniz reminded herself, not one observation repeated three times.

She placed the gate model beside the empty square without pressing it into the space. It was the right width, as the ruler had already shown, but physical fit alone proved very little. A bridge piece might have fitted the same grid. The alternating landmarks also needed to be considered. Bridge, lock, garden, lock, bridge: the visible sequence was regular until the gap. The gate model belonged at the interruption by position, yet its meaning still depended on the other evidence.

Deniz glanced at the two prepared sets. The Canal Walk materials remained on the left side of the table: route map, model landmarks, and labels. The Room B materials stayed together in their tray on the right. She had not moved either set into a new arrangement. The closure notice was now copied into her notebook, but she did not yet replace one plan with the other.

Instead, she drew four small boxes beneath her notes and filled them carefully:

empty square—same grid size;
schedule—square beside Canal Walk;
room map—square at route checking point;
route notice—north gate closed today.

Then she added a fifth line: gate model at corresponding route position.

Deniz read the list from top to bottom. The pieces, labels, schedule, room map, and public notice were beginning to form a single pattern. Beginning was not the same as finished. She wanted to test the relationships together before making a practical choice for the learners who would arrive at four.

She returned the room map to its frame, kept the notice visible beneath it, and left both activity setups ready on the table. The evidence was now gathered. The decision still belonged to the next comparison.

Deniz remained at the preparation table and read her five notes again. She did not begin with the most interesting fact, the closure notice. She began with the physical evidence.

The ruler had shown that the empty square was exactly the same width and height as every other space on the grid. It could therefore hold a route piece. But that possibility did not tell her whether a route piece belonged there. A space could be the right size for an object and still have another purpose.

She followed the landmarks from the beginning of the visible route. Bridge, lock, garden, lock, bridge. The arrangement repeated its relationships rather than simply repeating one picture. At the gap, the sequence was interrupted. If the missing item were another ordinary landmark, the pattern would need to continue in a way supported by the neighboring pieces. No loose bridge, lock, or garden piece explained the interruption.

Deniz opened the first tray. The bridge map piece, bridge model, and bridge label belonged together. The second tray held the lock set, and the third held the garden set. The gate model and its label were in the remaining section, aligned with the route materials. She had already tested these pairings. Each shape matched its label and model; none had been put in the wrong tray. The false explanation—that a visitor had misplaced one piece—had not survived comparison.

She turned to the schedule. Beside the words “Canal Walk, 4:00” was the same small square mark that appeared at the checking point on the posted room map. The mark did not mean “add any object that fits.” It meant that something at that position required attention before the route could be used.

Now Deniz placed the separate observations in order. The empty square occupied the gate’s position in the landmark arrangement. The gate model matched that position and had a matching printed label. The schedule marked the Canal Walk with a square, and the room map placed a square at the route’s checking point. Finally, the public notice stated plainly that the north gate was closed today. The date on the notice matched the date of the session.

She wrote the conclusion beneath the list:

EMPTY SQUARE = CLOSED-GATE MARKER.

Deniz paused with the pencil above the page. The conclusion was not based on the square’s size alone, or on the gate model alone. The grid fit made the marker possible. The landmark sequence placed it at the interruption. The trays confirmed the gate’s name and model. The schedule and room map showed that a square signaled a condition to check. The notice supplied that condition.

Together, the clues left no sensible reason to treat the empty space as an ordinary route segment. The north gate was closed, so the planned outdoor route was not usable for the four o’clock session.

She did not remove the Canal Walk materials in a hurry. First she made the explanation easy for someone else to follow. On a clean card, she printed:

CLOSED GATE — DO NOT USE OUTDOOR ROUTE TODAY.

She placed the card beside the empty square, then added a smaller label beneath the gate model: GATE MARKS A CONDITION, NOT A WALKING SECTION. The wording was precise enough to prevent the same guess she had first considered. She kept the bridge, lock, and garden labels beside their matching models and left the repeating sequence visible around the gap.

Next, Deniz moved the route map and outdoor pieces together into the Canal Walk tray. She did not discard them or mix them with unrelated materials. The map now carried its explanation, and the notice remained copied in her notebook.

From the other side of the table, she brought out the Room B materials. Room B was the scheduled backup, not a sudden alternative invented to solve the puzzle. She arranged the indoor map, the landmark models, the labels, and the ruler in a neat working order. At the top of the tray she placed a card reading:

4:00 SESSION — ROOM B INDOOR MAP ACTIVITY.

Then she checked the arrangement once more. The outdoor plan was clearly marked unusable. The indoor plan was clearly marked ready. The corrected map and the labels told the same story as the schedule, the models, and the public notice.

Deniz closed her notebook, leaving the five observations and the conclusion visible on the open page. She had not guessed which room to prepare. She had tested the pattern until the practical choice followed from it. Room B now held the materials for the learners, while the labeled route map preserved why the Canal Walk had been set aside.

At four o’clock, the first learners arrived in Room B carrying notebooks and pencils. Deniz welcomed them at the door and directed their attention to the table, where the indoor map lay beside the bridge, lock, and garden models. The labels stood upright, each beside its matching object. At the far end of the table, the gate model rested next to the card marked GATE MARKS A CONDITION, NOT A WALKING SECTION.

“Today’s Canal Walk has moved indoors,” Deniz said. “The route map remains useful, but the north gate is closed. We will use the map to examine the pattern rather than follow the outdoor route.”

She did not begin by giving them the conclusion. Instead, she invited the learners to look along the map. One person traced the visible sequence with a finger. “Bridge, lock, garden, lock, bridge,” he said. “Then the empty square.”

“What do you notice about the space?” Deniz asked.

“It is the same size as the others,” another learner replied.

“That tells us what could fit there,” Deniz said. “Does it tell us what belongs there?”

The learner considered the question and shook her head.

Deniz placed the gate model beside the empty square without covering it. She pointed to the matching label, then to the square mark copied from the schedule and the room map. “These observations agree,” she said. “The square is at the gate’s position in the pattern. The schedule uses the same mark where a condition must be checked. The public notice tells us the condition today: the north gate is closed.”

A learner compared the card with the notice written in Deniz’s notebook. “So the empty space is not waiting for another landmark?”

“Not for this activity,” Deniz replied. “It marks the closed gate. Treating it as an ordinary route section would send us toward a route that cannot be used.”

Together, they tested the explanation against the materials. The bridge model matched its label and map piece. The lock and garden did the same. The gate had its own model and label, but its place in the sequence was different: it identified a condition rather than a walking section. No one needed to move the pieces around at random. The labels and repeated relationships supplied a method for checking each claim.

Deniz gave the learners the ruler. They measured two ordinary map spaces and then the empty one. The measurements matched. This time, the result had a clear purpose. The square could belong to the grid while still carrying a warning. One learner wrote that distinction in his notebook.

The group then used the indoor map to trace the route as far as the marked gate. When they reached the closed-gate card, they paused, read the notice, and returned along the line to the Room B activity map. Deniz asked them to place the models in order and explain each choice. Their explanations were brief but complete: a landmark showed part of the pattern; a label confirmed its name; a square required a condition to be checked.

Before the learners left, Deniz gathered the materials into two clear groups. The Room B tray held the indoor map, models, labels, ruler, and the card reading 4:00 SESSION — ROOM B INDOOR MAP ACTIVITY. The Canal Walk tray held the outdoor map and pieces, with CLOSED GATE — DO NOT USE OUTDOOR ROUTE TODAY placed on top.

She added the date and the words NORTH GATE CLOSED beside the route in her notebook, then copied that note onto the route card. The corrected materials remained together so that the next person using the table would see the tested explanation, not merely an empty space.

As the room quieted, Deniz looked once more at the two trays. The indoor session had worked because the learners could inspect the pattern, ask about the mark, and follow the evidence to the correct choice. She had prepared it independently, but the arrangement now made the reasoning available for others to use.
