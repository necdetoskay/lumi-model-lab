The Island That Wasn't on Any Map

The storm left the way it came—slowly, dragging its tail of grey like something reluctant to go. Arda stood at the wooden rail of Stormbreak Watch, watching the clouds peel back from a stretch of sky that every chart in the guild library called empty.

It wasn't empty.

A forested island hung in the washed blue, green and whole and impossibly there.

"I saw it first," Nisa said, stepping up beside him.

"You did not."

"I absolutely did."

"You were inside getting tea."

"I was inside getting tea," she agreed, "while watching the sky. Which is how I saw it first."

Arda didn't answer, because the truth was he'd been staring at that patch of sky for ten minutes and she'd beaten him to saying it. He hated that she was always half a step ahead when it came to the things that mattered and never when it came to the things that didn't.

Below the island, a corridor of clear air threaded through the post-storm wreckage—a narrow lane of flyable sky between belts of tumbling cloud. It was open now. It would not stay open. The island itself was moving, sliding with terrible patience toward a wall of dense white cloud on the far side of the corridor, a wall that looked as solid as a cliff.

"We should report it," Nisa said. "Chart it from here. Let the guild send a proper—"

"We'd be charting a place that won't exist by the time anyone launches." Arda was already checking his glider's lines. "That corridor's shrinking. If we go now, we land. If we wait, we write a story about an island we almost saw."

Nisa looked at the island. Then at the corridor. Then at him, and her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was about to agree to something she wished she didn't want to do.

"One condition," she said. "We set a turnaround signal. When the corridor light dims—when it actually starts closing—we go home. No arguments, no 'five more minutes.' We go."

Arda remembered another day, another season, another cloud break he'd chased too long. The memory sat in his chest like a stone.

"Agreed," he said.

They launched together, and the sky took them.

The corridor was ragged. Wind came off the broken clouds in sideways gusts that shoved the gliders like hands on their shoulders. Arda rode the updrafts where he could find them, banking hard around a spinning slab of storm debris, and ahead of him Nisa chose a path that made his stomach drop, a narrow skim between two curling cloud walls, faster and uglier than anything he'd have chosen.

She was gambling. She always gambled.

Then a crosswind slammed into him, hard enough to tear one of his signal streamers clean off its ring, and the glider skidded sideways toward the corridor's edge.

"Gust from your left—three seconds—dip and hold!" Nisa's voice cracked across the air, and Arda dipped, held, and felt the gust roar past just over his wing.

He came out of it shaking and furious and alive. She came out of it grinning at him from a few wingspans away, and the grin said everything: *I beat you here, and I also just saved you, and both things are true.*

They reached the island together, with less room for error than either had expected.

The shore was not a beach. It was a tangled slope—a hillside of old roots spilled over the island's edge like the fingers of something buried. Wind had bent every tree inland until they all leaned the same way, as if the whole forest had spent centuries flinching from one direction. Below the roots, far below, the cloud sea turned in slow silver circles.

Arda brought the glider in at an angle, half-flying and half-falling, and a root caught the skid and tilted it sideways. For one bad second the whole machine leaned toward the drop.

"Line—now!" Nisa shouted, and Arda had the light expedition line off his harness and lashed to a stout trunk before he'd finished thinking about it, steadying the glider. The line went taut. The glider held. They stood there breathing, both of them with their hands pressed flat against its wing, like they were calming a horse.

Then Arda saw it.

A length of bleached rope ran around the trunk, half-sunk into bark that had grown over it, tied in a hitch he almost recognized, a guild-style anchor, but not one currently taught. The knot matched nothing he had ever seen. It was old. The bark had swallowed half of it.

"Nisa."

She came over, touched the fiber, and went very quiet.

"Someone was here," she said. "A long time ago."

Nisa knotted both gliders' landing tethers to separate living roots and tugged each one hard. Only when they held did Arda free his expedition line, coil it, and sling it over his shoulder. The ancient hitch had survived storms. He meant to bring his own line home.

The old knots continued inland.

They made a trail—spaced along trunk after trunk, a rope-route stitched through the forest by someone who had walked this exact path before them. Arda led, because he had found the knots and Nisa let him, which was its own kind of concession.

The forest got stranger the deeper they went. Bridges made of living root spanned gaps that dropped away to the cloud sea, grown in arcs no carpenter could copy. Pale seed-lanterns drifted up through the canopy like slow fireflies, each one a puff of glow on a wing of fluff, lighting the green from below. Somewhere behind them, back past the shore, the corridor they'd flown through was still open, though its light had not yet begun to dim. Somewhere beyond the corridor the cloud wall was, certainly, still closing in.

At a fork, Nisa stopped. "I'm taking the ridge. I want to see the corridor."

"Nisa—"

"I'll check the sky; if the light starts to dim, that's our signal to turn back. Someone should look at the sky while you chase knots." She was already climbing, and he knew she'd come back to him once she'd seen it. "We promised each other we'd be careful, Arda. Go."

He went.

The root bridge sagged under him in a way that made his palms sweat, and as he crossed, the seed-lanterns rose around him in a slow spiral, and through a gap in the canopy he saw it—the cloud wall, closer now, rolling toward the island's far edge like a tide made of white stone.

Past the bridge, he reached a cliff edge. The old knots continued as a trail marked with small carved symbols—wind-vanes, each one turned the same way. Every marker pointed in one direction. Deeper. Inland. Toward something.

He was still staring when Nisa's shout reached him.

"Arda!" She dropped down the ridge path at a run. "The corridor light's dimming. It's dimming *fast*—faster than it should. We need to go back *now*."

"The knots go somewhere. They all go the same way. Someone built a trail to a destination—"

"And we're not going to reach it before the sky closes!" Her voice cracked with real anger. "You promised. You *promised* the signal, and the signal is now."

"We came all this way to turn around at the first interesting thing?"

"We came all this way to *come back*!" She was in his face now, breathless. "Is this about the island or about you being the one who doesn't quit? Because those aren't the same thing, Arda, and only one of them keeps us alive!"

The words landed harder than the crosswind had. He turned away first. It was the only concession he could afford.

They turned toward the shore. An old camp sat beside the return trail in a hollow between two leaning trees. Something brass flashed beneath its rotted canvas, and Arda stopped before he could help himself.

A collapsed tent. Rust-eaten gear. Nisa caught his sleeve. "Our signal," she said. "We have to go." He nodded—and leaned in to grab the brass glint before following her.

Then the wind came.

A gust tore through the hollow and ripped the last canvas free, and a storm-chest burst open; the old signal kite inside lifted, caught a thread of updraft, and climbed into the branches above them, snapping and wheeling like a live thing. The sound it made was almost like laughter.

Under the torn canvas, Arda's hand closed on something hard.

A brass-cased expedition journal, swollen with damp but whole. And beside it, a scout's glider-tag—stamped with a name neither of them knew.

Arda flicked quickly through knot sketches, his fingers trembling. He stopped at the final page: *We depart by the same corridor we arrived through. If the charts still show nothing, we'll—*

The entry stopped. Mid-sentence. Mid-word.

"They were leaving," Arda said. His voice came out strange. "They planned to come back. Same route. They were—"

He looked at the unfinished line for a heartbeat. It was not a story of a failed trip. It was a story of an expedition that expected to go home.

Whether it did—he looked at the tag in his other hand—was a question with no answer in this hollow.

Then he saw the trail marker.

At the camp's far edge, half-hidden by ferns, a carved wind-vane pointed deeper inland—and beyond it, visible through a break in the trees, a shape on the high ground. Pale, vertical, deliberate. A stone gate, or the start of one, exactly where every knot and every symbol had been leading.

The corridor light, somewhere behind them, was dying.

Arda was caught between the two of them—the gate and the unfinished sentence—and felt his whole body leaning toward the gate, the way the flattened trees leaned toward the wind.

"Arda." Nisa's voice was quiet now, which was worse than shouting. "The signal."

He remembered the last time. The cloud break he'd chased. The way home he'd almost lost. The stone in his chest.

He turned his back on the gate.

"We take the journal and the tag," he said, "and we go home."

Nisa let out a breath. Then, to his genuine surprise, she said, "I wanted to go on too. I want you to know that. I wanted to follow the trail so badly I couldn't see straight. Going back isn't the same as not wanting to stay."

Arda looked at her. Something in the quarrel unlatched.

"Hurry, the light's dimming and the wind is picking up," he said.

They ran to the root bridge through mist. Its near end had torn free; its far end still hung from the shoreward ledge. Loose roots swung close to their bank above the clouds.

"We can't walk that," Nisa said.

Arda looked up. A living tree leaned over the gap, its thick bough stretching past the middle. "One swing. Then I'll follow."

"You're keeping the rope?"

"Only until we're both across."

He tied one end of their route line around the trunk and passed the middle over the bough. He clipped the other end to Nisa's harness, leaving enough length to reach the lower far ledge. She ran three steps and jumped. The line went taut. She swung under the bough and hit the far ledge feet-first, catching a root before she slid.

"Made it!" She unclipped, wrapped the free end around a stout root, and tied it off. The line now linked both banks.

Arda clipped his harness to the line as a safety rail. The bridge's loose roots swung within reach; he jumped onto them and scrambled upward toward Nisa. The last bridge anchor groaned.

"Arda, now!"

The bridge shuddered. A splintered root hooked the taut rope just ahead of his safety clip. As the bridge sagged into the clouds, the snag dragged the line downward, and the clip yanked him away from Nisa's reaching hand.

He grabbed the clip. Its gate jammed tight against his harness. The bridge pulled harder.

"Cut it!" Nisa shouted.

The rope had marked every turn back to the shore. Without it, they'd never find the same way again.

Arda drew his knife and sliced the loaded stretch between his clip and the falling root. The wrenching pull vanished. His clip went slack; he opened it and pushed off the last surviving root toward Nisa.

She dropped flat, seized his wrist, and held on. Below him the bridge tore away. His boots scraped empty air.

"Don't let go," he gasped.

"Then give me your other hand!"

He did. She dug her boots into the root and held him at the rim. Across the gap, the cut halves of their line dangled out of reach.

"Still with me?" Nisa gasped.

Arda tightened his grip. "Always."

He pulled himself up onto solid root, breathing like a bellows, and looked back at the gap where the bridge had been.

"My line," he said, touching his empty harness.

"Your line," Nisa agreed quietly, "and you cut yourself free in time." Arda managed a crooked smile. "Still worth it," he said, though it stung.

The shore, when they reached it, felt smaller than it had. Their gliders still lay where they had left them, waiting. The corridor beyond the gliders was a thread now, narrowing with every second, the cloud wall's edge close enough to see the churn inside it.

They launched together off the sloping roots, catching the last updraft with the island sliding away under them, and flew through the narrow corridor.

It was the hardest flying Arda had ever done. The corridor closed behind them the way a door closes—steadily, without malice—and they came through the last open stretch with the cloud wall breathing on their wings and the known sky opening ahead of them.

Behind them, the island went into the wall.

Arda turned in his harness and watched it happen. The green faded. The trees blurred. The whole impossible shape of it softened, then dissolved, and the cloud wall was just a cloud wall, and there was no island anywhere in the sky at all.

He couldn't say where it had gone. He didn't even know where it had come from. He knew only that it had been real, and that the journal and the tag were safely tucked away.

They landed at Stormbreak Watch as the sun sank low, shaking, mud-caked, out of rope, and entirely, completely victorious.

Nisa sat down on the wooden rail and laughed until she ran out of breath.

Arda stood a moment longer, looking back at the empty patch of sky where the charts said nothing could be.

Then he took out the glider-tag and held it in his open palm, turning it so the unknown name caught the sunset, and wondered who had tied those knots, and whether they had ever made it home.
